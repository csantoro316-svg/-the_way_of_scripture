## Genesis 12:1–9

Context follows call, obstacles, widening blessing, Babel contrast, journey, promise, tents/altars. Common section presents the shared faith/call/blessing reading without denominational scaffolding or adopting a disputed future-land scheme.

Plan section: included, because (a). Galatians 3:8–14 explicitly connects the blessing of the nations with the gospel, Christ, and the Spirit received through faith; Hebrews 11:8–10 explicitly explains Abraham's departure and life in tents as faith looking toward God's city.

Position source: none.

FLAGGED FOR REVIEW: The PDF says curse always means irreversible eternal damnation; that rule is not established by the passage or its varied biblical uses and is not used. A seven-appearance scheme, a numerical seventy-first-nation rule, and a new-name/reward system are not needed to explain the text and were not independently established, so omitted. Moreh as a specific pagan training center is not established by the place-name gloss alone; no independent archaeological/historical evidence obtained, so omitted. Genesis does not itself say Abram forfeited a specific estate, previously lived a purely urban life, or deliberately camped to confront a pagan teaching institution. No invented reconstruction of his former standard of living or Sarai's thoughts. Taking Lot is not declared sinful: the text records his presence but does not directly render that verdict. Chronological options for Terah's age were recorded in the chapter-11 evidence rather than resolved by assumption here. The public plan does not impose a dispensational timetable or a theory that eternal salvation and rewards map individually to each promise.

## Genesis 12:10–20

Context centers the actual sequence and Sarai's danger, not material gain as approval. It states no unreported palace events or specific disease. Position uses the existing study only for the developed conclusion about fearful deception and God's intervention.

Plan section: included, because (b)/(c). Supported narrative parallel: Egypt, judgment on Pharaoh's household, departure and possessions, compared with Exodus 12:29–36. Explicitly labeled parallel, not a declaration that every episode or actor has a one-to-one counterpart. Preservation of the promised family is connected to the larger blessing in Galatians 3:8–14.

Position source: Prayer and Faith, section 7. Existing-study relationship preserved.

FLAGGED FOR REVIEW: Nuzi tablets allegedly proving a widespread Egyptian wife-abduction practice were not independently corroborated; Genesis reports Abram's fear, not that cultural generalization, omitted. Sarai's motive for cooperating is not given; no claim she obeyed because she trusted more, and no rule requiring submission to deception taught. No age/beauty physiology reconstructed. Hagar is Egyptian later, but Genesis 12 does not identify her as one of these servants; exact acquisition story omitted. The plagues are not medically identified, and no assertion made that Pharaoh could not have sexual relations or that Sarai was immune. The source's always-worldly Egypt symbol is not a universal rule: Scripture also records divinely directed refuge there. The famine's precise divine purpose and its status as a designed first faith test are not stated. The site study criticizes Abram's departure; this page's sourced position is deliberately confined to its clearly developed criticism of deception, without turning silence about prayer into proof of every unrecorded action or claiming all prudent migration is sinful. Existing study quotations were not copied into the page; top-level BSB remains independently checked.

## Genesis 13:1–9

Context covers return to worship, abundance creating limited room, quarrel, and Abram's offered choice. The peace theme is developed as a concrete lesson without claiming the New Testament explicitly quotes this event.

Plan section: included, because (c). Peacemaking in Matthew 5:9 and Romans 12:17–21 adds the wider purpose of pursuing peace rather than insisting on advantage. Subsequent renewal in Genesis 13:14–18 shows generosity did not remove the promise, without promising prosperity for every peaceful act.

Position source: none.

FLAGGED FOR REVIEW: The continuing famine and God's alleged intent to force Lot away are not stated in chapter 13. Read chapters 12–13 and Wesley; these do not independently establish that explanation, omitted. Genesis says the renewed promise followed separation, not that Lot blocked all blessing until Abram achieved complete obedience. No claim that Lot's presence was itself disobedience, or that wealth proves approval. A cultural rule that Abram had an enforceable first-choice right was not independently researched; generosity is described from the offer itself, not an invented custom. No speech of repentance or particular inner calculation assigned to Abram; return and worship are stated actions.

## Genesis 13:10–13

Context preserves the visible appeal/moral warning contrast and impending Sodom story. Lot's inward status is not inferred from direction of travel or landscape alone.

Plan section: included, because (a). 2 Peter 2:6–9 explicitly interprets Sodom's judgment and Lot's rescue and calls him righteous. The page relates his troubling choice to that later description without equating an unwise choice with proof he was never God's.

Position source: none.

FLAGGED FOR REVIEW: The source's east=evil rule and Egypt=unbelief rule do not establish Lot's inward motives. Genesis emphasizes well-watered land, not a stated desire to join Sodom's immorality. Read Genesis and 2 Peter together; do not call Lot an unbeliever, say he knowingly chose every later consequence, or treat his later rescue as endorsement of the choice. An Eve/Lot sight resemblance was considered but adds less than the clear contrast within Genesis 13 and the explicit NT account; not promoted into a prediction or a hidden correspondence.

## Genesis 13:14–18

Context follows the renewed promise, the contrasting invitations to look, uncountable offspring, walk, and altar. BDB checks the actual word and cites the passage, without deriving a duration from a doctrinal system. Common reading fairly compares future-Israel restoration and the heavenly-inheritance emphasis using their own sources. No owner position supplied on the land's future fulfillment.

Plan section: included, because (a). Hebrews 11:13–16 explicitly teaches the patriarchs' patient hope for a heavenly country; Romans 4:13–16 and Galatians 3:26–29 develop inheritance through faith and belonging to Christ. This adds the practical shape of waiting and the heirs beyond visible physical descent, rather than choosing an ethnic-land timetable.

Position source: none.

FLAGGED FOR REVIEW: The PDF says `ad olam` literally specifies one age and identifies that age as the millennial one thousand years. Actual BDB entry 5769 lists Genesis 13:15 among divine promises and does not give that numerical definition; the fixed-duration lexical claim is excluded. A future millennium can be represented as a documented interpretation, not as the dictionary meaning. No assertion that Abram knew every later resurrection/timetable detail at this point. The immediate text states the promise was after Lot's separation; it does not state every earlier blessing was withheld until this moment. Catholic and other confessions were not made to speak about a land timetable they do not specify in the checked documents.
