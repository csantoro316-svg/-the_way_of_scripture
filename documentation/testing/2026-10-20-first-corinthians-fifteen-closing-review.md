## 1 Corinthians 15:29–34

Plan section: included, because (a)/(c): Romans 6:3–11 explicitly joins baptism with Christ’s death/resurrection and present newness of life, illuminating hope’s effect on conduct without deciding the disputed phrase.
Position source: none.
FLAGGED FOR REVIEW: Baptized for the dead is not conclusively resolved by adding the unprinted words resurrection of as if Paul wrote them. USCCB’s uncertainty and Wesley’s suggested readings remain visible. The LDS practice is described from its own document, including acceptance/rejection, rather than as automatic forced membership or guaranteed salvation by another person’s choice. The baseline’s insults, secret-ritual caricature and broad historical origin story are not adopted. No new ruling on postmortem conversion or every baptismal mode is originated here. Wild beasts may be figurative; an exact arena event, date or total identification with Acts 19 is not independently established. Eat/drink/tomorrow comes directly from Isaiah 22:13, not a verified statement by Solomon. The good-character saying is not attributed with certainty to Euripides as in the baseline; BSB’s note says probably Menander’s Thais, and that tentative author lead was not independently checked against the play. The quote is used as Scripture without a new historical attribution. Better resurrection in Hebrews 11 is not reduced to a fixed hierarchy of rewards without further evidence. No claim every mistaken association inevitably produces identical corruption or that only a teacher’s interpretation is obedience to God is made.

Plan section: included, because (a)/(c): Paul explicitly identifies fulfillment of Isaiah’s death-swallowed-up promise; Isaiah 25:6–9 and Revelation 21:1–4 place that victory within God’s shared life with His people, tears removed and joy in salvation.

No exact number-trigger or full account of Christ’s delay is imposed on this passage. Verse 58 is included even though the baseline lesson stops its quoted exposition at 57. Labor’s value follows the promised victory; no fixed merit calculation or demand that service earn salvation is created. Paul’s use of Hosea is retained within his victory proclamation rather than making the original judgment context a universal automatic-salvation statement.
