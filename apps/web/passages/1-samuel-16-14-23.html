Passage

1 Samuel 16:14-23

How does David’s music bring him into Saul’s service?

Berean Standard Bible

14 Now the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul, and a spirit of distress from the LORD began to torment him. 15 Saul’s servants said to him, “Surely a spirit of distress from God is tormenting you. 16 Let our lord command your servants here to seek out someone who can skillfully play the harp. Whenever the spirit of distress from God is upon you, he is to play it, and you will be well.” 17 And Saul commanded his servants, “Find me someone who plays well, and bring him to me.” 18 One of the servants answered, “I have seen a son of Jesse of Bethlehem who knows how to play the harp. He is a mighty man of valor, a warrior, eloquent and handsome, and the LORD is with him.”

19 So Saul sent messengers to Jesse and said, “Send me your son David, who is with the sheep.” 20 And Jesse took a donkey loaded with bread, a skin of wine, and one young goat and sent them to Saul with his son David. 21 When David came to Saul and entered his service, Saul loved him very much, and David became his armor-bearer. 22 Then Saul sent word to Jesse, saying, “Let David remain in my service, for I am pleased with him.” 23 And whenever the spirit from God came upon Saul, David would pick up his harp and play. Then Saul would find relief and feel better, and the spirit of distress would depart from him.

What is the context?

The story turns from the Spirit coming upon David to the Spirit departing from Saul. A spirit of distress from the LORD torments Saul. The account presents his suffering within the changed relationship between God and the king, rather than treating the loss of royal favor as an empty announcement.

Saul’s servants propose finding a skilled harp player to bring him relief. One recommends Jesse’s son, praising his music, courage, speech and appearance, and saying that the LORD is with him. Saul sends for David, and Jesse sends him with gifts.

Saul welcomes David warmly. He loves him, appoints him as an armor-bearer, and asks Jesse to let him remain. When David plays, Saul finds relief and the distressing spirit departs. The relationship begins with service and affection, even though the reader already knows David has been chosen for the kingship.

The need in Saul’s household opens a place for David’s skill. A young man known for tending sheep is now learning life in the royal court. Neither he nor Jesse is described as arranging a scheme to seize the throne.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill understand the departure of the Spirit especially in terms of the gifts that equipped Saul to rule. They read the distressing spirit as acting under God’s permission in judgment. Their explanation takes the spiritual language seriously while distinguishing God’s authority from approval of evil.

Wesley, Henry and Gill also recognize the relief associated with David’s music. Wesley and Gill allow both the ordinary calming effect of music and God’s particular purpose in bringing David into favor. The chapter describes the relief but does not provide a treatment formula or identify a modern diagnosis for Saul.

Wesley sees providence preparing David for future service through this unexpected opening. Saul’s need and David’s gift bring them together. Saul’s present affection is part of the story and should be allowed its place before their later conflict develops.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God’s care can reach a troubled person through the gifts and presence of another. David’s music brings real relief even in the household of a failing king. A wider Christian expression of comfort shared through others appears in 2 Corinthians 1:3–7: God comforts His people so they can comfort others, with that comfort overflowing through Christ.

Colossians 3:12–17 brings compassion, patience and the singing of psalms and hymns into the shared life of Christ’s people. These passages do not turn David’s playing into a universal method of driving away spirits. They extend the theme of gifts used in loving service, so that what one person has received becomes help for another.
