Passage

Genesis 21:1-8

The Birth of Isaac

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now the LORD attended to Sarah as He had said, and the LORD did for Sarah what He had promised. 2 So Sarah conceived and bore a son to Abraham in his old age, at the very time God had promised.

3 And Abraham gave the name Isaac to the son Sarah bore to him. 4 When his son Isaac was eight days old, Abraham circumcised him, as God had commanded him. 5 Abraham was a hundred years old when his son Isaac was born to him.

6 Then Sarah said, “God has made me laugh, and everyone who hears of this will laugh with me.” 7 She added, “Who would have told Abraham that Sarah would nurse children? Yet I have borne him a son in his old age.”

8 So the child grew and was weaned, and Abraham held a great feast on the day Isaac was weaned.

What is the context?

The long-awaited birth arrives with repeated emphasis on God's word: He attends to Sarah as He said, does what He promised, and gives the child at the promised time. The opening directs attention to God's faithfulness before describing the family's response. Abraham is a hundred years old when Isaac is born.

Abraham gives the child the name Isaac and circumcises him on the eighth day, as God had commanded. The promise is received with concrete obedience. Sarah's laughter is now the joy of a mother inviting others to rejoice with her, recalling the earlier laughter surrounding the announcement of the birth.

The child grows and is weaned, and Abraham holds a feast. This celebration completes the first part of the account, but it also introduces the setting for the dispute over Ishmael and Isaac that follows. One fulfilled promise brings joy into a household where earlier choices still have consequences.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read Isaac's birth as the fulfillment of God's promise to Abraham and Sarah, with their joy and obedience responding to His faithfulness. The US Catholic bishops' notes on Genesis 21:1–7 likewise identify the promised birth as the central expectation of the preceding chapters now coming true.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 11:11–12 explicitly remembers Sarah's conception through faith in the One who promised. The many descendants begin with this one child, received when the family's own circumstances offered little hope. The account gives faith a definite object: the trustworthiness of what God has actually said.

Galatians 4:22–28 goes on to describe believers as children of promise like Isaac. Paul uses the birth to explain an inheritance received through God's promise rather than secured by placing oneself under the law. The joy in Abraham's tent belongs to a larger story that will reach people who become God's children through Christ.
