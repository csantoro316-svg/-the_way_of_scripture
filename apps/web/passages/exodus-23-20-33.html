Passage

Exodus 23:20-33

How would God lead Israel into the land He promised?

Berean Standard Bible

20 Behold, I am sending an angel before you to protect you along the way and to bring you to the place I have prepared. 21 Pay attention to him and listen to his voice; do not defy him, for he will not forgive rebellion, since My Name is in him. 22 But if you will listen carefully to his voice and do everything I say, I will be an enemy to your enemies and a foe to your foes. 23 For My angel will go before you and bring you into the land of the Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Canaanites, Hivites, and Jebusites, and I will annihilate them.

24 You must not bow down to their gods or serve them or follow their practices. Instead, you are to demolish them and smash their sacred stones to pieces. 25 So you shall serve the LORD your God, and He will bless your bread and your water. And I will take away sickness from among you. 26 No woman in your land will miscarry or be barren; I will fulfill the number of your days.

27 I will send My terror ahead of you and throw into confusion every nation you encounter. I will make all your enemies turn and run. 28 I will send the hornet before you to drive the Hivites and Canaanites and Hittites out of your way. 29 I will not drive them out before you in a single year; otherwise the land would become desolate and wild animals would multiply against you. 30 Little by little I will drive them out ahead of you, until you become fruitful and possess the land.

31 And I will establish your borders from the Red Sea to the Sea of the Philistines, and from the desert to the Euphrates. For I will deliver the inhabitants into your hand, and you will drive them out before you. 32 You shall make no covenant with them or with their gods. 33 They must not remain in your land, lest they cause you to sin against Me. For if you serve their gods, it will surely be a snare to you.”

What is the context?

God promises an angel to guard Israel and bring them to the place He has prepared. The people must listen and refuse rebellion because God’s name is in His messenger. Following that voice and doing what God says are presented together. The promise of guidance comes with a serious demand for obedience.

Israel must reject the gods and worship practices of the land’s peoples. Blessings on food, water, health, childbirth and length of life are promised within this covenant setting. These promises address the people’s life in the land; they should not be turned into a diagnosis of an individual believer’s sickness or infertility.

God will act ahead of Israel, but the land will not be emptied in a single year. He gives the reason: rapid abandonment would leave it desolate and allow wild animals to multiply. The gradual pace is part of the promised care. The borders reach from the Red Sea to the Sea of the Philistines and from the desert to the Euphrates. The closing warning concerns the danger of being drawn into the worship of other gods through the relationships Israel forms.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Interpreters differ over the angel’s identity. Calvin and Wesley understand Him as the Son before His human birth. They connect the divine name and authority with Paul’s warning that Israel tested Christ in the wilderness (1 Corinthians 10:9). Rashi instead describes an angelic messenger acting under God’s name and commission, without independent authority to pardon. The difference is whether the messenger is Himself divine or bears the authority of the God who sent him.

The hornet in verse 28 has also been read literally or as an image of the terror that drives the inhabitants away. Calvin and Wesley understand actual hornets; Gill considers both possibilities and favors the literal reading. In either case, the promise emphasizes God’s action ahead of the people and His deliberate pace, rather than Israel succeeding through its own strength alone.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament treats Israel’s wilderness history as a warning for people who have received great gifts from God. 1 Corinthians 10:1–13 recalls the cloud, sea, food and water, yet warns that many still fell through disobedience. Paul specifically speaks of testing Christ. That establishes a connection with Christ in the wilderness story, while the precise identity of this angel remains the interpretive question described above.

Hebrews 3:12–19 likewise calls its readers to keep trusting God and encourage one another rather than become hardened by sin. The warning and the promise belong together: the God who leads His people calls for a lasting response of faith. This is the larger biblical connection, rather than a claim that every feature of Israel’s conquest predicts a separate event in Christian life.
