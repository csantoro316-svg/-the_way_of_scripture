Passage

Matthew 27:15-26

Barabbas Is Released, and Jesus Is Condemned

Berean Standard Bible

15 Now it was the governor’s custom at the feast to release to the crowd a prisoner of their choosing. 16 At that time they were holding a notorious prisoner named Barabbas. 17 So when the crowd had assembled, Pilate asked them, “Which one do you want me to release to you: Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ?” 18 For he knew it was out of envy that they had handed Jesus over to him. 19 While Pilate was sitting on the judgment seat, his wife sent him this message: “Have nothing to do with that innocent man, for I have suffered terribly in a dream today because of Him.”

20 But the chief priests and elders persuaded the crowds to ask for Barabbas and to have Jesus put to death. 21 “Which of the two do you want me to release to you?” asked the governor. “Barabbas,” they replied. 22 “What then should I do with Jesus who is called Christ?” Pilate asked. They all answered, “Crucify Him!” 23 “Why?” asked Pilate. “What evil has He done?” But they shouted all the louder, “Crucify Him!”

24 When Pilate saw that he was accomplishing nothing, but that instead a riot was breaking out, he took water and washed his hands before the crowd. “I am innocent of this man’s blood,” he said. “You bear the responsibility.” 25 All the people answered, “His blood be on us and on our children!” 26 So Pilate released Barabbas to them. But he had Jesus flogged, and handed Him over to be crucified.

What is the context?

Pilate offers the crowd a choice between Barabbas and Jesus, following the governor's custom of releasing a prisoner at the feast. Matthew calls Barabbas notorious and says Pilate knows envy has motivated Jesus' accusers. Mark 15:6–15 and Luke 23:18–25 connect Barabbas with insurrection and murder.

While Pilate sits on the judgment seat, his wife sends a message calling Jesus innocent and warning him against involvement, after suffering because of Him in a dream. Matthew does not tell us her name, describe the dream, or say what later became of her. Her warning adds another acknowledgment of Jesus' innocence within the account.

The chief priests and elders persuade the crowds to ask for Barabbas and Jesus' death. Pilate asks what he should do with Jesus and what evil He has done. The crowd answers with louder demands for crucifixion. Their insistence does not supply evidence that the charges are true.

Seeing a riot developing, Pilate washes his hands and declares himself innocent of Jesus' blood. The people accept the blood on themselves and their children. Matthew then records what Pilate actually does: he releases Barabbas, has Jesus flogged, and hands Him over for crucifixion. His declaration and the crowd's words do not remove his part in that decision.

The scene concerns the people acting in it. Deuteronomy 24:16 states the principle that children are not to be executed for their fathers' sins. The crowd's statement must not be turned into permission to blame or harm Jewish people in other times and places.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see the release of a guilty prisoner alongside the condemnation of the innocent Jesus, and Pilate's handwashing as an unsuccessful attempt to shift responsibility. Readers have differed over how far to extend the crowd's words about their children.

Wesley on 27:25 connects those words with Jerusalem's later destruction and subsequent calamities among Jewish people. That is his historical commentary, rather than a statement of what every Methodist today believes.

The Catholic Church expressly rejects assigning the guilt of the Passion to all Jews then living or to Jewish people today. Nostra Aetate, 4, and the Catechism, 597–598, also reject presenting Jewish people as rejected or cursed by God on the basis of these events. The Catechism places Christ's suffering within the larger reality of human sin, including Christians' own sin.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Barabbas' release supplies a striking, supported parallel to the wider saving purpose of Jesus' death: a guilty person goes free while the innocent One is condemned. 2 Corinthians 5:14–21 explains Christ's death in relation to reconciliation and the sinless One acting on behalf of sinners. The parallel does not tell us that Barabbas himself came to faith; it helps us see the contrast between the guilt of those being rescued and the innocence of their Savior.

The New Testament also brings responsibility and mercy together. In Acts 3:13–19, Peter recalls the choice of a murderer over Jesus, declares that God raised Jesus, and calls his hearers to turn back so that their sins may be wiped away. His message confronts wrongdoing and offers forgiveness.

Romans 11:1–6, 17–32 then gives the wider setting for God's dealings with Israel and the nations. Paul denies that God has rejected His people, warns Gentile believers against arrogance, speaks of Israel's partial hardening and promised salvation, and emphasizes God's mercy and enduring call. That larger purpose gives no ground for inherited ethnic guilt or pride toward the people through whom the promises came.
