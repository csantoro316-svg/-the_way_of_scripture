Passage

Acts 17:25

Why does God need nothing from human hands?

Berean Standard Bible

25 Nor is He served by human hands, as if He needed anything, because He Himself gives everyone life and breath and everything else.

What is the context?

Paul has just described God as the Maker and Lord of the world, who does not live in temples made by human hands. He now explains why God does not need people to sustain Him: the direction of dependence runs the other way. He gives everyone life, breath and everything else.

The word served comes from θεραπεύω (therapeuō, theh-rah-PYOO-oh). Thayer’s lexicon lists serving or doing service as its meaning in Acts 17:25. Elsewhere the word often describes healing, but Paul is not saying God needs medical care. The qualifying phrase about needing anything explains the kind of mistaken dependence he is rejecting.

This does not make obedience, generosity or service to others pointless. Paul is correcting the idea that the Creator relies on His creatures to meet a lack in Himself. The following verses explain our dependence on Him and His call for us to repent.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this verse as teaching that God sustains creation without needing creation to sustain Him. Serving God is therefore not a way of supplying something missing from His life or making Him dependent on our gifts.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The giver of life to everyone is also the One who calls everyone to repent in Acts 17:30–31. Creation and judgment belong together: people remain accountable to the God on whom they depend. Acts 10:42–43 connects the risen Jesus’ role as Judge with forgiveness for those who believe in Him. God’s freedom from need does not mean indifference toward the people He made.

What is our position?

Worship as the Early Church Knew It directly uses Acts 17:25 to explain that our acts of devotion do not sustain God or add something He lacks. He gives life; we receive it. The study calls for reverence grounded in that truth, rather than treating religious activity as a way to maintain or impress a needy deity.
