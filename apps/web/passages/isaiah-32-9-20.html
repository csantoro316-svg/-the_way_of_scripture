Passage

Isaiah 32:9-20

What can turn complacency and ruined land into lasting peace?

Berean Standard Bible

9 Stand up, you complacent women; listen to me. Give ear to my word, you overconfident daughters. 10 In a little more than a year you will tremble, O secure ones. For the grape harvest will fail and the fruit harvest will not arrive. 11 Shudder, you ladies of leisure; tremble, you daughters of complacency. Strip yourselves bare and put sackcloth around your waists. 12 Beat your breasts for the pleasant fields, for the fruitful vines, 13 and for the land of my people, overgrown with thorns and briers— even for every house of merriment in this city of revelry. 14 For the palace will be forsaken, the busy city abandoned. The hill and the watchtower will become caves forever— the delight of wild donkeys and a pasture for flocks—

15 until the Spirit is poured out upon us from on high. Then the desert will be an orchard, and the orchard will seem like a forest. 16 Then justice will inhabit the wilderness, and righteousness will dwell in the fertile field. 17 The work of righteousness will be peace; the service of righteousness will be quiet confidence forever. 18 Then my people will dwell in a peaceful place, in safe and secure places of rest.

19 But hail will level the forest, and the city will sink to the depths. 20 Blessed are those who sow beside abundant waters, who let the ox and donkey range freely.

What is the context?

The prophet addresses women who are comfortable and unconcerned, calling them to listen before their security disappears. In a little more than a year the grape and fruit harvest will fail. Fields, vines, joyful houses and the busy city become reasons for mourning.

The abandoned palace and city are pictured as places where animals range. The decisive change comes when the Spirit is poured out from on high. The land becomes fruitful, and justice and righteousness take up residence where ruin had been.

Peace is described as the work of righteousness, with quiet confidence and secure places of rest following it. The closing lines hold blessing and judgment together: the forest is struck by hail and the city brought low, while those sowing beside abundant waters are called blessed.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley connect the warning with complacency that refuses to take coming judgment seriously. Both look to the work of the Spirit for the promised change; Wesley places its full realization in the days of the Messiah.

Gill argues that the outpouring in verse 15 points beyond Pentecost to a future work of the Spirit associated with the turning of Jewish people to Christ and wider fruitfulness among the nations. The difference concerns the timing and reach of the promise, rather than whether the Spirit produces the change.

Wesley emphasizes peace as the outcome of justice and God’s mercy. Gill especially connects the peace with the righteousness Christ provides for His people. Both explanations place the source of lasting confidence in what God does, rather than in the complacency challenged at the beginning.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

At Pentecost, Peter explains the outpouring of the Spirit in Acts 2:14–21, 32–39. His direct quotation is from Joel, not Isaiah 32. He then declares that the risen and exalted Jesus has poured out the promised Spirit, and calls his hearers to repent.

That makes a strong parallel with Isaiah’s movement from a warning to the Spirit bringing new life. Galatians 5:16–26 develops what that life looks like: the Spirit produces love, joy, peace and other qualities that stand against hostility, rivalry and envy.

The larger purpose is more than a return to comfortable circumstances. God gives His Spirit to form people whose life bears the fruit of His character, including the peace and righteousness Isaiah sets together.
