Passage

Genesis 41:46-52

The Seven Years of Plenty

Berean Standard Bible

46 Now Joseph was thirty years old when he entered the service of Pharaoh king of Egypt. And Joseph left Pharaoh’s presence and traveled throughout the land of Egypt.

47 During the seven years of abundance, the land brought forth bountifully. 48 During those seven years, Joseph collected all the excess food in the land of Egypt and stored it in the cities. In every city he laid up the food from the fields around it. 49 So Joseph stored up grain in such abundance, like the sand of the sea, that he stopped keeping track of it; for it was beyond measure.

50 Before the years of famine arrived, two sons were born to Joseph by Asenath daughter of Potiphera, priest of On. 51 Joseph named the firstborn Manasseh, saying, “God has made me forget all my hardship and all my father’s household.” 52 And the second son he named Ephraim, saying, “God has made me fruitful in the land of my affliction.”

What is the context?

Joseph is thirty when he enters Pharaoh's service and travels through Egypt. During the seven years of abundance, he gathers food and stores it in the cities near the fields that produce it. The amount becomes so great that the account compares it with the sand of the sea and says he stops keeping track.

Before the famine arrives, Asenath bears him two sons. Joseph names the first Manasseh, מְנַשֶּׁה, Mĕnaššeh (meh-nash-SHEH), connecting the name with God making him forget his hardship and his father's household. Brown–Driver–Briggs records the connection with forgetting in Genesis 41:51.

He names the second Ephraim, אֶפְרַיִם, ʾEprayim (ef-RAH-yim), connecting the name with God making him fruitful in the land of his affliction. Brown–Driver–Briggs likewise connects Genesis 41:52 with bearing fruit. Both names acknowledge God in the new life Joseph is building.

The names hold relief and painful history together. Egypt has brought real hardship, and Joseph still calls it the land of his affliction even as he gives thanks for fruitfulness there. Later chapters will show how deeply his family still matters to him. The birth notices prepare for the significant place these two sons will have in Israel's future.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read Joseph's work as faithful preparation and his sons' names as an acknowledgment of God's care after suffering. Wesley's note on Genesis 41:50 emphasizes both meanings while recognizing that relief from misery does not mean Joseph has ceased to care about his father's family.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The stored grain and growing household show provision taking shape before the famine. Psalm 105:16–22 later places Joseph's authority within God's advance care, and Genesis 48:5 will give Ephraim and Manasseh a place among Jacob's own sons. A family established in Egypt will become part of the people who inherit God's promise.

Joseph also gives thanks while naming the place of his earlier pain. That fits the wider theme of God's comfort within affliction in 2 Corinthians 1:3–7. Gratitude for new good does not require the story of suffering to disappear. Scripture can acknowledge both the wound and the care that sustains a life beyond it.
