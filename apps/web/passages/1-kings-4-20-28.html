Passage

1 Kings 4:20-28

What does peace under the vine and fig tree mean?

Berean Standard Bible

20 The people of Judah and Israel were as numerous as the sand on the seashore, and they were eating and drinking and rejoicing. 21 And Solomon reigned over all the kingdoms from the Euphrates to the land of the Philistines, as far as the border of Egypt. These kingdoms offered tribute and served Solomon all the days of his life. 22 Solomon’s provisions for a single day were thirty cors of fine flour, sixty cors of meal, 23 ten fat oxen, twenty range oxen, and a hundred sheep, as well as deer, gazelles, roebucks, and fattened poultry. 24 For Solomon had dominion over everything west of the Euphrates —over all the kingdoms from Tiphsah to Gaza—and he had peace on all sides. 25 Throughout the days of Solomon, Judah and Israel dwelt securely from Dan to Beersheba, each man under his own vine and his own fig tree.

26 Solomon had 4,000 stalls for his chariot horses and 12,000 horses. 27 Each month the governors in turn provided food for King Solomon and all who came to his table. They saw to it that nothing was lacking. 28 Each one also brought to the required place their quotas of barley and straw for the chariot horses and other horses.

What is the context?

Judah and Israel are described as numerous, eating, drinking and rejoicing. Solomon rules over kingdoms that bring tribute, and peace surrounds his realm.

The daily food required for his court is substantial. The governors supply it in turn and also provide feed for the horses. The account presents both the prosperity of the land and the resources needed to maintain the king’s household.

The image of each person under his own vine and fig tree gives that peace a homely setting. People can enjoy what grows around their homes without living in fear of attack.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the vine and fig tree as a picture of enjoying the fruit of one’s work in safety and comfort. Security extends beyond defended cities into the countryside.

Henry sees the prosperity and peace as part of the brightness of Solomon’s reign, and draws a further comparison with the peace of Christ’s kingdom.

The scene describes a high point in Israel’s life under Solomon. Its gifts should be read alongside the responsibilities and burdens that the rest of Kings will continue to show.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The description of people as numerous as the seashore’s sand recalls God’s promise to Abraham in Genesis 22:17–18. The nation’s growth belongs within the story of God’s faithfulness, while the promise also reaches to blessing for all nations.

The vine and fig tree picture appears again in Micah 4:1–4. There, God teaches the nations, settles disputes and brings an end to training for war. Each person sits without fear under a vine and fig tree.

Solomon’s peace offers a limited comparison with that larger hope. Micah looks beyond the success of one royal household to peace among peoples under God’s instruction. The shared picture helps readers recognize what safe and settled life is meant to be.
