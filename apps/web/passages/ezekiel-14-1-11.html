Passage

Ezekiel 14:1-11

Can people seek God’s counsel while holding on to their idols?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then some of the elders of Israel came and sat down before me. 2 And the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 3 “Son of man, these men have set up idols in their hearts and put wicked stumbling blocks before their faces. Should I consult with them in any way? 4 Therefore speak to them and tell them that this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘When any Israelite sets up idols in his heart and puts a wicked stumbling block before his face, and then comes to the prophet, I the LORD will answer him according to his great idolatry, 5 so that I may take hold of the hearts of the people of Israel. For because of their idols, they are all estranged from Me.’

6 Therefore tell the house of Israel that this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Repent and turn away from your idols; turn your faces away from all your abominations. 7 For when any Israelite or any foreigner dwelling in Israel separates himself from Me, sets up idols in his heart, and puts a wicked stumbling block before his face, and then comes to the prophet to inquire of Me, I the LORD will answer him Myself. 8 I will set My face against that man and make him a sign and a proverb; I will cut him off from among My people. Then you will know that I am the LORD.

9 But if the prophet is enticed to speak a message, then it was I the LORD who enticed him, and I will stretch out My hand against him and destroy him from among My people Israel. 10 They will bear their punishment—the punishment of the inquirer will be the same as that of the prophet— 11 in order that the house of Israel may no longer stray from Me and no longer defile themselves with all their transgressions. Then they will be My people and I will be their God, declares the Lord GOD.’”

What is the context?

Some elders come and sit before Ezekiel. Before their questions are reported, the Lord exposes what is in their hearts: they have given themselves to idols while approaching His prophet for counsel.

The answer will address that idolatry. The Lord intends to take hold of the hearts that have become estranged from Him, and His command is to repent and turn away from the abominations. The warning applies both to Israelites and to foreigners living among them.

Those who insist on separating themselves from Him will face His answer in judgment. A visit to the prophet cannot replace the change of heart and direction He requires.

The warning also reaches the prophet who is enticed into speaking. Both prophet and inquirer will bear punishment. The stated purpose is that Israel should stop straying and defiling itself, and belong to the Lord as His people.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley emphasize that an outward approach to God cannot make an idolatrous heart acceptable. Wesley understands God’s answer as exposing the inquirer’s condition and bringing just judgment.

Verse 9 raises a difficult question: how can the Lord speak of enticing a prophet and also judge him? Wesley explains it as God permitting the false prophet to err or leaving him in his chosen blindness. Calvin’s discussion of verse 9 puts stronger emphasis on God actively judging a people who prefer lies. He compares the scene with the lying spirit in Ahab’s prophets. These are different explanations of God’s part in the deception, while both retain the prophet’s responsibility.

James 1:13–18 states that God does not tempt anyone to evil and traces temptation to a person’s own desires. The difficulty in Ezekiel should be read alongside that teaching. The chapter describes judgment on people committed to idols and false messages, rather than making wrongdoing something they can blame on God.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

First Corinthians 10:1–22 uses Israel’s earlier experiences to warn Christians against idolatry and false confidence. Sharing in remarkable gifts and experiences did not make disobedience harmless.

Paul calls his readers to flee idolatry and explains their shared participation in Christ through the cup and bread. They cannot treat communion with the Lord and participation in idolatry as compatible.

Paul does not explicitly quote Ezekiel 14. He develops the same larger concern: God calls people into fellowship with Himself, and that relationship demands a response of faithfulness rather than an outward religious act alongside a heart devoted elsewhere.
