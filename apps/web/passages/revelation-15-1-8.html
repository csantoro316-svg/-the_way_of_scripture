Passage

Revelation 15:1-8

Why do the victors sing before the final plagues are poured out?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I saw another great and marvelous sign in heaven: seven angels with the seven final plagues, with which the wrath of God is completed.

2 And I saw something like a sea of glass mixed with fire, beside which stood those who had conquered the beast and its image and the number of its name. They were holding harps from God, 3 and they sang the song of God’s servant Moses and of the Lamb: “Great and wonderful are Your works, O Lord God Almighty! Just and true are Your ways, O King of the nations! 4 Who will not fear You, O Lord, and glorify Your name? For You alone are holy. All nations will come and worship before You, for Your righteous acts have been revealed.”

5 After this I looked, and the temple—the tabernacle of the Testimony—was opened in heaven. 6 And out of the temple came the seven angels with the seven plagues, dressed in clean and bright linen and girded with golden sashes around their chests. 7 Then one of the four living creatures gave the seven angels seven golden bowls full of the wrath of God, who lives forever and ever. 8 And the temple was filled with smoke from the glory of God and from His power; and no one could enter the temple until the seven plagues of the seven angels were completed.

What is the context?

John sees another great sign in heaven: seven angels with seven final plagues. Before those plagues are poured out, the vision turns to people who have conquered the beast, its image and the number of its name. They stand beside something like a sea of glass mixed with fire, holding harps from God.

Their victory is expressed in worship. They sing the song of God’s servant Moses and of the Lamb, praising God’s great works and His just and true ways. The song looks toward all nations worshiping Him because His righteous acts have been revealed. The beast’s demand for worship has not determined their allegiance.

The heavenly temple, described as the tabernacle of the Testimony, then opens. Seven angels emerge in bright linen and golden sashes, and a living creature gives them golden bowls filled with God’s wrath. The Greek word phiale means a broad, shallow bowl; see Thayer’s entry. These are vessels for the pouring out described in the next chapter.

Smoke from God’s glory and power fills the temple, and no one can enter until the seven plagues are completed. The scene presents the coming judgment in connection with God’s holiness and the praise of people who have remained faithful. Their worship comes before the account of the bowls reaching the earth.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Walvoord and the USCCB notes understand this as a heavenly scene of victory and worship before the plagues. They connect the singers with people who have resisted the beast. The encouragement is that the beast’s apparent success has not deprived God’s people of their place with Him.

The song’s relationship to Moses is explained with different emphases. The USCCB notes point especially to Exodus 15, after Israel’s escape through the sea. Wesley connects its language with both Exodus 15 and Deuteronomy 32 and understands the song as belonging to Moses and the Lamb together. Walvoord favors two songs and gives particular weight to Deuteronomy 32. The readings share the themes of God’s faithfulness, rescue and justice.

The bowl sequence is generally understood as bringing the judgments toward their climax, although teachers differ over how it fits with the seals, trumpets and later events. The word final describes these plagues in the vision; it should be read alongside the later judgment scenes that Revelation still presents.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The reference to Moses makes the connection with Israel’s earlier rescue explicit. In Exodus 15:1–18, the people sing after God defeats the pursuing army and brings them through the sea. Revelation places the victors over the beast beside a glassy sea and names the song of Moses and the Lamb. The earlier rescue helps readers understand the larger pattern: God overcomes the power that oppresses His people and receives their worship.

Deuteronomy 32:3–4 and 35–36 likewise joins God’s greatness, just ways, judgment and compassion for His servants. These scriptural parallels give substance to the song’s praise of His justice. His people’s rescue and His judgment of evil belong within the same story.

The temple filled with glory also recalls Exodus 40:34–35 and 1 Kings 8:10–11, where God’s presence prevents ordinary entry or ministry. Revelation draws on that recognizable sanctuary imagery. Hebrews 9:23–24 helps explain the wider connection between the earthly sanctuary and heaven, where Christ appears before God on behalf of His people.
