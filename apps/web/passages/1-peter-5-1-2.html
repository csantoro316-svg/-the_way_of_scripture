Passage

1 Peter 5:1-2

What work is entrusted to the elders?

Berean Standard Bible

1 As a fellow elder, a witness of Christ’s sufferings, and a partaker of the glory to be revealed, I appeal to the elders among you: 2 Be shepherds of God’s flock that is among you, watching over them not out of compulsion, but willingly, as God would have you; not out of greed, but out of eagerness;

What is the context?

After encouraging believers to entrust themselves to God while doing good, Peter addresses their elders. He speaks as a fellow elder, sharing both the reality of Christ’s sufferings and the hope of His revealed glory.

The word for fellow elder identifies a shared responsibility. The task is to shepherd God’s flock among them, watching over people willingly and eagerly. Compulsion and greed are set against the motives that fit this care.

The following verses clarify how the work is carried out: by example, under Christ the Chief Shepherd. The people remain God’s flock rather than a leader’s possession.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin and Wesley understand the charge as active care through faithful teaching and conduct, offered willingly rather than for gain.

The Reformed Baptist 1689 Confession, 26.8–9, treats bishop and elder as names for the same office. The Catholic Catechism, 1554–1558, distinguishes bishops, priests and deacons within ordained ministry. The shared call to shepherd is therefore read within different accounts of church leadership.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John 21:15–19 shows the risen Jesus entrusting care of His sheep to Peter. Here that responsibility is shared with the elders as they care for people who belong to God.

Acts 20:17, 28–35 gives the same joined description of elders, oversight and shepherding, with protection and generosity directed toward the people Christ has gathered.

What is our position?

Our study Who Shepherds the Church? understands elder, overseer and shepherd here as descriptions of the same work: mature, qualified believers caring for the people among whom they serve. It reads this alongside Acts 20 and Titus 1 as a pattern of plural leadership, rather than separate ranks of spiritual standing.
