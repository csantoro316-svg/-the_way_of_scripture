Passage

Isaiah 15:1-9

Why does the prophet grieve over Moab’s ruin?

Berean Standard Bible

1 This is the burden against Moab: Ar in Moab is ruined, destroyed in a night! Kir in Moab is devastated, destroyed in a night! 2 Dibon goes up to its temple to weep at its high places. Moab wails over Nebo, as well as over Medeba. Every head is shaved, every beard is cut off. 3 In its streets they wear sackcloth; on the rooftops and in the public squares they all wail, falling down weeping. 4 Heshbon and Elealeh cry out; their voices are heard as far as Jahaz. Therefore the soldiers of Moab cry out; their souls tremble within.

5 My heart cries out over Moab; her fugitives flee as far as Zoar, as far as Eglath-shelishiyah. With weeping they ascend the slope of Luhith; they lament their destruction on the road to Horonaim. 6 The waters of Nimrim are dried up, and the grass is withered; the vegetation is gone, and the greenery is no more. 7 So they carry their wealth and belongings over the Brook of the Willows. 8 For their outcry echoes to the border of Moab. Their wailing reaches Eglaim; it is heard in Beer-elim. 9 The waters of Dimon are full of blood, but I will bring more upon Dimon— a lion upon the fugitives of Moab and upon the remnant of the land.

What is the context?

This message concerns Moab, and it continues into chapter 16. The opening announces the sudden ruin of Ar and Kir. The sound that follows is mourning: people weep at their places of worship, wear sackcloth in the streets and cry out from rooftops and public squares.

The shaved heads and cut beards belong to the scene of grief, as the NABRE notes explain. Even the soldiers tremble. The repeated place names let the reader hear distress spreading across the country.

The speaker’s own heart cries out over Moab while fugitives escape with their belongings. Water dries up, vegetation disappears, and wealth must be carried away. The disaster affects the homes, food and security on which ordinary life depends.

The closing warning announces still more danger for those who escape. Yet the passage does not celebrate their suffering: the voice that announces the judgment also laments what is happening to them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry understands the grief as compassion for people who, though enemies of Israel, remain fellow human beings. Gill likewise reads verse 5 as the prophet pitying Moab, while noting that other interpreters understand it as the Moabites voicing their own sorrow.

The final lion is read in more than one way. Wesley understands actual lions finding those who escaped the invading army. Gill includes that reading but also reports the interpretation of a further conquering king. The shared warning is that fleeing the first disaster will not necessarily bring safety.
