Passage

Matthew 24:9-14

Faithful Witness Through Persecution

Berean Standard Bible

9 Then they will deliver you over to be persecuted and killed, and you will be hated by all nations because of My name. 10 At that time many will fall away and will betray and hate one another, 11 and many false prophets will arise and deceive many. 12 Because of the multiplication of wickedness, the love of most will grow cold.

13 But the one who perseveres to the end will be saved. 14 And this gospel of the kingdom will be preached in all the world as a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come.

What is the context?

After speaking about deception, war, famine, and earthquakes, Jesus turns to what His followers will face because they bear His name. They will be handed over, persecuted, and killed. The danger also reaches inside their relationships: many will fall away, betray one another, and hate one another.

Fall away is σκανδαλίζω, skandalizō (skahn-dah-LEE-zoh). Thayer's entry explains that the word can describe stumbling into sin or turning away from someone a person should trust; it specifically includes Matthew 24:10 in that latter use. The warning involves a serious failure of loyalty, seen here alongside betrayal and hatred.

False prophets add deception to the pressure, and increasing wickedness chills love. Jesus then promises salvation to the one who perseveres. The verb is ὑπομένω, hypomenō (hoo-poh-MEH-noh), which Thayer's entry explains here as holding fast to faith in Christ under trials. His call is to remain faithful through the pressure He has described.

The passage ends with the gospel of the kingdom being proclaimed throughout the world as a testimony to all nations, followed by the end. Persecution and betrayal do not cancel that witness. The promise stands beside the warning: Jesus prepares His followers for costly faithfulness while speaking of the message reaching beyond them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly hear a call to faithfulness through persecution and a promise that God's good news will be proclaimed among the nations. They differ over the period in view and how the warning about falling away relates to salvation.

On timing, Wesley's notes on 24:9–14 connect the passage first with the early church and Jerusalem's destruction. He understands the worldwide proclamation in light of the apostles' mission. The US Catholic bishops' note on 24:4–14 describes suffering that continues until the gospel reaches the nations and the end comes.

Christians also differ over whether the church will pass through the final tribulation. The Catholic Church teaches that it will face a final trial before Christ's return (Catechism, 675–677). Dallas Theological Seminary's statement of belief, XVIII–XX, expects Christ to gather the church into heaven before a future period of tribulation. These are different frameworks for reading the larger discourse; the seminary's statement is its own teaching, rather than a belief shared by all Protestants.

On enduring faith, Reformed churches following the Westminster Confession, 17, and Baptists following the 1689 Confession, 17, teach that God keeps those truly brought to faith so that they finally persevere, even though they may fall into serious sin for a time. Endurance is the outcome of His keeping them, rather than a way of earning salvation.

Catholic teaching says that faith is God's free gift and can be lost, and calls believers to nourish it and persevere (Catechism, 162). The Methodist Articles of Religion, XII, likewise allow that a person may depart from grace, while affirming repentance and restoration through God's grace. These readings take the danger of falling away seriously without treating every failure as beyond forgiveness.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament shows witness continuing under pressure. In Acts 4:23–31, believers respond to threats by praying for boldness, and the Spirit enables them to speak God's word. 1 Peter 4:12–19 calls those suffering for Christ to entrust themselves to their faithful Creator and continue doing good. God's purpose includes forming faithful witnesses whose conduct reflects Him even when they are opposed.

Revelation 7:9–17 gives a hopeful picture of a multitude from every nation, standing before God and the Lamb after great tribulation. Their salvation is attributed to God and the Lamb, who shepherds them and wipes away their tears. This provides a supported connection between witness among the nations, suffering, and God's saving care; it does not by itself settle every question about the timetable in Matthew.
