Passage

Isaiah 46:1-13

Who carries whom: the worshiper and his image, or God and His people?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Bel crouches; Nebo cowers. Their idols weigh down beasts and cattle. The images you carry are burdensome, a load to the weary animal. 2 The gods cower; they crouch together, unable to relieve the burden; but they themselves go into captivity. 3 “Listen to Me, O house of Jacob, all the remnant of the house of Israel, who have been sustained from the womb, carried along since birth. 4 Even to your old age, I will be the same, and I will bear you up when you turn gray. I have made you, and I will carry you; I will sustain you and deliver you.

5 To whom will you liken Me or count Me equal? To whom will you compare Me, that we should be alike? 6 They pour out their bags of gold and weigh out silver on scales; they hire a goldsmith to fashion it into a god, so they can bow down and worship. 7 They lift it to their shoulder and carry it along; they set it in its place, and there it stands, not budging from that spot. They cry out to it, but it does not answer; it saves no one from his troubles.

8 Remember this and be brave; take it to heart, you transgressors! 9 Remember what happened long ago, for I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like Me. 10 I declare the end from the beginning, and from ancient times what is still to come. I say, ‘My purpose will stand, and all My good pleasure I will accomplish.’ 11 I summon a bird of prey from the east, a man for My purpose from a far-off land. Truly I have spoken, and truly I will bring it to pass. I have planned it, and I will surely do it.

12 Listen to Me, you stubborn people, far removed from righteousness: 13 I am bringing My righteousness near; it is not far away, and My salvation will not be delayed. I will grant salvation to Zion and adorn Israel with My splendor.

What is the context?

Bel and Nebo are pictured bowing and cowering while their images become loads for weary animals. The gods cannot relieve the burden; they themselves are taken into captivity. What was carried as a source of security now needs carrying away.

The Lord’s promise to Israel reverses the picture. He has sustained His people from birth and will carry them even when they are old and gray. Their increasing weakness does not turn Him into a burden they must support.

The comparison returns to the expensive image made by a goldsmith. It stays wherever its worshiper places it and cannot answer a cry for help. The living God, by contrast, announces His purpose and brings it about.

The bird of prey from the east and the man from a distant land belong to that announced purpose. The final appeal is to stubborn people far from righteousness: the Lord is bringing His righteousness and salvation near to Zion.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry, Wesley and Gill emphasize the contrast between idols that require human support and the Lord who sustains His people. Wesley reads the carrying from birth through the history of Israel; Henry and Gill also apply the promise to believers needing care in old age.

Wesley and Gill identify the figure from the east with Cyrus, connecting it with the named commission of the previous chapters. Henry likewise emphasizes the Lord’s ability to use people to accomplish His purpose even when they are not seeking to serve Him.

The assurances of power and care belong together. The Lord is unlike the images both in what He can accomplish and in His lasting relationship with the people He made.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The picture of carrying those who need rescue has a strong parallel in Jesus’ story of the lost sheep in Luke 15:3–7. The shepherd goes after the lost sheep, puts it on his shoulders and calls others to rejoice when he brings it home.

Jesus explains the story through heaven’s joy over a sinner who repents. The need for rescue becomes an occasion for active care and joy, rather than abandonment.

This is a connection through the shared picture of saving care, not a direct quotation of Isaiah 46. It helps place the promise within the larger biblical account: the God who can carry His people also seeks their return and rejoices in their restoration.
