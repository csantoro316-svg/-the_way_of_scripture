Passage

1 Samuel 27:1-12

What does David gain—and risk—by settling among the Philistines?

Berean Standard Bible

1 David, however, said to himself, “One of these days now I will be swept away by the hand of Saul. There is nothing better for me than to escape to the land of the Philistines. Then Saul will stop searching for me all over Israel, and I will slip out of his hand.” 2 So David set out with his six hundred men and went to Achish son of Maoch, the king of Gath. 3 David and his men settled in Gath with Achish. Each man had his family with him, and David had his two wives: Ahinoam of Jezreel and Abigail of Carmel, the widow of Nabal. 4 And when Saul learned that David had fled to Gath, he no longer searched for him.

5 Then David said to Achish, “If I have found favor in your eyes, let me be assigned a place in one of the outlying towns, so I can live there. For why should your servant live in the royal city with you?” 6 That day Achish gave him Ziklag, and to this day it still belongs to the kings of Judah. 7 And the time that David lived in Philistine territory amounted to a year and four months.

8 Now David and his men went up and raided the Geshurites, the Girzites, and the Amalekites. (From ancient times these people had inhabited the land extending to Shur and Egypt.) 9 Whenever David attacked a territory, he did not leave a man or woman alive, but he took the flocks and herds, the donkeys, camels, and clothing. Then he would return to Achish, 10 who would ask him, “What have you raided today?” And David would reply, “The Negev of Judah,” or “The Negev of Jerahmeel,” or “The Negev of the Kenites.” 11 David did not leave a man or woman alive to be brought to Gath, for he said, “Otherwise they will report us, saying, ‘This is what David did.’” And this was David’s custom the whole time he lived in Philistine territory. 12 So Achish trusted David, thinking, “Since he has made himself an utter stench to his people Israel, he will be my servant forever.”

What is the context?

David tells himself that Saul will eventually kill him and decides that escape to Philistine territory is his best hope. He takes his six hundred men and their families to Achish at Gath. The move stops Saul’s pursuit, but it places the future king of Israel in the service of a Philistine ruler.

At David’s request, Achish gives him Ziklag as a place to live outside the royal city. David remains in Philistine territory for a year and four months. The note that Ziklag still belongs to Judah’s kings reflects the narrator’s own time.

From there David raids the Geshurites, Girzites and Amalekites. He takes livestock and clothing and leaves no man or woman alive. The account gives his reason for leaving no survivors: no one will be able to tell Achish what he has really been doing.

When Achish asks about the raids, David names areas associated with Judah and its neighbors. Achish believes David has turned his own people against him and will therefore remain his servant. The apparent security depends on a deception that will become dangerous when Achish expects him to fight Israel.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Henry criticize David’s fear as distrust of God’s promise after repeated protection. They recognize the immediate shelter he gains while questioning the reasoning that leads him there. The absence of a recorded inquiry contrasts with the earlier decisions he brought to the LORD.

Henry connects the raids with earlier judgment on peoples Israel had been commanded to defeat. That is his reading of their place in Israel’s history. Here the account expressly describes the killing of men and women and David’s concern to prevent reports reaching Gath; it gives no new command for these particular raids.

Wesley and Henry also condemn the misleading answers to Achish. Avoiding a plainly false sentence would not make speech honest if its purpose is to create a false impression. The USCCB Bible notes likewise identify the reports about Judah as deception.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

David’s calling does not remove the need to keep trusting God or to face the harm of his own choices. The chapter is part of a larger story in which God’s purposes continue while the chosen king still needs correction.

Hebrews 3:12–14 brings the danger of unbelief and sin’s deceit into the life of Christ’s people. It calls them to hold firmly to their confidence and to encourage one another daily. This is a shared theme, rather than a claim that Hebrews directly interprets David’s stay at Ziklag: people who have received God’s promises still need help to remain faithful to Him.
