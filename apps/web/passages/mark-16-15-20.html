Passage

Mark 16:15-20

How does the risen Lord send His witnesses?

Berean Standard Bible

15 And He said to them, “Go into all the world and preach the gospel to every creature. 16 Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved, but whoever does not believe will be condemned. 17 And these signs will accompany those who believe: In My name they will drive out demons; they will speak in new tongues; 18 they will pick up snakes with their hands, and if they drink any deadly poison, it will not harm them; they will lay their hands on the sick, and they will be made well.”

19 After the Lord Jesus had spoken to them, He was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God. 20 And they went out and preached everywhere, and the Lord worked through them, confirming His word by the signs that accompanied it.

What is the context?

Some early manuscripts end Mark at verse 8; others include these final verses. The BSB prints verses 9–20 and explains the difference in its chapter footnote. The question is whether these verses belonged to Mark’s original ending; the resurrection itself is already announced in verse 6 and is also described in the other Gospels.

After confronting the Eleven’s unbelief, Jesus sends them to proclaim the gospel throughout the world. Belief and baptism are joined in the promise of salvation; unbelief is named in the warning of condemnation. Signs accompany believers, and the closing sentence describes the Lord confirming the message through those signs.

The ending brings together Jesus’ return to heaven, His place at God’s right hand, and the witnesses’ work on earth. His departure does not mean He has stopped acting: the final verse describes Him working through those who preach. Acts gives a fuller account of His instruction, ascension, and promise of the Spirit’s power. (Acts 1:1–11)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that Jesus calls people to believe the gospel and that baptism belongs to Christian life. They differ over how baptism relates to receiving salvation.

The Catholic Church teaches that baptism is necessary for those who hear the gospel and can receive it. It also teaches that God can save beyond the ordinary reception of the sacrament, including through martyrdom or a sincere desire for baptism. This is a claim about God giving grace through baptism, not about someone earning salvation by performing a ceremony. (Catechism 1257–1261)

Luther’s Small Catechism cites this verse to teach that baptism gives forgiveness and salvation to those who believe God’s promise. The saving gift rests on God’s word and promise. (Small Catechism, Holy Baptism)

The Reformed Westminster Confession calls baptism a sign and seal of God’s grace, while explicitly denying that everyone baptized is saved or that no one can be saved without baptism. The 1689 Baptist Confession describes baptism as a sign of sharing Christ’s death and resurrection and cites this verse when limiting baptism to people who profess repentance and faith. (Westminster 28.1, 5–6; Baptist Confession 29.1–2)

Wesley explains baptism here as the outward expression of belief, and says unbelief condemns whether a person has been baptized or not. Methodist teaching also gives baptism a greater place than a public badge: its Articles describe the sacraments as ways God strengthens faith and baptism as a sign of new birth. (Wesley on Mark 16:16; Methodist Articles XVI–XVII)

Christians also differ over whether these signs describe gifts that continue throughout the church’s life or signs with a special place in its beginning. Dallas Theological Seminary teaches that the signs establishing the apostles’ authority ended with that foundational period, while affirming that God can still work miracles. The Assemblies of God teaches that the Spirit continues to empower the church’s mission with signs, including tongues and healing. These are these organizations’ stated positions; neither represents every Protestant believer. (Dallas statement, Article XVI; Assemblies of God, sections 7–8, 10 and 12)

The signs are read alongside the rest of the New Testament. Acts records Paul surviving an unexpected snakebite and praying for the sick; it does not describe him arranging a dangerous test of faith. Wesley likewise explains the poison statement as protection if it happens, not permission to experiment. Jesus’ refusal to test God also matters here. (Acts 28:1–9; Wesley on Mark 16:18; Matthew 4:5–7)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The mission belongs to the larger purpose of bringing the news of repentance and forgiveness to the nations. Luke records that purpose in the risen Jesus’ teaching, and Acts shows the witnesses receiving power for it. The strength for the task comes from God rather than from the disciples’ earlier courage or understanding. (Luke 24:44–49; Acts 1:8)

Jesus’ place at God’s right hand also looks beyond the immediate mission. Ephesians describes Him as raised above every power and made head over everything for the church. Hebrews connects signs and gifts with God’s confirmation of the salvation first announced by the Lord. Together these passages keep the focus on the risen King, the saving message, and God’s work through His people. (Ephesians 1:19–23; Hebrews 2:1–4)

What is our position?

Baptism examines this verse directly. The study observes that the promise joins belief and baptism, while the warning names unbelief as the reason for condemnation. Its conclusion is that baptism is the expected response of genuine faith, rather than a separate condition that earns salvation. The study treats baptism as commanded and meaningful. This supplies our position on verse 16; it does not settle the original ending of Mark or whether particular miraculous gifts continue today.
