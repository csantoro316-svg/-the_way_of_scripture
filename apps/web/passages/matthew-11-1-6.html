Passage

Matthew 11:1-6

John’s Question and Jesus’ Answer

Berean Standard Bible

1 After Jesus had finished instructing His twelve disciples, He went on from there to teach and preach in their cities. 2 Meanwhile John heard in prison about the works of Christ, and he sent his disciples 3 to ask Him, “Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?”

4 Jesus replied, “Go back and report to John what you hear and see: 5 The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor. 6 Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me.”

What is the context?

After instructing the Twelve, Jesus continues teaching and preaching. John the Baptist hears about His works from prison and sends disciples with a direct question: is Jesus the promised One, or should they expect someone else? Matthew records the question without explaining everything that prompted it.

Jesus answers by pointing to what the messengers can report. People are being healed, the dead are being raised, and the poor are hearing good news. These are the kinds of works Matthew has just described in chapters 8–9. In the related account, Luke 7:18–23, the messengers themselves see Jesus healing people before He sends them back.

His answer also points back to Scripture. Isaiah 35:5–6 describes sight restored, deaf ears opened, and the lame walking; Isaiah 61:1 speaks of good news for the poor. His works and His message belong together as evidence of who He is.

The final blessing calls for receiving Him without turning away because of Him. It gives the question a personal edge: the promised Savior must be received as He reveals Himself. The next verses show Jesus honoring John's ministry after the messengers leave.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readers commonly understand Jesus' works and their connection with Isaiah as His answer about His identity. Interpreters differ over whose uncertainty lies behind the question. Wesley on Matthew 11:2–6 understands John as sending the disciples to strengthen their faith, while the US Catholic bishops' note on 11:3 considers it likely that John himself is struggling with expectations Jesus has not met. These are commentators' readings, rather than separate binding church teachings. Both take the messengers' report as the answer John is to receive.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus connects His mission with Isaiah even more directly in Luke 4:16–21. After reading the promise of good news and release, He declares its fulfillment in the hearing of those present. God's saving purpose is taking shape in a person who teaches, heals, and restores.

The healing here also belongs within the Bible's wider hope of life with God. Revelation 21:1–5 looks ahead to a new creation where death and pain are gone. That is a wider connection, not a claim that every part of that future has already arrived in John's lifetime. The works he hears about give a real glimpse of the restoration God promises.
