Passage

1 Samuel 19:1-24

How is David protected when Saul openly orders his death?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then Saul ordered his son Jonathan and all his servants to kill David. But Jonathan delighted greatly in David, 2 so he warned David, saying, “My father Saul intends to kill you. Be on your guard in the morning; find a secret place and hide there. 3 I will go out and stand beside my father in the field where you are, so I can ask about you. And if I find out anything, I will tell you.” 4 Then Jonathan spoke well of David to his father Saul and said to him, “The king should not sin against his servant David; he has not sinned against you. In fact, his actions have been highly beneficial to you. 5 He took his life in his hands when he struck down the Philistine, and the LORD worked a great salvation for all Israel. You saw it and rejoiced, so why would you sin against innocent blood by killing David for no reason?” 6 Saul listened to the voice of Jonathan and swore an oath: “As surely as the LORD lives, David will not be put to death.” 7 So Jonathan summoned David and told him all these things. Then Jonathan brought David to Saul, and David was with Saul as before.

8 When war broke out again, David went out and fought the Philistines and struck them with such a mighty blow that they fled before him. 9 But as Saul was sitting in his house with his spear in his hand, a spirit of distress from the LORD came upon him. While David was playing the harp, 10 Saul tried to pin him to the wall with his spear. But David eluded him and the spear struck the wall. And David fled and escaped that night.

11 Then Saul sent messengers to David’s house to watch him and kill him in the morning. But David’s wife Michal warned him, “If you do not run for your life tonight, tomorrow you will be dead!” 12 So Michal lowered David from the window, and he ran away and escaped. 13 Then Michal took a household idol and laid it in the bed, placed some goat hair on its head, and covered it with a garment. 14 When Saul sent the messengers to seize David, Michal said, “He is ill.” 15 But Saul sent the messengers back to see David and told them, “Bring him up to me in his bed so I can kill him.” 16 And when the messengers entered, there was the idol in the bed with the goat hair on its head. 17 And Saul said to Michal, “Why did you deceive me like this? You sent my enemy away, and he has escaped!” Michal replied, “He said to me, ‘Help me get away, or I will kill you!’”

18 So David ran away and escaped. And he went to Samuel at Ramah and told him all that Saul had done to him. Then he and Samuel went to Naioth and stayed there. 19 When Saul was told that David was at Naioth in Ramah, 20 he sent messengers to seize him. But when they saw the group of prophets prophesying, with Samuel leading them, the Spirit of God came upon them, and Saul’s messengers also began to prophesy. 21 When this was reported to Saul, he sent more messengers, but they began to prophesy as well. So Saul tried again and sent messengers a third time, and even they began to prophesy. 22 Finally, Saul himself left for Ramah and came to the large cistern at Secu, where he asked, “Where are Samuel and David?” “At Naioth in Ramah,” he was told. 23 So Saul went to Naioth in Ramah. But the Spirit of God came upon even Saul, and he walked along prophesying until he came to Naioth in Ramah. 24 Then Saul stripped off his robes and also prophesied before Samuel. And he collapsed and lay naked all that day and night. That is why it is said, “Is Saul also among the prophets?”

What is the context?

Saul now tells Jonathan and his servants openly to kill David. Jonathan warns his friend, then appeals to his father: David has served him well, risked his life against Goliath and helped bring deliverance to Israel. Killing him would shed innocent blood. Saul listens and swears that David will not be put to death, allowing a brief return to their earlier relationship.

That peace does not last. After another victory over the Philistines, David is playing the harp when Saul throws his spear. David escapes, but Saul sends men to watch his house and kill him in the morning. Michal warns him and lowers him through a window. She puts a household idol in his bed, adds goat hair and a covering, and says he is ill. The disguise delays the messengers until David has gone.

When Saul confronts her, Michal says David threatened to kill her if she would not help him escape. That is her explanation to Saul; the account has just shown her urging David to flee. The household idol is also an unsettling detail, but the chapter does not identify its owner or explain why it was there.

David reaches Samuel at Ramah, and they stay at Naioth. Saul sends three groups to seize him. Each begins to prophesy when the Spirit of God comes upon them. Finally Saul goes himself, only to be overtaken in the same way. He strips off his robes and lies naked before Samuel through the day and night. The king who came to capture David is unable to carry out his purpose.

The question about Saul being among the prophets recalls his earlier experience in chapter10. Here it comes at a very different point in his life: the Spirit interrupts his pursuit of the man he wants dead. David is protected through Jonathan’s appeal, Michal’s help and an intervention Saul cannot overpower.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley highlights Jonathan’s courage in defending an innocent friend before his own father. He takes Saul’s oath as sincerely intended at that moment. Henry emphasizes the deeper hostility that survives the temporary change. These readings agree about the brief reconciliation while placing different weight on Saul’s immediate intention and lasting character.

Henry distinguishes Michal’s effort to gain time for David from her false statements, especially the claim that he threatened her. Gill also sees that accusation as protecting herself at David’s expense. The rescue is real, but these commentators do not treat every action taken during it as morally approved.

Wesley and Gill explain Saul’s nakedness as the removal of his outer garments. They read the scene as God restraining him long enough for David to escape. The BSB retains the description naked; the commentators’ explanation concerns the extent of undress, rather than a different event.

Henry draws a further distinction between receiving a striking spiritual gift and having a changed heart. Saul’s prophesying does not erase his pursuit of David. The event shows God’s power over him without announcing that his character has been restored.

The USCCB Bible notes see a tension between Saul appearing before Samuel here and the statement in 15:35 that Samuel did not visit Saul again, and read it as evidence of different accounts brought together. Gill’s comment on 15:35 distinguishes Samuel visiting Saul from Saul coming to Samuel. These are explanations of how the accounts relate; the narrative here places Saul before Samuel.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Psalm59 places this danger within David’s life of prayer: its heading identifies the time when Saul sent men to watch his house and kill him. The psalm moves from an urgent plea for rescue to confidence that God is his fortress. David’s innocence of the accusation does not mean he feels no fear or has no need to ask for help.

That theme of refuge reaches further in Hebrews6:17–20. Those who take hold of God’s promised hope are encouraged by His faithfulness, and their secure hope rests in Jesus, who has entered God’s presence on their behalf. This is a shared theme, rather than a claim that Hebrews directly interprets David’s escape.

God’s purpose is larger than keeping one night free from danger. David’s rescue preserves the life of the king God has chosen, while his prayer gives later readers words for trusting God under threat. In Christ, the invitation to seek refuge rests on a hope that reaches beyond immediate circumstances.
