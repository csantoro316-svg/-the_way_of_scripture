Passage

Acts 4:13-22

Why do Peter and John refuse to stop speaking?

Berean Standard Bible

13 When they saw the boldness of Peter and John and realized that they were unschooled, ordinary men, they marveled and took note that these men had been with Jesus. 14 And seeing the man who had been healed standing there with them, they had nothing to say in response. 15 So they ordered them to leave the Sanhedrin and then conferred together. 16 “What shall we do with these men?” they asked. “It is clear to everyone living in Jerusalem that a remarkable miracle has occurred through them, and we cannot deny it. 17 But to keep this message from spreading any further among the people, we must warn them not to speak to anyone in this name.”

18 Then they called them in again and commanded them not to speak or teach at all in the name of Jesus. 19 But Peter and John replied, “Judge for yourselves whether it is right in God’s sight to listen to you rather than God. 20 For we cannot stop speaking about what we have seen and heard.” 21 After further threats they let them go. They could not find a way to punish them, because all the people were glorifying God for what had happened. 22 For the man who was miraculously healed was over forty years old.

What is the context?

The council sees the apostles’ boldness and recognizes them as ordinary, unschooled men who have been with Jesus. The healed man is standing beside them. The leaders cannot deny that a remarkable miracle has occurred.

Their response is to try to stop the message spreading. They order Peter and John not to speak or teach in Jesus’s name. The apostles answer by placing the command alongside their responsibility to God and to what they have seen and heard.

The leaders threaten them again but let them go because of the people, who are praising God. Luke adds that the healed man is over forty years old. His long-standing condition gives weight to the public recognition described in the preceding chapter.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the refusal as obedience to God’s commission when a human command directly forbids it. Calvin on verse 19 emphasizes God’s authority over conflicting human decrees, while Wesley on verse 13 observes that God works through ordinary people without making lack of learning a requirement for service.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Luke 21:12–15, Jesus says that arrest and questioning will become opportunities to bear witness. The command to remain silent becomes just such an occasion: Peter and John explain why they must continue speaking about Him.

The mission’s progress is therefore not measured only by whether its messengers avoid opposition. Their task is to testify faithfully, with the help Jesus promised, even when those hearing them try to prevent the message from going further.
