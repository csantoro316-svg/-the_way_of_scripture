Passage

Exodus 15:1-21

What does Israel’s song tell us about the God who rescued them?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then Moses and the Israelites sang this song to the LORD: “I will sing to the LORD, for He is highly exalted. The horse and rider He has thrown into the sea. 2 The LORD is my strength and my song, and He has become my salvation. He is my God, and I will praise Him, my father’s God, and I will exalt Him. 3 The LORD is a warrior, the LORD is His name. 4 Pharaoh’s chariots and army He has cast into the sea; the finest of his officers are drowned in the Red Sea. 5 The depths have covered them; they sank there like a stone.

6 Your right hand, O LORD, is majestic in power; Your right hand, O LORD, has shattered the enemy. 7 You overthrew Your adversaries by Your great majesty. You unleashed Your burning wrath; it consumed them like stubble. 8 At the blast of Your nostrils the waters piled up; like a wall the currents stood firm; the depths congealed in the heart of the sea. 9 The enemy declared, ‘I will pursue, I will overtake. I will divide the spoils; I will gorge myself on them. I will draw my sword; my hand will destroy them.’ 10 But You blew with Your breath, and the sea covered them. They sank like lead in the mighty waters.

11 Who among the gods is like You, O LORD? Who is like You—majestic in holiness, revered with praises, performing wonders? 12 You stretched out Your right hand, and the earth swallowed them up. 13 With loving devotion You will lead the people You have redeemed; with Your strength You will guide them to Your holy dwelling. 14 The nations will hear and tremble; anguish will grip the dwellers of Philistia. 15 Then the chiefs of Edom will be dismayed; trembling will seize the leaders of Moab; those who dwell in Canaan will melt away, 16 and terror and dread will fall on them. By the power of Your arm they will be as still as a stone until Your people pass by, O LORD, until the people You have bought pass by. 17 You will bring them in and plant them on the mountain of Your inheritance— the place, O LORD, You have prepared for Your dwelling, the sanctuary, O Lord, Your hands have established. 18 The LORD will reign forever and ever!”

19 For when Pharaoh’s horses, chariots, and horsemen went into the sea, the LORD brought the waters of the sea back over them. But the Israelites walked through the sea on dry ground. 20 Then Miriam the prophetess, Aaron’s sister, took a tambourine in her hand, and all the women followed her with tambourines and dancing. 21 And Miriam sang back to them: “Sing to the LORD, for He is highly exalted; the horse and rider He has thrown into the sea.”

What is the context?

The people who cried out in fear now sing about the LORD’s victory. Moses and Israel recount the destruction of Pharaoh’s army, confess that their strength and rescue come from God, and praise Him as the warrior no enemy can overcome. Miriam, named as a prophetess, leads the women with tambourines and dancing and answers with the song’s opening refrain.

The song retells the crossing through vivid pictures: the enemy sinks like stone and lead, and God’s breath piles up the water. Its paired lines return to the same events from different angles, giving the people words with which to remember and praise. Verse 19 brings the song back to the event itself: the army went into the sea, the water returned and Israel walked through on dry ground.

The song also looks ahead. The people God has redeemed are to be led to His dwelling, while surrounding nations hear and tremble. Joshua 2:8–13 later gives a concrete example: Rahab says Jericho has heard of the crossing and lost courage. Yet she also acknowledges the LORD as God and seeks protection for her household. The news can lead to an appeal for mercy as well as fear.

The praise of God’s rule also speaks into a world with rival claims about divine power. The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes Ugaritic tablets in which Baal defeats the sea god Yamm and gains a royal palace. That is useful cultural background for hearing a song about the LORD’s power over the sea, His dwelling and His reign. The song itself directs Israel’s worship to the LORD who has actually delivered them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The central reading is praise for God’s saving power and confidence that He will bring His people to the place He has promised. Wesley and Calvin both connect Israel’s thanksgiving with that continuing hope.

There are different ways of relating the poem to the preceding account. Calvin reads it as a poetic celebration confirming the events just narrated. The Catholic USCCB study notes propose that the poem preserves a different telling of the victory, emphasizing a storm at sea. This is a proposal in those study notes, rather than an official definition binding all Catholics.

Miriam’s instruments also receive different applications to later worship. Calvin regards them as belonging to Old Testament ceremonies that Christians should leave behind. The Catholic Church’s Catechism, 1156–1158, describes the church as continuing and developing the older tradition of sacred song and music. Calvin’s application is his own stated position here; it should not be treated as the practice of every Reformed church.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 15:1–4 explicitly names the song of God’s servant Moses and of the Lamb. Those who have overcome the beast stand beside a sea and praise God’s great works, just ways and holiness. The rescue celebrated in Exodus thus belongs to a larger story of God defeating oppressive powers and bringing His people to worship Him.

Revelation gives the words sung there; they are not simply a repeat of every line in Exodus 15. The connection rests on the named song, deliverance, divine judgment and worship. It does not require treating every image in Israel’s song as a separate prediction.

The hope of being brought to God’s dwelling also reaches beyond the wilderness journey. Hebrews 12:22–24 describes believers as having come to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant. This is a broader connection in Scripture: God rescues a people and brings them into His presence. Hebrews describes that relationship as already opened to believers through Jesus.
