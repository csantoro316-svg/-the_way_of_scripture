Passage

1 Samuel 24:1-15

Why does David spare Saul when he has the chance to kill him?

Berean Standard Bible

1 After Saul had returned from pursuing the Philistines, he was told, “David is in the wilderness of En-gedi.” 2 So Saul took three thousand chosen men from all Israel and went to look for David and his men in the region of the Rocks of the Wild Goats. 3 Soon Saul came to the sheepfolds along the road, where there was a cave, and he went in to relieve himself. And David and his men were hiding in the recesses of the cave. 4 So David’s men said to him, “This is the day about which the LORD said to you, ‘Behold, I will deliver your enemy into your hand, that you may do with him as you wish.’” Then David crept up and stealthily cut off a corner of Saul’s robe. 5 Afterward, David’s conscience was stricken because he had cut off the corner of Saul’s robe. 6 So he said to his men, “The LORD forbid that I should do such a thing to my master, the LORD’s anointed. May I never lift my hand against him, since he is the LORD’s anointed.” 7 With these words David restrained his men, and he did not let them rise up against Saul. Then Saul left the cave and went on his way.

8 After that, David got up, went out of the cave, and called out to Saul, “My lord the king!” When Saul looked behind him, David bowed facedown in reverence 9 and said to Saul, “Why do you listen to the words of men who say, ‘Look, David intends to harm you’? 10 Behold, this day you have seen with your own eyes that the LORD delivered you into my hand in the cave. I was told to kill you, but I spared you and said, ‘I will not lift my hand against my lord, since he is the LORD’s anointed.’ 11 See, my father, look at the corner of your robe in my hand. For I cut it off, but I did not kill you. Know and see that there is no evil or rebellion in my hands. I have not sinned against you, even though you are hunting me down to take my life. 12 May the LORD judge between you and me, and may the LORD take vengeance on you, but my hand will never be against you. 13 As the old proverb says, ‘Wickedness proceeds from the wicked.’ But my hand will never be against you. 14 Against whom has the king of Israel come out? Whom are you pursuing? A dead dog? A flea? 15 May the LORD be our judge and decide between you and me. May He take notice and plead my case and deliver me from your hand.”

What is the context?

Saul returns from pursuing the Philistines and takes three thousand chosen men to look for David near En-gedi. He enters a cave to relieve himself, unaware that David and his men are hiding farther inside. David’s men interpret the opportunity as the moment to deal with his enemy.

David creeps forward and cuts a corner from Saul’s robe, but afterward his conscience troubles him. The Hebrew word for the garment’s corner is kanaph (kah-NAHF). Brown–Driver–Briggs gives skirt, corner or loose end as its garment sense. The same word describes the hem Saul seized from Samuel in 1 Samuel 15:27–28, when the torn robe accompanied the announcement of Saul’s lost kingdom.

David refuses to lift his hand against the LORD’s anointed and restrains his men. After Saul leaves the cave, David follows and calls to him respectfully. He holds up the piece of robe as evidence that he could have killed Saul but chose to spare him.

He denies intending rebellion or harm and asks the LORD to judge between them, plead his case and deliver him. Calling himself a dead dog or a flea exposes how far the king’s pursuit exceeds the threat David presents. David seeks justice while refusing to make himself Saul’s executioner.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the men’s appeal to God’s promise as their interpretation of David’s calling, rather than a recorded command to kill Saul. Henry makes the same distinction: the promise of a kingdom does not itself authorize murder as the means of obtaining it.

Gill reads David’s troubled conscience as regret for an indignity toward his sovereign, even though he has refused the greater harm his men urged. David then uses the cut cloth to demonstrate his restraint. His speech to Saul is a defense against treason and murderous intent, rather than an admission that he has been plotting Saul’s death.

Henry emphasizes David’s refusal to avenge himself and his choice to return good for evil. The appeal to God’s judgment makes room for justice without turning the chance to retaliate into a command to do so.

Wesley understands the cave visit as rest or sleep; Gill discusses that reading as well as relieving himself. The BSB uses the latter. The shared point is Saul’s vulnerability and David’s refusal to exploit it by killing him.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

David’s restraint illustrates the wider biblical call to leave personal vengeance with God. Romans 12:17–21 tells believers to refuse repayment of evil with evil, seek peace where possible and overcome evil with good. David’s appeal for God to judge gives a concrete picture of that trust.

The greater example is Jesus in 1 Peter 2:21–25. He does not retaliate against abuse but entrusts Himself to the One who judges justly, and He bears sins to bring people into a life of righteousness. David spares the king who threatens him; Jesus carries that self-giving faithfulness into His saving death.

This parallel does not make Saul’s conduct acceptable or require someone facing violence to remain within reach of it. David has fled danger and continues to seek deliverance. His refusal of vengeance belongs alongside that pursuit of safety and justice.
