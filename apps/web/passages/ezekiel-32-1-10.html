Passage

Ezekiel 32:1-10

What happens when the Lord catches Pharaoh in His net?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In the twelfth year, on the first day of the twelfth month, the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, take up a lament for Pharaoh king of Egypt and say to him: ‘You are like a lion among the nations; you are like a monster in the seas. You thrash about in your rivers, churning up the waters with your feet and muddying the streams.’ 3 This is what the Lord GOD says: ‘I will spread My net over you with a company of many peoples, and they will draw you up in My net. 4 I will abandon you on the land and hurl you into the open field. I will cause all the birds of the air to settle upon you, and all the beasts of the earth to eat their fill of you.

5 I will put your flesh on the mountains and fill the valleys with your remains. 6 I will drench the land with the flow of your blood, all the way to the mountains— the ravines will be filled. 7 When I extinguish you, I will cover the heavens and darken their stars. I will cover the sun with a cloud, and the moon will not give its light. 8 All the shining lights in the heavens I will darken over you, and I will bring darkness upon your land,’ declares the Lord GOD.

9 ‘I will trouble the hearts of many peoples, when I bring about your destruction among the nations, in countries you do not know. 10 I will cause many peoples to be appalled over you, and their kings will shudder in horror because of you when I brandish My sword before them. On the day of your downfall each of them will tremble every moment for his life.’

What is the context?

The Lord commands a lament for Pharaoh. He is pictured as a lion among the nations and a monster thrashing in the waters, churning and muddying the streams.

The Lord will spread a net with a company of peoples and haul the creature onto land. The exposed remains become food for birds and animals, and the land is drenched with blood.

The scene expands to darkened stars, sun and moon. Egypt’s fall shakes the hearts of peoples in lands it has not known, and kings tremble for their own lives. The ruler who spread disturbance now becomes a warning that reaches far beyond his borders.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the monster’s activity as the harmful effect of Pharaoh’s armies on neighboring peoples. Henry sees the beast picture as exposing an oppressor beneath the appearance of worldly grandeur.

Cambridge reads the darkened heavens as the shock of so great a power’s destruction, using language also associated with the Day of the Lord. Henry also carries the judgment picture toward the defeat of Christ’s enemies. The wider connection should be distinguished from assigning every detail to one exact future calendar.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A supported parallel appears in Matthew 24:29–31: darkened sun and moon, shaken heavenly powers, mourning peoples and the coming of the Son of Man. Jesus also speaks there of His angels gathering His elect.

The shared prophetic language connects the humbling of earthly power with the larger display of God’s authority. Matthew does not explicitly identify Ezekiel’s Egyptian judgment as that same event. Its additional emphasis is the Son’s glory and the gathering of His people, alongside the terror associated with judgment.
