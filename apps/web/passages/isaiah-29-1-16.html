Passage

Isaiah 29:1-16

What happens when worship continues but the heart is far from God?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Woe to you, O Ariel, the city of Ariel where David camped! Year upon year let your festivals recur. 2 And I will constrain Ariel, and there will be mourning and lamentation; she will be like an altar hearth before Me. 3 I will camp in a circle around you; I will besiege you with towers and set up siege works against you. 4 You will be brought low, you will speak from the ground, and out of the dust your words will be muffled. Your voice will be like a spirit from the ground; your speech will whisper out of the dust.

5 But your many foes will be like fine dust, the multitude of the ruthless like blowing chaff. Then suddenly, in an instant, 6 you will be visited by the LORD of Hosts with thunder and earthquake and loud noise, with windstorm and tempest and consuming flame of fire. 7 All the many nations going out to battle against Ariel— even all who war against her, laying siege and attacking her— will be like a dream, like a vision in the night, 8 as when a hungry man dreams he is eating, then awakens still hungry; as when a thirsty man dreams he is drinking, then awakens faint and parched. So will it be for all the many nations who go to battle against Mount Zion.

9 Stop and be astonished; blind yourselves and be sightless; be drunk, but not with wine; stagger, but not from strong drink. 10 For the LORD has poured out on you a spirit of deep sleep. He has shut your eyes, O prophets; He has covered your heads, O seers. 11 And the entire vision will be to you like the words sealed in a scroll. If it is handed to someone to read, he will say, “I cannot, because it is sealed.” 12 Or if the scroll is handed to one unable to read, he will say, “I cannot read.”

13 Therefore the Lord said: “These people draw near to Me with their mouths and honor Me with their lips, but their hearts are far from Me. Their worship of Me is but rules taught by men. 14 Therefore I will again confound these people with wonder upon wonder. The wisdom of the wise will vanish, and the intelligence of the intelligent will be hidden.” 15 Woe to those who dig deep to hide their plans from the LORD. In darkness they do their works and say, “Who sees us, and who will know?” 16 You have turned things upside down, as if the potter were regarded as clay. Shall what is formed say to him who formed it, “He did not make me”? Can the pottery say of the potter, “He has no understanding”?

What is the context?

Ariel is the name used here for Jerusalem, the city where David camped. Its festivals keep returning, but the Lord announces mourning and a siege. The city will be brought so low that its speech is pictured as a whisper from the dust.

The name itself carries more than one association. The Brown–Driver–Briggs lexicon discusses Ariel, pronounced ah-ree-EL, as a name for Jerusalem and records both a lion-related explanation and an altar-hearth explanation. The passage makes the altar connection explicit in verse 2: the city will become like an altar hearth before God.

The scene then turns to the nations attacking Zion. In the BSB, the foes become like dust and chaff, and their expected satisfaction is compared with a hungry person dreaming of food and waking still hungry. The Lord’s intervention overturns what appears certain.

The deeper trouble is a refusal to see. Prophets and seers are covered in sleep, and God’s message is like a sealed scroll that neither the reader nor the nonreader receives. Religious words continue, but the heart is far away. Those who imagine they can hide their plans from God have reversed the relationship between the potter and the clay.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and the USCCB notes read the sudden change in verses 5–8 as the defeat of Jerusalem’s attackers, with the Assyrian threat in view. Gill instead reads much of the opening as Jerusalem’s destruction by Rome; he takes the dust and chaff to describe the attackers’ numbers and speed. The readings differ over how the siege and the sudden reversal fit together.

For the worship warning, Henry, Wesley and Gill emphasize the gap between outward religious practice and a heart that receives God’s word. Jesus’ own use of verse 13 makes the danger of human rules replacing obedience especially important.

Where traditions differ is in how they distinguish faithful tradition from the human rules condemned here. The Catholic Church, in Catechism 80–83, teaches that Scripture and apostolic Tradition pass on the same revelation and deserve equal reverence. It distinguishes that apostolic inheritance from later local customs, which can change or be abandoned.

The Reformed Westminster Confession 1.6 and the 1689 Baptist Confession 1.6 teach that everything necessary for salvation, faith and life is stated in Scripture or follows from it, and that human traditions must not add to it. Both also allow practical arrangements governed by Scripture’s general rules. These documents explain a difference over authority; none presents a license to replace God’s command with a human preference.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus directly quotes Isaiah’s warning about lips and hearts in Matthew 15:1–9 and Mark 7:6–13. His example is a tradition that allows people to avoid helping their parents. The problem has visible consequences: religious language is being used to set aside what God commands.

Paul directly quotes the warning about the wisdom of the wise in 1 Corinthians 1:18–25. He explains it through the cross: Christ crucified appears foolish or offensive to human expectations, yet He is God’s power and wisdom for those who are called, both Jews and Greeks.

Romans 11:1–12 also draws on Isaiah’s language about deep sleep. Paul immediately keeps that warning from becoming a claim that God has rejected His people: he points to a remnant, denies that the stumble is beyond recovery, and later describes the hardening as partial. God’s unfolding purpose brings mercy to Gentiles without giving them grounds for pride over Israel.

What is our position?

In [Worship as the Early Church Knew It](../studies/worship.html), we read Jesus’ use of this passage as a warning against honoring God in appearance while withholding obedience from the heart. Human tradition cannot make hollow reverence faithful.
