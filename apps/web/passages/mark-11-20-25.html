Passage

Mark 11:20-25

Faith, Prayer, and Forgiveness

Berean Standard Bible

20 As they were walking back in the morning, they saw the fig tree withered from its roots. 21 Peter remembered it and said, “Look, Rabbi! The fig tree You cursed has withered.” 22 “Have faith in God,” Jesus said to them. 23 “Truly I tell you that if anyone says to this mountain, ‘Be lifted up and thrown into the sea,’ and has no doubt in his heart but believes that it will happen, it will be done for him. 24 Therefore I tell you, whatever you ask for in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours.

25 And when you stand to pray, if you hold anything against another, forgive it, so that your Father in heaven will forgive your trespasses as well.”

What is the context?

The next morning, the disciples see the fig tree withered from its roots. Peter draws Jesus' attention to it, and Jesus directs them to faith in God. The tree's condition closes the sequence that has placed His temple confrontation between the warning to the tree and its visible result.

Faith here is πίστις (pistis, PIS-tis), trust or confidence. Thayer specifically explains Mark 11:22 as trusting in God. Jesus' opening instruction identifies the One on whom that confidence rests.

The mountain thrown into the sea is a striking picture of what is beyond human ability. Jesus calls for undivided trust and confidence in prayer. The words about believing one has received are strong; the following instruction about forgiveness is part of the same teaching, rather than an unrelated addition.

Jesus then tells people praying to forgive what they hold against another, connecting their conduct with receiving the Father's forgiveness. Prayer is presented within relationship with God and with other people. Confidence before the Father belongs alongside a willingness to release grievances.

Other passages help readers consider the promise within the wider teaching on prayer. 1 John 5:14–15 joins confidence with asking according to God's will, while James 4:1–3 warns about requests driven by selfish motives. Mark 14:32–36 later shows Jesus making an honest request while surrendering to His Father's will.

Verse 26 appears in the BSB footnotes, where some manuscripts add a further warning about refusing to forgive. The quotation above follows the main text and ends this group at verse 25.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly take this as a strong invitation to confident prayer and a serious call to forgive. They differ over how confidently a believer may claim a particular outcome before it occurs and what role spoken declarations play.

The Catholic Catechism 2610–2611 directly uses this promise to encourage bold faith while placing the praying heart within the Father's will. Its discussion of trust in prayer, 2734–2741, stresses trust in the Father, the transformation of the person praying, and perseverance when the expected result is delayed. Reformed Westminster 21.3 and the 1689 Baptist Confession 22.3 likewise place faithful prayer in Christ's name, with the Spirit's help, according to God's will.

Within Word of Faith teaching, Kenneth Copeland Ministries' explanation of this passage puts particular emphasis on speaking the desired result, believing it received, and acting on the promise before circumstances change, including in matters of healing or prosperity. The ministry also says prayer should agree with God's Word. This is that ministry's stated approach, not the position of every Pentecostal or charismatic Christian.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Prayer belongs within the relationship God gives through His Son and Spirit. Romans 8:14–17,26–27 describes believers receiving the Spirit of adoption and calling God Father. That same Spirit helps them when they do not know how to pray and intercedes according to God's will. The wider picture is people brought into God's family and helped in their weakness, not left to make prayer depend on their own ability.

The call to forgive also flows from what God accomplishes in Christ. Ephesians 4:29–32 connects life in the Spirit with putting bitterness and malice aside and forgiving one another as God has forgiven in Christ. The faith Jesus calls for is to shape a community whose relationships show the mercy it has received.

What is our position?

In Prayer and Faith, in the discussion of Mark 11:24, we read the promise within the fig-tree and temple setting and alongside Scripture's teaching about God's will. Our conclusion is that Jesus calls for trust in God's purpose, rather than confidence that personal desire can determine the result. The study compares this promise with Jesus' surrender in Gethsemane and Paul's prayer about his thorn. We retain both the invitation to ask and the call to rest in God.
