Passage

James 5:13-16

How should we care for one another in suffering and sickness?

Berean Standard Bible

13 Is any one of you suffering? He should pray. Is anyone cheerful? He should sing praises. 14 Is any one of you sick? He should call the elders of the church to pray over him and anoint him with oil in the name of the Lord. 15 And the prayer offered in faith will restore the one who is sick. The Lord will raise him up. If he has sinned, he will be forgiven.

16 Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous man has great power to prevail.

What is the context?

James calls suffering people to pray and cheerful people to sing praises. Neither condition is to leave God out of the picture. When someone is sick, the community’s elders are to be called to pray and anoint the person with oil in the Lord’s name.

The Lord is the One who raises the sick person up. James also speaks of forgiveness if the person has sinned, then calls believers to confess and pray for one another. The care embraces a person’s life before God as well as the condition that prompted the request.

The word translated sick can describe weakness or physical illness, and the word behind restore can describe physical rescue or salvation. Those words do not by themselves exclude bodily healing or settle every question about the promise. The conditional statement about sin does not say that every illness is punishment for a personal offense.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The Catholic Church understands this anointing as a sacrament for the sick (Catechism, 1510–1513). It describes spiritual strengthening and bodily healing if God wills (1520). This is not an unconditional guarantee of physical recovery.

Calvin reads the anointing as a sign associated with the early church’s temporary gift of healing. Wesley instead thinks the healing practice was intended to continue, and understands verse 16’s healing to include spiritual diseases. Their different accounts should not be treated as one Protestant view.

On confession, Wesley emphasizes the instruction’s mutual character: believers may confess to others who can pray for them, rather than only to elders. Catholic teaching also recognizes confession and forgiveness within the church’s priestly ministry (Catechism, 1441–1448); it teaches that the forgiveness itself comes from God.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Mark 6:12–13 connects repentance, the disciples’ mission and anointing sick people with oil. James brings prayer and care into the ongoing life of the community.

John 9:1–3 prevents a simple diagnosis of every illness as someone’s personal sin. 2 Corinthians 12:7–10 also shows Christ sustaining a servant through weakness after a request for its removal. Together these passages keep care, restoration and trust in Christ’s strength in view.

What is our position?

Our study Prayer and Faith reads this passage as a call to seek spiritual restoration and renewed fellowship with God, with physical recovery remaining in His hands. It understands faithful prayer as dependence on God’s will, rather than an unconditional promise of healing produced by the strength of someone’s belief. Our study Baptism understands the mutual confession here as accountability and healing among believers, rather than a priestly requirement for receiving God’s forgiveness.
