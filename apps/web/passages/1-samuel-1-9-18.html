Passage

1 Samuel 1:9-18

Can Hannah bring this grief to God?

Berean Standard Bible

9 So after they had finished eating and drinking in Shiloh, Hannah stood up. Now Eli the priest was sitting on a chair by the doorpost of the temple of the LORD. 10 In her bitter distress, Hannah prayed to the LORD and wept with many tears. 11 And she made a vow, saying, “O LORD of Hosts, if only You will look upon the affliction of Your maidservant and remember me, not forgetting Your maidservant but giving her a son, then I will dedicate him to the LORD all the days of his life, and no razor shall ever touch his head.”

12 As Hannah kept on praying before the LORD, Eli watched her mouth. 13 Hannah was praying in her heart, and though her lips were moving, her voice could not be heard. So Eli thought she was drunk 14 and said to her, “How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine!” 15 “No, my lord,” Hannah replied. “I am a woman troubled in spirit. I have not had any wine or strong drink, but I have poured out my soul before the LORD. 16 Do not take your servant for a wicked woman, for all this time I have been praying out of the depth of my anguish and grief.”

17 “Go in peace,” Eli replied, “and may the God of Israel grant the petition you have asked of Him.” 18 “May your maidservant find favor with you,” said Hannah. Then she went on her way, and she began to eat, and her face was no longer downcast.

What is the context?

Hannah brings her distress directly to the LORD. She asks for a son and vows to dedicate him to God for his entire life. Her promise that no razor will touch his head marks a life set apart for God; compare the uncut hair in the Nazirite instructions in Numbers 6:1–8. A Nazirite was someone specially dedicated to God under those instructions.

Her prayer is silent: her lips move, but Eli cannot hear her voice. He mistakes this for drunkenness and rebukes her. Hannah explains that she has been pouring out her grief before the LORD. The priest has seen her outward appearance and misunderstood what is happening.

Eli then sends her away in peace and asks that God grant her petition. His words are a blessing, phrased as a request to God. Hannah begins to eat, and her face is no longer downcast. The change comes before she has a child; the narrator does not report that her circumstances have already changed.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads this as a clear example of bringing a particular need to God, rather than speaking only in general terms. He also warns against Eli’s rushed judgment. Hannah’s distress is not proof of drunkenness, and she answers the accusation by explaining herself.

Wesley understands the lifelong dedication as a permanent Nazirite commitment. He also sees God’s Spirit giving Hannah assurance through Eli’s words. That assurance is Wesley’s explanation of her changed expression; the narrator states the visible change without describing every thought or inward experience behind it. Neither the vow nor the blessing supplies a promise that every person who follows the same steps will receive a child.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hannah can speak to God even when the priest misunderstands her. This belongs within the Bible’s wider theme of bringing human weakness before God. Hebrews 4:14–16 presents Jesus as the great high priest who understands our weaknesses and invites His people to approach God for mercy and help.

This is a connection between the passages, rather than a claim that Eli represents Jesus in every detail. Eli misreads Hannah; Jesus’ care rests on His full understanding. The larger hope is access to God through a compassionate and faithful priest, even when other people fail to understand our distress.

What is our position?

Our study [Prayer and Faith](../studies/prayer-and-faith.html) reads Hannah’s request as an honest desire placed within God’s purposes. She asks for a son and promises his whole life to God’s service. The account says that God remembered her; it does not make the answer a reward for reaching a certain level of persistence or emotion. Samuel’s life will serve a purpose much larger than the relief of Hannah’s sorrow.
