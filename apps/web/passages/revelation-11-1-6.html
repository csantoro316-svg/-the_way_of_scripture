Passage

Revelation 11:1-6

What do the measured temple and the two witnesses show?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I was given a measuring rod like a staff and was told, “Go and measure the temple of God and the altar, and count the number of worshipers there. 2 But exclude the courtyard outside the temple. Do not measure it, because it has been given over to the nations, and they will trample the holy city for 42 months.

3 And I will empower my two witnesses, and they will prophesy for 1,260 days, clothed in sackcloth.” 4 These witnesses are the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth. 5 If anyone wants to harm them, fire proceeds from their mouths and devours their enemies. In this way, anyone who wants to harm them must be killed. 6 These witnesses have power to shut the sky so that no rain will fall during the days of their prophecy, and power to turn the waters into blood and to strike the earth with every kind of plague as often as they wish.

What is the context?

John is given a measuring rod and told to measure God’s temple and altar and count the worshipers. The outer courtyard is excluded because it has been given to the nations, who will trample the holy city for forty-two months. The vision distinguishes what is measured from what is handed over to hostile powers.

The focus then turns to two witnesses who receive authority to prophesy for 1,260 days. They wear sackcloth, clothing associated with mourning. Their task is to speak during a period of opposition, with power to defend their testimony.

The witnesses are called two olive trees and two lampstands standing before the Lord of the earth. This language points back to Zechariah 4, where olive trees stand beside a lampstand and God promises to complete His work by His Spirit. Their strength comes from the One they serve.

Fire destroys anyone seeking to harm them. They can prevent rain, turn water into blood and strike the earth with plagues. The description recalls powers associated with Elijah and Moses. It shows that resistance cannot silence their testimony before its appointed work is done.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The temple receives different readings. Wesley expects a temple in Jerusalem connected with Ezekiel’s vision. Gill understands it as the church being ordered according to Scripture. The USCCB notes understand the temple and worshipers as pictures of God’s faithful people. These explanations differ over whether the scene concerns a building or uses temple imagery to describe a community.

The witnesses are likewise understood as either two individuals or a larger body of witnesses. Wesley expects two prophets but rejects certainty that they are Moses and Elijah. Gill understands a continuing succession of faithful churches and ministers. The USCCB notes favor the church bearing witness, especially through its martyrs.

Walvoord favors a future temple and two individual prophets whose names are not disclosed. He also acknowledges disagreement over which half of a future seven-year period their ministry occupies, favoring the latter half. The passage’s stated periods should be distinguished from the larger timelines teachers build around them.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Zechariah 4:1–14 supplies more than a visual resemblance. Its message to Zerubbabel connects the olive trees and lampstand with work accomplished by God’s Spirit. Revelation draws on that picture as it presents witnesses equipped to speak in the face of opposition. The connection highlights God’s provision for His work, without settling every question about the witnesses’ identity.

The powers given to them also recall Elijah’s announcement of drought in 1 Kings 17:1 and the water turned to blood in Exodus 7:19–21. These are scriptural parallels with earlier confrontations between God’s messengers and resistant rulers. The resemblance places the witnesses in that larger story of God making His authority known; it does not by itself prove that Moses and Elijah have returned.
