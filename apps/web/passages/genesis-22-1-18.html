Passage

Genesis 22:1-18

Abraham Tested on Mount Moriah

Berean Standard Bible

1 Some time later God tested Abraham and said to him, “Abraham!”

“Here I am,” he answered.

2 “Take your son,” God said, “your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah. Offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains, which I will show you.”

3 So Abraham got up early the next morning, saddled his donkey, and took along two of his servants and his son Isaac. He split the wood for a burnt offering and set out for the place God had designated.

4 On the third day Abraham looked up and saw the place in the distance. 5 “Stay here with the donkey,” Abraham told his servants. “The boy and I will go over there to worship, and then we will return to you.”

6 Abraham took the wood for the burnt offering and placed it on his son Isaac. He himself carried the fire and the sacrificial knife, and the two of them walked on together.

7 Then Isaac said to his father Abraham, “My father!”

“Here I am, my son,” he replied.

“The fire and the wood are here,” said Isaac, “but where is the lamb for the burnt offering?”

8 Abraham answered, “God Himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering, my son.” And the two walked on together.

9 When they arrived at the place God had designated, Abraham built the altar there and arranged the wood. He bound his son Isaac and placed him on the altar, atop the wood. 10 Then Abraham reached out his hand and took the knife to slaughter his son.

11 Just then the angel of the LORD called out to him from heaven, “Abraham, Abraham!”

“Here I am,” he replied.

12 “Do not lay a hand on the boy or do anything to him,” said the angel, “for now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your only son from me.”

13 Then Abraham looked up and saw behind him a ram in a thicket, caught by its horns. So he went and took the ram and offered it as a burnt offering in place of his son. 14 And Abraham called that place The LORD Will Provide. So to this day it is said, “On the mountain of the LORD it will be provided.”

15 And the angel of the LORD called to Abraham from heaven a second time, 16 saying, “By Myself I have sworn, declares the LORD, that because you have done this and have not withheld your only son, 17 I will surely bless you, and I will multiply your descendants like the stars in the sky and the sand on the seashore. Your descendants will possess the gates of their enemies. 18 And through your offspring all nations of the earth will be blessed, because you have obeyed My voice.”

What is the context?

God tests Abraham by telling him to offer Isaac, the son through whom He has promised Abraham's descendants. The opening identifies a test; the ending reveals God's provision. Reading the whole movement matters, especially when considering the fearful command at its center.

Abraham prepares for the journey, leaves the servants behind, and goes on with Isaac, who carries the wood. He tells the servants they will return and tells Isaac that God will provide the animal. At the altar he binds his son and reaches for the knife. Genesis does not give Isaac's age or describe his thoughts, so claims about his exact age or his fully informed agreement go beyond the account.

The angel of the LORD calls from heaven and stops the sacrifice. Abraham must not harm the boy. A ram caught in a thicket becomes the offering in Isaac's place, and Abraham names the place for the LORD's provision. The LORD then confirms His promise with an oath: Abraham's descendants will multiply, and blessing will reach all nations through his offspring.

The outcome is a living son and a provided sacrifice. Elsewhere Scripture explicitly condemns offering children to God in Deuteronomy 12:31. This extraordinary test is therefore not permission to harm a child or to treat a private impression as such a command.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand this as a test of Abraham's trust in God, with obedience making that trust visible. The Catholic Catechism, paragraph 2572, and Wesley's notes on Genesis 22 both connect the test with Abraham's confidence that God could keep His promise even in the face of death.

Christians differ over the angel's identity. Wesley, on Genesis 22:11, 15, identifies Him as the eternal Son; Augustine, On the Trinity 3.11.25, understands God to be speaking through an angelic messenger. These are readings offered by Christian teachers, rather than a rule that every member of their respective traditions must interpret this scene in the same way.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament returns directly to this event. Hebrews 11:17–19 says Abraham believed God could raise Isaac from the dead. James 2:21–23 emphasizes his faith working through his actions and reaching its fulfillment in obedience. The test puts the trust described earlier in Abraham's life into action.

A further parallel helps show the scale of God's love. Abraham is willing to surrender his beloved son, but God stops him. In Romans 8:32, God gives His own Son for us. The Catechism, paragraph 2572, explicitly draws this comparison. Isaac is rescued by an animal dying in his place; Christ actually dies and is raised. The similarities point us toward Christ without making the two events identical.

The oath also reaches beyond Abraham's household. Hebrews 6:13–20 cites God's swearing by Himself to explain why the heirs of the promise can have secure hope. Its conclusion brings that hope to Jesus, who has entered God's presence on our behalf. The foundation is God's unchanging purpose and truthfulness.

What is our position?

Our study Worship as the Early Church Knew It uses Genesis 22:5 to explain worship as humble trust expressed in obedience. Abraham calls the journey worship before he knows how the crisis will be resolved. The study draws attention to the surrender involved, rather than reducing worship to music or a meeting.

Our study Who is The Angel of the Lord? understands the angel here as the Son of God before His human birth. It points to His speaking as the One from whom Abraham has not withheld his son, followed by the LORD's oath in the first person. This is the study's conclusion about the speaker's identity.
