Passage

Exodus 22:1-15

What happens when someone steals or damages another person’s property?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “If a man steals an ox or a sheep and slaughters or sells it, he must repay five oxen for an ox and four sheep for a sheep. 2 If a thief is caught breaking in and is beaten to death, no one shall be guilty of bloodshed. 3 But if it happens after sunrise, there is guilt for his bloodshed. A thief must make full restitution; if he has nothing, he himself shall be sold for his theft. 4 If what was stolen is actually found alive in his possession—whether ox or donkey or sheep—he must pay back double.

5 If a man grazes his livestock in a field or vineyard and allows them to stray so that they graze in someone else’s field, he must make restitution from the best of his own field or vineyard. 6 If a fire breaks out and spreads to thornbushes so that it consumes stacked or standing grain, or the whole field, the one who started the fire must make full restitution.

7 If a man gives his neighbor money or goods for safekeeping and they are stolen from the neighbor’s house, the thief, if caught, must pay back double. 8 If the thief is not found, the owner of the house must appear before the judges to determine whether he has taken his neighbor’s property. 9 In all cases of illegal possession of an ox, a donkey, a sheep, a garment, or any lost item that someone claims, ‘This is mine,’ both parties shall bring their cases before the judges. The one whom the judges find guilty must pay back double to his neighbor.

10 If a man gives a donkey, an ox, a sheep, or any other animal to be cared for by his neighbor, but it dies or is injured or stolen while no one is watching, 11 an oath before the LORD shall be made between the parties to determine whether or not the man has taken his neighbor’s property. The owner must accept the oath and require no restitution. 12 But if the animal was actually stolen from the neighbor, he must make restitution to the owner. 13 If the animal was torn to pieces, he shall bring it as evidence; he need not make restitution for the torn carcass.

14 If a man borrows an animal from his neighbor and it is injured or dies while its owner is not present, he must make full restitution. 15 If the owner was present, no restitution is required. If the animal was rented, the fee covers the loss.

What is the context?

These laws continue the question of responsibility raised by the dangerous animals and uncovered pits in the previous chapter. Here the cases involve theft, damage to crops, safekeeping and borrowed animals. The person who suffers the loss is not simply left to absorb it.

A thief who has sold or slaughtered a stolen animal owes more than one who still has it alive. The law requires repayment, and a thief unable to pay can be sold for the theft. It also distinguishes a break-in from killing a thief after sunrise. Theft itself does not give the homeowner an unrestricted right to take the thief’s life.

The cases then move from taking property to harming it or failing a trust. Someone whose livestock stray into another field must repay from the best of his own produce. Someone who starts a fire is answerable when it spreads. Disputed ownership goes before judges; an oath before the LORD and evidence of an animal’s death can also resolve a case. Being entrusted with an animal, borrowing it and hiring it do not all carry identical terms. The details matter because responsibility depends on what happened and what obligation the person had taken on.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The usual reading is that these laws require honest repayment and careful treatment of what belongs to someone else. Wesley emphasizes that carelessness can cause a real wrong even when the damage was not intended. He also sees the borrowing rule as a reason to take particular care with something another person has kindly lent. The passage distinguishes circumstances rather than assigning the same penalty to every loss.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus says that love for God and love for one’s neighbor are the two commands on which the Law and the Prophets hang (Matthew 22:34–40). These cases give that love a practical shape: another person’s livelihood and trust must matter to us. This is a connection with the law’s larger purpose, rather than Jesus directly commenting on each repayment amount.

The New Testament carries the theme into changed lives. In Luke 19:1–10, Zacchaeus responds to Jesus by promising generosity to the poor and repayment to people he has cheated. Jesus announces salvation and explains His mission to seek and save the lost. Ephesians 4:25–32 likewise calls the former thief to work and share with someone in need. God’s purpose reaches beyond stopping the theft: the person who once took from others can become someone who provides for them.
