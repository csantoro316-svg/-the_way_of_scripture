Passage

Nehemiah 13:30-31

What does Nehemiah leave in place, and what does he leave with God?

Berean Standard Bible

30 Thus I purified the priests and Levites from everything foreign, and I assigned specific duties to each of the priests and Levites. 31 I also arranged for contributions of wood at the appointed times, and for the firstfruits. Remember me, O my God, with favor.

What is the context?

The book ends with a brief summary: Nehemiah purifies the priests and Levites from what is foreign, assigns their duties and arranges the wood contributions and firstfruits.

His final words are a prayer asking God to remember him with favor. After walls, registers, promises, celebrations and renewed failures, he ends by placing his work before God.

The restored arrangements matter, but the closing chapter has shown that a past promise or celebration does not make continued faithfulness automatic. The book leaves the reader with both the responsibilities that must be carried forward and the appeal to God’s care.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry understands the closing request as humble dependence on God after imperfect service. Gill connects it with God graciously remembering the particular work Nehemiah has described. Both read the ending as an appeal to God’s favor rather than a declaration that every problem has been permanently solved.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The need for God’s continuing work within His people is expressed in the prayer of Hebrews 13:20–21. It asks the God who raised Jesus, the great Shepherd, to equip His people to do His will and work in them what pleases Him through Christ.

This is the larger theme to which Nehemiah’s unfinished struggle points: faithful service matters, and God’s people continue to need His help. The New Testament grounds that hope in the risen Shepherd and God’s work through Him.
