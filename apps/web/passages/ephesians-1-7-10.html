Passage

Ephesians 1:7-10

How does redemption belong to God’s larger purpose in Christ?

Berean Standard Bible

7 In Him we have redemption through His blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of His grace 8 that He lavished on us with all wisdom and understanding.

9 And He has made known to us the mystery of His will according to His good pleasure, which He purposed in Christ 10 as a plan for the fullness of time, to bring all things in heaven and on earth together in Christ.

What is the context?

Paul continues the blessing that began in verse 3. After praising God for adoption through Jesus Christ, he names redemption through Christ’s blood and the forgiveness of our trespasses.

The Greek word for redemption is ἀπολύτρωσις, apolytrōsis (ah-poh-LOO-troh-sis). Thayer’s lexicon explains it as release obtained through a ransom payment and connects its use here with deliverance through Christ’s death. Paul’s words join the cost of that release with the riches of God’s grace.

God’s generosity also includes making His purpose known. The mystery in verse 9 is a purpose God has revealed in Christ. Thayer’s entry for mystery identifies this New Testament use with God’s saving purpose, once hidden and now made known.

Verse 10 states the reach of that purpose: all things in heaven and on earth brought together in Christ at the fullness of time. Forgiveness belongs within this larger Christ-centered purpose. The following verses continue with the place of God’s people and the promised Spirit.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand these verses as connecting forgiveness through Christ’s death with God’s revealed purpose to bring all things together in Him. Wesley’s notes on verses 7–10 and the USCCB notes on verses 3–14 and 9 both connect the gifts of grace with that larger purpose centered on Christ.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Colossians 1:15–20 describes all things as created through the Son and for Him. It then connects reconciliation of things in heaven and on earth with the peace made through the blood of His cross. The same Son through whom creation exists is central to its reconciliation.

Romans 8:18–23 widens the hope beyond an individual’s forgiveness: creation itself waits for freedom from decay, alongside God’s children waiting for the redemption of their bodies.

Revelation 21:1–5 describes God dwelling with His people in a new heaven and new earth, with death and suffering removed. Revelation 22:1–5 adds the river and tree of life and the end of the curse. Read alongside the garden account in Genesis 3, these are strong connections between humanity’s broken beginning and the life God promises with Himself.

These passages give substance to the broad purpose named here. Christ’s redemption reaches toward a people and a creation freed to share life in God’s presence. That hope is grounded in the Son’s work, while each passage contributes its own part of the picture.
