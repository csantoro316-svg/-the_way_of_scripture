Passage

1 Samuel 13:16-23

How serious is Israel’s lack of weapons?

Berean Standard Bible

16 Now Saul and Jonathan his son and the troops with them were staying in Geba of Benjamin, while the Philistines camped at Michmash. 17 And raiders went out of the Philistine camp in three divisions. One headed toward Ophrah in the land of Shual, 18 another toward Beth-horon, and the third down the border road overlooking the Valley of Zeboim facing the wilderness.

19 And no blacksmith could be found in all the land of Israel, because the Philistines had said, “The Hebrews must not be allowed to make swords or spears.” 20 Instead, all the Israelites would go down to the Philistines to sharpen their plowshares, mattocks, axes, and sickles. 21 The charge was a pim for sharpening a plowshare or mattock, a third of a shekel for sharpening a pitchfork or an axe, and a third of a shekel for repointing an oxgoad. 22 So on the day of battle not a sword or spear could be found in the hands of the troops with Saul and Jonathan; only Saul and his son Jonathan had weapons. 23 And a garrison of the Philistines had gone out to the pass at Michmash.

What is the context?

Saul and Jonathan remain with their troops at Geba, while the Philistines hold Michmash. Raiding parties leave the Philistine camp in three directions, extending pressure across the surrounding territory. The chapter ends with a Philistine garrison taking the pass at Michmash.

The narrator pauses to explain a serious disadvantage. No blacksmith is available in Israel because the Philistines seek to prevent the Hebrews from making swords and spears. Even sharpening agricultural tools requires going to the Philistines, and the account lists the charges.

When battle comes, only Saul and Jonathan among the troops with them have the weapons described. The statement concerns this force and its swords and spears; it does not require imagining that no Israelite anywhere has a sling, bow or other means of fighting. The situation prepares for Jonathan’s action in the next chapter.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes dependence on an enemy even for farming tools. Wesley and Gill both take the weapon shortage seriously while considering how it fits earlier battles. Wesley allows for other kinds of weapons and confines the immediate description to the men with Saul and Jonathan. Gill discusses possible explanations but does not remove the stated disadvantage.

The USCCB study notes see the details as emphasizing the Philistines’ military strength and Israel’s dependence on God for victory. That is consistent with Jonathan’s next words about the LORD being able to save through many or few (1 Samuel 14:1–6). The narrative supplies this contrast without requiring an independently established history of every ancient metalworking technique.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The military imbalance leads directly into Jonathan’s confidence that God is not limited by numbers. The following account ultimately credits the LORD with saving Israel (1 Samuel 14:6, 23). Human resources do not set the limit of His ability to rescue.

The same larger theme of unexpected divine power appears in 1 Corinthians 1:18–25. Paul identifies Christ crucified as God’s power and wisdom, despite the weakness and foolishness people imagine they see. This is a comparison of themes, not a claim that a missing sword predicts a particular event at the cross. Christian confidence rests in God’s saving work rather than the outward impressiveness of the means.
