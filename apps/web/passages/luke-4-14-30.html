Passage

Luke 4:14-30

What does Jesus announce in His hometown?

Berean Standard Bible

14 Jesus returned to Galilee in the power of the Spirit, and the news about Him spread throughout the surrounding region. 15 He taught in their synagogues and was glorified by everyone. 16 Then Jesus came to Nazareth, where He had been brought up. As was His custom, He entered the synagogue on the Sabbath. And when He stood up to read, 17 the scroll of the prophet Isaiah was handed to Him. Unrolling it, He found the place where it was written: 18 “The Spirit of the Lord is on Me, because He has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor. He has sent Me to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovery of sight to the blind, to release the oppressed, 19 to proclaim the year of the Lord’s favor.” 20 Then He rolled up the scroll, returned it to the attendant, and sat down. The eyes of everyone in the synagogue were fixed on Him, 21 and He began by saying, “Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing.”

22 All spoke well of Him and marveled at the gracious words that came from His lips. “Isn’t this the son of Joseph?” they asked. 23 Jesus said to them, “Surely you will quote this proverb to Me: ‘Physician, heal yourself! Do here in Your hometown what we have heard that You did in Capernaum.’” 24 Then He added, “Truly I tell you, no prophet is accepted in his hometown. 25 But I tell you truthfully that there were many widows in Israel in the time of Elijah, when the sky was shut for three and a half years and great famine swept over all the land. 26 Yet Elijah was not sent to any of them, but to the widow of Zarephath in Sidon. 27 And there were many lepers in Israel in the time of Elisha the prophet. Yet not one of them was cleansed—only Naaman the Syrian.”

28 On hearing this, all the people in the synagogue were enraged. 29 They got up, drove Him out of the town, and led Him to the brow of the hill on which the town was built, in order to throw Him over the cliff. 30 But Jesus passed through the crowd and went on His way.

What is the context?

Jesus returns to Galilee in the Spirit’s power and teaches in the synagogues. At Nazareth, where He grew up, He follows His custom of attending on the Sabbath. He stands to read from Isaiah, then sits and declares that the Scripture is being fulfilled in their hearing.

The setting is a local Jewish gathering for reading and teaching Scripture. An ancient synagogue inscription from Jerusalem likewise names reading the Law and teaching the commandments among its purposes. Luke shows those practices in action without giving a complete order of service. (Jerusalem synagogue inscription)

The reading announces the Spirit’s anointing for good news to the poor, release for captives, sight for the blind, freedom for the oppressed, and the Lord’s favor. Its language comes from Isaiah 61 and also recalls Isaiah 58’s call to set the oppressed free. Jesus identifies His own ministry with this promised work. (Isaiah 61:1–3; Isaiah 58:6)

The word for release is ἄφεσις, aphesis (AH-feh-sis). It can describe freedom from captivity or the forgiveness of sins; Luke also uses it in John’s preaching about forgiveness. Both uses help us hear the saving mercy in Jesus’ announcement. (Thayer’s lexicon; Luke 3:3)

The listeners initially marvel at His gracious words but question Him as Joseph’s son. Jesus addresses their expectation that the reports from Capernaum should be repeated at home. He then recalls Elijah helping a widow in Sidon and Elisha’s part in Naaman the Syrian’s healing. God’s mercy in these accounts reaches beyond Israel’s borders and is not something His hometown can claim as its own privilege.

Their response turns to rage, and they try to throw Him from the hill. Jesus passes through the crowd and goes on His way. Luke does not explain how He does so. The announcement of grace is followed immediately by opposition.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian traditions commonly read this as Jesus announcing the fulfillment of God’s promised saving work, with good news for the poor and mercy reaching beyond hometown privilege. Wesley explicitly includes both material and spiritual need in the good news to the poor, while the Catholic Catechism likewise connects His kingdom with the lowly, active care for the poor, and mercy for sinners. (Wesley on Luke 4:18–28; Catechism 544–545)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

There is a supported connection with the Jubilee instructions in Leviticus. That year proclaimed liberty and restored Israelite family land, with release for Israelites serving because of poverty. Jesus’ announcement of the Lord’s favor belongs to this scriptural picture of God bringing freedom and restoration. John’s Gospel also records Jesus explaining the freedom He gives from slavery to sin. (Leviticus 25:8–17, 39–43; John 8:31–36)

Jesus Himself connects His ministry with the earlier prophets’ work among foreigners. The widow’s story records both provision and the restoration of her son’s life; Naaman’s story includes initial resistance followed by obedience and healing. Their accounts show God’s mercy reaching people outside Israel without portraying those people as having no struggles or questions. (1 Kings 17:8–24; 2 Kings 5:1–19)

That reach continues in the mission after Jesus’ resurrection. When Peter speaks to Cornelius’s household, he recognizes that God does not show favoritism and proclaims Jesus’ Spirit-anointed ministry, death, resurrection, and forgiveness for everyone who believes. The hometown scene thus fits a saving purpose that reaches the nations while remaining rooted in God’s promises to Israel. (Acts 10:34–43)
