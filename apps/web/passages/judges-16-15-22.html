Passage

Judges 16:15-22

What has Samson forgotten about the source of his strength?

Berean Standard Bible

15 “How can you say, ‘I love you,’” she asked, “when your heart is not with me? This is the third time you have mocked me and failed to reveal to me the source of your great strength!” 16 Finally, after she had pressed him daily with her words and pleaded until he was sick to death, 17 Samson told her all that was in his heart: “My hair has never been cut, because I have been a Nazirite to God from my mother’s womb. If I am shaved, my strength will leave me, and I will become as weak as any other man.”

18 When Delilah realized that he had revealed to her all that was in his heart, she sent this message to the lords of the Philistines: “Come up once more, for he has revealed to me all that is in his heart.” Then the lords of the Philistines came to her, bringing the money in their hands. 19 And having lulled him to sleep on her lap, she called a man to shave off the seven braids of his head. In this way she began to subdue him, and his strength left him. 20 Then she called out, “Samson, the Philistines are here!” When Samson awoke from his sleep, he thought, “I will escape as I did before and shake myself free.” But he did not know that the LORD had departed from him.

21 Then the Philistines seized him, gouged out his eyes, and brought him down to Gaza, where he was bound with bronze shackles and forced to grind grain in the prison. 22 However, the hair of his head began to grow back after it had been shaved.

What is the context?

Delilah presses Samson day after day, questioning his love until he tells her what is in his heart. He connects his uncut hair with being a Nazirite to God from his mother’s womb. נָזִיר, nazir (nah-ZEER), means someone consecrated or devoted. Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically identifies Samson’s dedication from birth, in contrast with the usual voluntary vow for a limited period.

After he falls asleep on her lap, she calls a man to shave off his seven braids. His strength leaves him. Samson expects to escape as before, but the narrator explains what he does not know: the LORD has departed from him. The hair belongs to his consecration; the power is God’s.

The Philistines seize him, gouge out his eyes, take him to Gaza, bind him in bronze shackles, and make him grind grain in prison. Then comes a brief detail that leaves room for what follows: his hair begins growing again. The account has not ended with his capture.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the hair as a condition attached to Samson’s calling, rather than a substance that produces strength on its own. Henry likewise treats the loss of strength as the consequence of abandoning his dedication to God. Both read his confidence that he can escape again as a warning about not recognizing what has been lost.

They also read the returning hair as a sign of repentance and renewed mercy. The narrator reports the growth without recording a prison confession; the next scene supplies Samson’s prayer. Their reading connects this detail with his later dependence on God, rather than treating hair growth as an automatic way to restore divine power.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament includes Samson among people commended for faith in Hebrews 11:32–40. His failures are serious, yet they are not the whole account of his relationship with God. Hebrews names him without telling readers to imitate each choice he made.

The next paragraph directs the reader’s attention to Jesus: Hebrews 12:1–3 calls us to lay aside entangling sin, endure, and fix our eyes on the One who begins and completes our faith. The larger story gives both warning and hope, with Jesus as the person to follow.
