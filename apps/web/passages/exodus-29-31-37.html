Passage

Exodus 29:31-37

Why did the priests eat a sacred meal and remain for seven days?

Berean Standard Bible

31 You are to take the ram of ordination and boil its flesh in a holy place. 32 At the entrance to the Tent of Meeting, Aaron and his sons are to eat the meat of the ram and the bread that is in the basket. 33 They must eat those things by which atonement was made for their ordination and consecration. But no outsider may eat them, because these things are sacred. 34 And if any of the meat of ordination or any bread is left until the morning, you are to burn up the remainder. It must not be eaten, because it is sacred.

35 This is what you are to do for Aaron and his sons based on all that I have commanded you, taking seven days to ordain them. 36 Sacrifice a bull as a sin offering each day for atonement. Purify the altar by making atonement for it, and anoint it to consecrate it. 37 For seven days you shall make atonement for the altar and consecrate it. Then the altar will become most holy; whatever touches the altar will be holy.

What is the context?

The remaining flesh of the ordination ram is boiled in a holy place. Aaron and his sons eat it with the bread at the entrance to the tent. These are sacred portions connected with their ordination; someone outside that appointed group may not eat them. Anything left until morning is to be burned.

The setting apart of the priests takes seven days, with a bull offered for atonement each day and the altar purified and anointed. The altar becomes most holy. The ceremony concerns both those who serve and the place where they will offer Israel’s sacrifices.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the meal as the priests receiving the benefit of the atonement and enjoying fellowship with God. He sees the seven-day period as giving weight and seriousness to their admission to service. He also connects the repeated sacrifices with the need for the better priesthood and offering of Christ, as explained in Hebrews.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The provision of food for those serving at the sanctuary becomes an explicit comparison in 1 Corinthians 9:7–18. Paul recalls that those serving at the altar receive food from its offerings and applies that pattern to support for those who preach the gospel.

Paul also explains why he sometimes chooses not to exercise that right: he wants to remove obstacles to the gospel. The connection brings provision and service together. God’s people can support the work, while the person serving keeps the good of others and the advance of the gospel at the center.
