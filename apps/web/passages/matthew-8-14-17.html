Passage

Matthew 8:14-17

Healing and the Promise of Isaiah

Berean Standard Bible

14 When Jesus arrived at Peter’s house, He saw Peter’s mother-in-law sick in bed with a fever. 15 So He touched her hand, and the fever left her, and she got up and began to serve Him.

16 When evening came, many who were demon-possessed were brought to Jesus, and He drove out the spirits with a word and healed all the sick. 17 This was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah: “He took up our infirmities and carried our diseases.”

What is the context?

Jesus' healing ministry reaches into Peter's home. His mother-in-law is lying sick with a fever. Jesus touches her hand, the fever leaves, and she rises to serve Him. Matthew moves from this personal encounter to an evening when many sick and demon-possessed people are brought to Him. He heals the sick and drives out the spirits with a word.

Mark 1:29–34 and Luke 4:38–41 describe the household's appeal for her and her immediate return to service. Matthew's brief account keeps the attention on Jesus seeing her need and acting. The woman is restored to active life; the text does not identify the cause of her fever.

Matthew then explains these healings by quoting Isaiah 53:4. This is his own stated connection, rather than a resemblance added by a later reader. The servant described by Isaiah bears the suffering of others, and Matthew sees Jesus' care for sick people as fulfilling that promise.

The Hebrew noun חֳלִי, choli (khoh-LEE), means sickness or disease. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes its use in Isaiah 53:3–4. This helps explain why Matthew applies Isaiah's language to actual bodily suffering. The wider chapter also speaks of sin, forgiveness, and the servant's death; its concern reaches beyond one kind of suffering.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand these healings as signs of Jesus' compassion and His fulfillment of Isaiah's servant passage. Readers place different emphasis on how the healings relate to His saving death and to healing today.

Wesley on Matthew 8:17 sees these bodily healings as one way Isaiah's words come true, while placing the main emphasis on Christ bearing our sins. The Catholic Catechism, 1505, describes the healings as signs announcing the greater victory over sin and death through Christ's death and resurrection.

Among Pentecostal churches, the Assemblies of God explicitly connects bodily healing with Christ's saving sacrifice in its Statement of Fundamental Truths, 12. Its Divine Healing position paper also says that some people are not healed now and that the full restoration of our bodies awaits Christ's return. This should not be confused with a promise that every faithful person will immediately recover.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Matthew's quotation places Jesus' healing work within the mission of Isaiah's suffering servant. Isaiah 53:4–12 moves from bearing suffering to bearing sins and giving His life. 1 Peter 2:21–25 explicitly applies that servant language to Jesus' suffering on the cross, His bearing of sins, and the return of wandering people to their Shepherd. The healings and the cross belong within the mission of the same Savior.

That rescue includes a future for the body as well. Romans 8:18–25 describes believers still suffering and waiting for the redemption of their bodies. 1 Corinthians 15:42–55 describes the dead raised beyond decay and death. The hope is larger than repeated temporary relief: Christ's saving work leads toward life in which death itself is defeated.
