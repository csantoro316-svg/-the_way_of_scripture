Passage

Judges 6:25-32

Why does Gideon’s first task begin at home?

Berean Standard Bible

25 On that very night the LORD said to Gideon, “Take your father’s young bull and a second bull seven years old, tear down your father’s altar to Baal, and cut down the Asherah pole beside it. 26 Then build a proper altar to the LORD your God on the top of this stronghold. And with the wood of the Asherah pole you cut down, take the second bull and offer it as a burnt offering.” 27 So Gideon took ten of his servants and did as the LORD had told him. But because he was too afraid of his father’s household and the men of the city, he did it by night rather than in the daytime.

28 When the men of the city got up in the morning, there was Baal’s altar torn down, with the Asherah pole cut down beside it and the second bull offered up on the newly built altar. 29 “Who did this?” they said to one another. And after they had investigated thoroughly, they were told, “Gideon son of Joash did it.” 30 Then the men of the city said to Joash, “Bring out your son. He must die, because he has torn down Baal’s altar and cut down the Asherah pole beside it.” 31 But Joash said to all who stood against him, “Are you contending for Baal? Are you trying to save him? Whoever pleads his case will be put to death by morning! If Baal is a god, let him contend for himself with the one who has torn down his altar.” 32 So on that day Gideon was called Jerubbaal, that is to say, “Let Baal contend with him,” because he had torn down Baal’s altar.

What is the context?

On the same night, the LORD gives Gideon a task within his own community. He must tear down his father’s altar to Baal, cut down the nearby Asherah pole, and build an altar to the LORD. The wood from the pole is to be used for the burnt offering. The coming rescue begins by confronting the worship that helped bring Israel into its present condition.

Gideon takes ten servants and obeys, but does the work at night because he fears his father’s household and the townspeople. Morning reveals how strongly the town is attached to Baal: its men demand Gideon’s death for destroying the altar and pole.

Joash answers by challenging them to let Baal defend himself if he really is a god. Gideon receives the name Jerubbaal, which the passage explains as letting Baal contend with him. The scene exposes the contrast between an idol its worshipers must defend and the LORD who calls Gideon to rescue His people.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry sees grace at work in God raising a reformer from a household involved in idolatry. The rescue requires a return to the LORD, beginning with the worship in Gideon’s own home.

Wesley and Gill give a practical reading of the nighttime action: Gideon fears being prevented from carrying out the command. Gill therefore treats the timing as prudent obedience. The verse explicitly names fear and also says Gideon did what the LORD commanded; these commentators’ explanation differs from readings that treat the nighttime action simply as a failure to obey.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Turning from idols to the living God becomes part of the New Testament’s account of receiving the gospel. First Thessalonians 1:8–10 describes people turning to God from idols, serving Him, and awaiting His risen Son, Jesus, who delivers from the coming wrath.

Titus 2:11–14 connects Christ’s rescue with a changed life: He gives Himself to redeem and purify a people eager to do good. God’s purpose includes both deliverance and renewed loyalty. Gideon’s first task helps us see why rescue and the question of whom people worship belong together.
