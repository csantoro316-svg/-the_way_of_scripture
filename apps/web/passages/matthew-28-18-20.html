Passage

Matthew 28:18-20

All Authority, All Nations, and His Continuing Presence

Berean Standard Bible

18 Then Jesus came to them and said, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me. 19 Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, 20 and teaching them to obey all that I have commanded you. And surely I am with you always, even to the end of the age.”

What is the context?

The risen Jesus speaks to His disciples in Galilee. The full scene in Matthew 28:16–20 begins with the eleven coming to the mountain He designated, worshiping Him, and some doubting. His commission rests on His authority and ends with His promise to remain with them.

Making disciples means helping people become followers of Jesus. The verb is μαθητεύω, mathēteuō (mah-thay-TYOO-oh). Thayer's entry explains its use here as making a disciple, teaching, or instructing. Baptizing and teaching obedience belong within that work. The passage joins new allegiance to Jesus with continued learning and living by His commands.

Jesus names the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit together in baptism. The reach of the mission is all nations. This expands the disciples' earlier, limited mission to Israel in Matthew 10:5–6.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Catholic, Reformed, Baptist, Wesleyan, and Lutheran readings share the command to make disciples, baptize in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and teach obedience to Jesus. They differ over who should receive baptism, what God gives through it, and how it should be administered.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 28.1–6, includes believers and their children, calls baptism a sign and seal of God's promise of grace, and permits pouring or sprinkling. It does not teach that every baptized person is necessarily born again or that God's grace is confined to the moment of baptism. The 1689 Baptist Confession, 29.1–4, instead limits baptism to people who profess repentance and faith, and requires immersion. It describes baptism as a sign of fellowship with Christ in His death and resurrection and a commitment to new life.

The Catholic Catechism, 1247–1255, describes preparation in faith for adults and baptism of infants, who are to grow in the faith of the Church with help from parents and godparents. It understands baptism as the beginning of new life in Christ. The Lutheran Augsburg Confession, Article IX, likewise affirms infant baptism and emphasizes that God offers His saving grace through baptism.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, Article XVII, call baptism both a mark of Christian profession and a sign of new birth, and retain the baptism of young children. Wesley's note on 28:19 explains that adults are taught before baptism, while children are baptized and then taught. These documents identify particular traditions' teachings; individual congregations and members do not all explain every detail in the same way.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The mission to all nations belongs to a promise running through Scripture. God tells Abraham that the families of the earth will be blessed through him (Genesis 12:1–3); Galatians 3:6–9 explicitly connects that promise with the good news reaching the Gentiles through faith. The commission carries that worldwide purpose forward.

Jesus' authority also recalls Daniel 7:13–14, where one like the Son of Man receives enduring rule and peoples of every nation serve Him. This is a scriptural parallel, not a quotation introduced by Matthew. The Gospel's closing promise of His presence also recalls its opening description of Immanuel, God with us (Matthew 1:22–23). The disciples are sent under His authority and with His presence, rather than left to carry the mission alone.

What is our position?

I Am a Disciple and The True Church According to Jesus understand disciple-making as helping people become lifelong learners and followers of Jesus. It includes knowing His teaching, practicing it, loving others, and bearing witness in ordinary life. The mission belongs to Christ's people across generations and reaches beyond a single event or a program run by a few leaders.

Baptism supplies our position on that part of the commission: baptism is commanded and is a meaningful response of faith in Christ, while salvation is received by grace through faith. The study prefers immersion because it pictures burial and new life with Him, while recognizing that Scripture does not explicitly declare every other mode invalid. These points come from those existing studies.
