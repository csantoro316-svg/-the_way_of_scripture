Passage

Genesis 9:1-17

The Covenant of the Rainbow

Berean Standard Bible

1 And God blessed Noah and his sons and said to them, “Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth. 2 The fear and dread of you will fall on every living creature on the earth, every bird of the air, every creature that crawls on the ground, and all the fish of the sea. They are delivered into your hand. 3 Everything that lives and moves will be food for you; just as I gave you the green plants, I now give you all things. 4 But you must not eat meat with its lifeblood still in it. 5 And surely I will require the life of any man or beast by whose hand your lifeblood is shed. I will demand an accounting from anyone who takes the life of his fellow man:

6 Whoever sheds the blood of man,

by man his blood will be shed;

for in His own image

God has made mankind.

7 But as for you,

be fruitful and multiply;

spread out across the earth

and multiply upon it.”

8 Then God said to Noah and his sons with him, 9 “Behold, I now establish My covenant with you and your descendants after you, 10 and with every living creature that was with you—the birds, the livestock, and every beast of the earth—every living thing that came out of the ark. 11 And I establish My covenant with you: Never again will all life be cut off by the waters of a flood; never again will there be a flood to destroy the earth.”

12 And God said, “This is the sign of the covenant I am making between Me and you and every living creature with you, a covenant for all generations to come: 13 I have set My rainbow in the clouds, and it will be a sign of the covenant between Me and the earth.

14 Whenever I form clouds over the earth and the rainbow appears in the clouds, 15 I will remember My covenant between Me and you and every living creature of every kind. Never again will the waters become a flood to destroy all life. 16 And whenever the rainbow appears in the clouds, I will see it and remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature of every kind that is on the earth.”

17 So God said to Noah, “This is the sign of the covenant that I have established between Me and every creature on the earth.”

What is the context?

God blesses the family He has preserved and repeats the call to fill the earth. He permits animals as food while forbidding meat with its lifeblood. He then requires an accounting for the taking of human life. These instructions hold provision and restraint together: receiving food does not make human beings owners of life without limits.

The reason given for the value of human life reaches back to creation: God made humanity in His image. The fall and flood have not erased that dignity. Verse 6 also gives human beings a role in answering bloodshed, which raises a question Christians continue to discuss about punishment and the protection of life.

God then makes His covenant with Noah's family, their descendants, and the living creatures. The Hebrew בְּרִית, bĕrît (beh-REET), means covenant; Brown–Driver–Briggs identifies this passage as a divine promise with a sign. Its content is stated clearly: God will not again destroy all life by the waters of a flood. The rainbow marks that promise. It is not a promise that no local flood will ever occur.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read the covenant as God's commitment to preserve the world and the commands as requiring respect for life. Where do traditions differ? On verse 6, Wesley's notes understand the punishment of murder as entrusted to human authorities. The Westminster Confession, 23.1, and 1689 Baptist Confession, 24.1, likewise teach that God gives civil rulers the power of the sword to punish wrongdoing; those statements do not prescribe the sentence for every modern case.

The Catholic Church's current Catechism, 2267, teaches that the death penalty is inadmissible and calls for its abolition. It appeals to the continuing dignity of the person and the possibility of protecting society without execution. The disagreement concerns how punishment should protect life; the value of human life remains central to the documents on both sides.

The rainbow's significance is God's promise, rather than a claim about when rainbows first existed. Wesley's notes on verse 13 suggest that an existing rainbow was given a new covenant meaning. Genesis states the sign and its purpose without explaining its first physical appearance.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Isaiah 54:9–10, God explicitly recalls the promise to Noah when assuring His people of enduring mercy and a covenant of peace. The earlier promise becomes an example of the trustworthiness of what He says later. God's purpose moves forward through commitments He keeps, not through human confidence that disaster is impossible.

2 Peter 3:5–13 also keeps the flood and the future together. God's patience gives room for repentance, while His promise looks toward a new heaven and earth where righteousness dwells. The Noah covenant preserves the setting in which His continuing work of rescue unfolds; it does not mean that He has ceased to judge evil.
