Passage

2 Samuel 2:18-32

When will the pursuit of fellow Israelites end?

Berean Standard Bible

18 The three sons of Zeruiah were there: Joab, Abishai, and Asahel. Now Asahel was fleet of foot like a wild gazelle, 19 and he chased Abner, not turning to the right or to the left in his pursuit. 20 And Abner glanced back and said, “Is that you, Asahel?” “It is,” Asahel replied. 21 So Abner told him, “Turn to your right or to your left, seize one of the young men, and take his equipment for yourself.” But Asahel would not stop chasing him. 22 Once again, Abner warned Asahel, “Stop chasing me. Why should I strike you to the ground? How could I show my face to your brother Joab?” 23 But Asahel refused to turn away. So Abner thrust the butt of his spear into his stomach, and it came out his back, and he fell dead on the spot. And every man paused when he came to the place where Asahel had fallen and died.

24 But Joab and Abishai pursued Abner. By sunset, they had gone as far as the hill of Ammah opposite Giah on the way to the wilderness of Gibeon. 25 The Benjamites rallied to Abner, formed a single unit, and took their stand atop a hill. 26 Then Abner called out to Joab: “Must the sword devour forever? Do you not realize that this will only end in bitterness? How long before you tell the troops to stop pursuing their brothers?” 27 “As surely as God lives,” Joab replied, “if you had not spoken up, the troops would have continued pursuing their brothers until morning.” 28 So Joab blew the ram’s horn, and all the troops stopped; they no longer pursued Israel or continued to fight. 29 And all that night Abner and his men marched through the Arabah. They crossed the Jordan, marched all morning, and arrived at Mahanaim.

30 When Joab returned from pursuing Abner, he gathered all the troops. In addition to Asahel, nineteen of David’s servants were missing, 31 but they had struck down 360 Benjamites who were with Abner. 32 And they took Asahel and buried him in his father’s tomb in Bethlehem. Then Joab and his men marched all night and reached Hebron at daybreak.

What is the context?

Joab’s brothers Abishai and Asahel are present in the battle. Asahel, noted for his speed, pursues Abner without turning aside. Abner twice warns him to choose another opponent, asking how he could face Joab if he killed his brother.

Asahel refuses to stop. Abner strikes him in the stomach with the butt of his spear, and the blow passes through him. Asahel dies, and those who reach the place pause. Joab and Abishai continue the pursuit until Abner’s Benjamite forces gather on a hill.

Abner then asks whether the sword must devour forever and warns that continued fighting will end in bitterness. Joab answers and sounds the ram’s horn. The pursuit stops, and Abner’s men travel through the night back to Mahanaim.

The cost is counted: nineteen of David’s men besides Asahel are missing, while 360 Benjamites from Abner’s company have fallen. Asahel is buried in his father’s tomb at Bethlehem, and Joab’s men reach Hebron at daybreak. The contest between royal houses has become grief within families.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley see a warning in Asahel’s pursuit: the very ability that makes him confident brings him into danger. The account stresses his speed and refusal to turn aside without giving a full history of his military experience.

Henry emphasizes the bitterness of fighting fellow Israelites. Abner’s appeal speaks a necessary truth even though he helped begin the day’s bloodshed. Recognizing that continued violence will cause harm is a reason to stop before the losses grow.

Wesley and Gill read Joab’s reply as referring back to Abner’s original challenge: without it, the battle need not have begun. The BSB wording instead presents him saying that, without Abner’s appeal to stop, the pursuit would have continued until morning. The readings give the reply a different emphasis, but both leave the halt to fighting clearly in view.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The chapter exposes how the pursuit of advantage can leave a people divided and grieving. Its stopping point helps us look beyond which side has more survivors to the kind of wisdom that can interrupt destruction.

James 3:13–18 contrasts jealousy and selfish ambition with wisdom from above, which is peace-loving, gentle, merciful and sincere. God’s purpose for His people includes a harvest of righteousness sown in peace.

This is a shared theme, rather than a claim that James directly interprets the battle at Gibeon. It keeps the larger story of David’s rise from turning every act of violence along the way into something to admire. Faithfulness includes learning when to stop pursuing one another.
