Passage

Ezekiel 30:20-26

Why is Pharaoh’s broken arm contrasted with Babylon’s strengthened arms?

Berean Standard Bible

20 In the eleventh year, on the seventh day of the first month, the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 21 “Son of man, I have broken the arm of Pharaoh king of Egypt. See, it has not been bound up for healing, or splinted for strength to hold the sword. 22 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: Behold, I am against Pharaoh king of Egypt. I will break his arms, both the strong one and the one already broken, and will make the sword fall from his hand. 23 I will disperse the Egyptians among the nations and scatter them throughout the lands.

24 I will strengthen the arms of Babylon’s king and place My sword in his hand, but I will break the arms of Pharaoh, who will groan before him like a mortally wounded man. 25 I will strengthen the arms of Babylon’s king, but Pharaoh’s arms will fall limp. Then they will know that I am the LORD, when I place My sword in the hand of Babylon’s king, and he wields it against the land of Egypt. 26 I will disperse the Egyptians among the nations and scatter them throughout the lands. Then they will know that I am the LORD.”

What is the context?

This dated message pictures Pharaoh with an already broken arm that has not been bound up to heal or strengthened to hold a sword. The image makes his inability to sustain the fight visible.

The Lord will break both arms, including the one still strong, so that the sword falls from Pharaoh’s hand. In contrast, He will strengthen Babylon’s king and place His sword in that king’s hand.

The movement of power is therefore part of the Lord’s announced action. Pharaoh’s groaning, Egypt’s defeat and the scattering of its people expose the limits of the power on which Israel had leaned.

The repeated promise is that they will know the Lord when these things happen. The final emphasis is not admiration for Babylon’s might, but recognition of the One who gives and removes strength.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the strong and broken arms as the remaining and already damaged parts of Egypt’s power. Cambridge considers the possibility that the already broken arm refers to Egypt’s failed attempt to relieve Jerusalem, while continuing to treat the arms as a figure for military strength.

Henry reads the contrast as Egypt growing weaker while Babylon grows stronger. The picture explains the rise and fall of these powers without requiring an otherwise unrecorded medical diagnosis of Pharaoh.
