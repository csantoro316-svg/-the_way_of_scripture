Passage

Ezekiel 10:9-17

Why does Ezekiel describe the living creatures again?

Berean Standard Bible

9 Then I looked and saw four wheels beside the cherubim, one wheel beside each cherub. And the wheels gleamed like a beryl stone. 10 As for their appearance, all four had the same form, like a wheel within a wheel. 11 When they moved, they would go in any of the four directions, without turning as they moved. For wherever the head faced, the cherubim would go in that direction, without turning as they moved. 12 Their entire bodies, including their backs, hands, and wings, were full of eyes all around, as were their four wheels. 13 I heard the wheels being called “the whirling wheels.” 14 Each of the cherubim had four faces: the first face was that of a cherub, the second that of a man, the third that of a lion, and the fourth that of an eagle.

15 Then the cherubim rose upward. These were the living creatures I had seen by the River Kebar. 16 When the cherubim moved, the wheels moved beside them, and even when they spread their wings to rise from the ground, the wheels did not veer away from their side. 17 When the cherubim stood still, the wheels also stood still, and when they ascended, the wheels ascended with them, for the spirit of the living creatures was in the wheels.

What is the context?

Ezekiel looks again at the wheels and the creatures beside them. Each creature has a wheel, and all four wheels share the same appearance, like a wheel within a wheel. They move in any of four directions without turning.

The creatures and wheels are covered with eyes. Ezekiel hears the wheels called whirling wheels and describes four faces on each creature. Here the first face is called the face of a cherub; chapter 1 described the face of an ox among the four.

The creatures rise, move and stand with the wheels. Their movement is joined because the spirit of the living creatures is in the wheels. Ezekiel explicitly recognizes them as the beings he saw beside the River Kebar.

The repetition ties the Jerusalem vision to his first encounter in exile. The Lord whose glory he saw beside the river is the same Lord now revealing what is happening at the temple.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read the joined movement as a picture of God directing events toward His purposes. Wesley connects the many eyes with wisdom: events that appear confusing to people remain under God’s direction.

The emphasis is coordinated movement and divine rule. The creatures do not act independently of one another, and the Lord’s glory is not confined to one earthly place. Ezekiel’s recognition of them makes the continuity between the visions explicit.
