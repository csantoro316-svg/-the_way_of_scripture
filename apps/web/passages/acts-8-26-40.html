Passage

Acts 8:26-40

How does Philip help the Ethiopian official understand Jesus?

Berean Standard Bible

26 Now an angel of the Lord said to Philip, “Get up and go south to the desert road that goes down from Jerusalem to Gaza.” 27 So he started out, and on his way he met an Ethiopian eunuch, a court official in charge of the entire treasury of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians. He had gone to Jerusalem to worship, 28 and on his return was sitting in his chariot reading Isaiah the prophet. 29 The Spirit said to Philip, “Go over to that chariot and stay by it.” 30 So Philip ran up and heard the man reading Isaiah the prophet. “Do you understand what you are reading?” Philip asked. 31 “How can I,” he said, “unless someone guides me?” And he invited Philip to come up and sit with him.

32 The eunuch was reading this passage of Scripture: “He was led like a sheep to the slaughter, and as a lamb before the shearer is silent, so He did not open His mouth. 33 In His humiliation He was deprived of justice. Who can recount His descendants? For His life was removed from the earth.” 34 “Tell me,” said the eunuch, “who is the prophet talking about, himself or someone else?” 35 Then Philip began with this very Scripture and told him the good news about Jesus.

36 As they traveled along the road and came to some water, the eunuch said, “Look, here is water! What is there to prevent me from being baptized?” 38 And he gave orders to stop the chariot. Then both Philip and the eunuch went down into the water, and Philip baptized him. 39 When they came up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord carried Philip away, and the eunuch saw him no more, but went on his way rejoicing. 40 But Philip appeared at Azotus and traveled through that region, preaching the gospel in all the towns until he came to Caesarea.

What is the context?

An angel directs Philip to the desert road from Jerusalem toward Gaza. There he meets an Ethiopian royal official returning from worship in Jerusalem and reading Isaiah. The Spirit directs Philip to join him, and a question about understanding the text becomes an invitation to sit alongside him.

Luke calls the man a eunuch and says he manages the queen’s treasury. Eunuchs were men unable to father children, often through castration, and could hold important court responsibilities; Thayer’s entry explains the connection. Candace is a form of the royal title kandake, associated with Nubia south of Egypt. The Met’s account of Nubia describes the title for a ruling queen mother in that region.

The official is reading about the suffering servant and asks whether Isaiah is speaking about himself or someone else. Philip begins with that Scripture and tells him the good news about Jesus. Luke gives the starting point and the subject, rather than a full transcript of Philip’s explanation.

When they come to water, the official asks about baptism. Philip baptizes him, the Spirit carries Philip away, and the official continues rejoicing. Philip continues preaching as he travels toward Caesarea. The meeting joins God’s direction, careful attention to Scripture and a personal response to the message.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand Philip’s explanation as identifying the suffering servant of Isaiah 53 with Jesus and leading the official to baptism. Traditions differ over how baptism should be performed and over how much this particular account establishes about it.

The Catholic Church’s Catechism, paragraph 1239, calls immersion—being put fully under the water—the most expressive way of performing baptism while also allowing pouring. The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 28, paragraph 3, says immersion is not necessary and accepts pouring or sprinkling. The 1689 Baptist Confession, chapter 29, paragraph 4, requires immersion for proper administration.

Commentators also differ about the descent into the water. Calvin on verse 38 understands it as the ancient practice of immersing the body, while allowing other ways of baptizing. Wesley on verse 38 does not regard the account of going down as establishing immersion by itself.

Some Bible editions include a confession of faith at verse 37. The BSB places it in a footnote, so its main text goes from verse 36 to verse 38. The USCCB’s note on verse 37 explains its absence from the earliest manuscripts. The official’s question, baptism and rejoicing remain in the account.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah 53:7–12 moves from the servant’s suffering and death to bearing the sins of many and bringing them righteousness. Acts explicitly shows Philip beginning with that passage to announce Jesus. Scripture’s suffering servant is central to the good news, not an obstacle that has to be explained away.

Isaiah 56:3–7 also promises welcome and a lasting name for faithful foreigners and eunuchs, joining their hope to God’s house of prayer for all nations. Luke does not explicitly quote that passage here. It provides a meaningful biblical parallel to the welcome and joy of a foreign eunuch responding to Jesus.

What is our position?

Baptism uses the official’s question and baptism as a direct example of responding to the gospel with obedience. The study also reads going down into and coming up from the water alongside the wider biblical picture as supporting immersion as the normal practice. It does not treat every other way of baptizing as automatically invalid in every circumstance. The emphasis is faith expressed in baptism, rather than delaying the response without reason.
