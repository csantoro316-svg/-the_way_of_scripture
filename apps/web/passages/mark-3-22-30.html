Passage

Mark 3:22-30

The Strong Man and the Warning About the Spirit

Berean Standard Bible

22 And the scribes who had come down from Jerusalem were saying, “He is possessed by Beelzebul,” and, “By the prince of the demons He drives out demons.” 23 So Jesus called them together and began to speak to them in parables: “How can Satan drive out Satan?

24 If a kingdom is divided against itself, it cannot stand. 25 If a house is divided against itself, it cannot stand. 26 And if Satan is divided and rises against himself, he cannot stand; his end has come. 27 Indeed, no one can enter a strong man’s house to steal his possessions unless he first ties up the strong man. Then he can plunder his house.

28 Truly I tell you, the sons of men will be forgiven all sins and blasphemies, as many as they utter. 29 But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; he is guilty of eternal sin.” 30 Jesus made this statement because they were saying, “He has an unclean spirit.”

What is the context?

Scribes from Jerusalem explain Jesus' authority over demons by claiming He is possessed and works through the ruler of demons. Jesus answers their charge directly. If Satan is driving out Satan, his kingdom is working against itself and cannot stand.

Jesus then gives a different explanation through the picture of a strong man's house. Someone must first overpower the strong man before taking his possessions. In the surrounding discussion, the picture describes Jesus defeating Satan's hold over people, rather than cooperating with him. Matthew 12:22–29 explicitly identifies these works with God's Spirit and the arrival of God's kingdom.

Jesus speaks of the wide reach of forgiveness, then gives a grave warning about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. Blasphemy comes from βλασφημία, blasphēmia (blas-fay-MEE-ah), speech that slanders or reviles; Thayer's lexicon includes speech dishonoring God. Here Mark supplies the setting for the warning: they are calling the work before them the work of an unclean spirit.

Verse 30 keeps the warning tied to that accusation. Jesus is not discussing an accidental choice of words or a sincere request to understand Him. The passage describes people responding to His freeing work by naming its source evil. Christians differ over how this warning applies beyond that confrontation, as explained below.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly recognize Jesus as the one stronger than Satan. On the Spirit warning, Wesley's note on Mark 3:30 emphasizes attributing to the devil the miracles Jesus works by the Holy Spirit.

The Reformed teacher Calvin, on the parallel Matthew 12:31–32, understands the offense as knowingly and maliciously calling God's revealed work evil. He distinguishes this from ignorance and holds that someone hardened in this way does not return in repentance. The Reformed Westminster Confession, 15.3–4, teaches that pardon is God's grace and that those who truly repent are not condemned by the greatness of their sin; it does not give a verse-by-verse definition here.

The Catholic Church teaches in Catechism 1864 that deliberately refusing the mercy and salvation offered by the Spirit can harden a person into dying without repentance. This puts the emphasis on refusing God's mercy, while Calvin gives a more specific account of knowingly slandering His revealed work. Neither explanation makes God's willingness to show mercy the problem.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The strong-man picture opens onto the larger freedom Jesus brings. Colossians 2:13–15 joins forgiveness with Christ's victory over hostile powers through the cross. Hebrews 2:14–15 says He shares human life and dies to break the devil's power and free people held by fear of death. The deliverances in Mark are signs of a saving work that reaches further than relief in one encounter.

The warning belongs alongside Scripture's account of real mercy. In 1 Timothy 1:12–16, Paul recalls that he had been a blasphemer and persecutor, yet received mercy after acting in ignorance and unbelief. He presents Christ's patience toward him as an example for others who will believe. That testimony shows why serious past opposition should not be turned into a blanket declaration that a person is beyond the reach of grace.
