Passage

Nehemiah 9:26-31

Why does the prayer keep returning to both rebellion and mercy?

Berean Standard Bible

26 But they were disobedient and rebelled against You; they flung Your law behind their backs. They killed Your prophets, who had admonished them to return to You. They committed terrible blasphemies. 27 So You delivered them into the hands of enemies who oppressed them, and in their time of distress they cried out to You. From heaven You heard them, and in Your great compassion You gave them deliverers who saved them from the hands of their enemies. 28 But as soon as they had rest, they again did evil in Your sight. So You abandoned them to the hands of their enemies, who had dominion over them. When they cried out to You again, You heard from heaven, and You delivered them many times in Your compassion.

29 You admonished them to turn back to Your law, but they were arrogant and disobeyed Your commandments. They sinned against Your ordinances, by which a man will live if he practices them. They turned a stubborn shoulder; they stiffened their necks and would not obey. 30 You were patient with them for many years, and Your Spirit admonished them through Your prophets. Yet they would not listen, so You gave them into the hands of the neighboring peoples. 31 But in Your great compassion, You did not put an end to them; nor did You forsake them, for You are a gracious and compassionate God.

What is the context?

The prayer recalls the people rejecting the Law and killing prophets sent to turn them back to God. Oppression follows, but God hears their cries and gives deliverers. Once they have relief, they do evil again.

The pattern continues over many years. God warns them by His Spirit through the prophets, but they will not listen, and they come under the rule of other peoples.

Even then, the prayer gives thanks that God has not destroyed or abandoned them completely. The community now praying exists because His compassion has outlasted their repeated unfaithfulness.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes that the prophets’ warnings came through God’s Spirit, while mercy remained the people’s hope. Gill traces the repeated rescues and the preservation of a people through conquest and exile. The prayer does not minimize judgment; it recognizes that judgment has not canceled every expression of God’s compassion.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Stephen returns to this pattern in Acts 7:35–53. He recalls the rejection of Moses, the golden calf and the persecution of the prophets, then confronts his hearers with the betrayal and killing of the Righteous One.

This is a connection through the same repeated rejection of God’s messengers, brought to its most serious point in the rejection of Christ. Remembering earlier failures is meant to lead to listening now, rather than assuming that knowing the history has already made the response faithful.
