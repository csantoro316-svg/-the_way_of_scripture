Passage

Isaiah 1:1-9

Why does God describe His people as rebellious children?

Berean Standard Bible

1 This is the vision concerning Judah and Jerusalem that Isaiah son of Amoz saw during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah.

2 Listen, O heavens, and give ear, O earth, for the LORD has spoken: “I have raised children and brought them up, but they have rebelled against Me. 3 The ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master’s manger, but Israel does not know; My people do not understand.”

4 Alas, O sinful nation, a people laden with iniquity, a brood of evildoers, children who act corruptly! They have forsaken the LORD; they have despised the Holy One of Israel and turned their backs on Him. 5 Why do you want more beatings? Why do you keep rebelling? Your head has a massive wound, and your whole heart is afflicted. 6 From the sole of your foot to the top of your head, there is no soundness— only wounds and welts and festering sores neither cleansed nor bandaged nor soothed with oil.

7 Your land is desolate; your cities are burned with fire. Foreigners devour your fields before you— a desolation demolished by strangers. 8 And the Daughter of Zion is abandoned like a shelter in a vineyard, like a shack in a cucumber field, like a city besieged. 9 Unless the LORD of Hosts had left us a few survivors, we would have become like Sodom, we would have resembled Gomorrah.

What is the context?

The opening identifies Isaiah son of Amoz and places his vision concerning Judah and Jerusalem within the reigns of four kings: Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. It introduces God’s complaint against people He has raised and cared for, but who have turned away from Him.

The call for heaven and earth to listen recalls the witnesses named in Deuteronomy 4:23–31 and 31:24–29. Israel’s rebellion is measured against its relationship with God and the instruction already received from Him.

An ox recognizes its owner and a donkey its feeding place, yet God’s people fail to recognize Him. The picture then becomes a wounded body, followed by burned cities, devastated fields and Jerusalem left exposed. The charges concern a community’s rebellion and its ruin.

The final verse makes survival depend on God’s mercy. The Hebrew שָׂרִיד, sarid, pronounced sah-REED, means a survivor; Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically lists this verse. A remnant here means those left when destruction has overtaken the rest. Without the Lord preserving survivors, the people would have shared the fate of Sodom and Gomorrah.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ over the main emphasis of the wounded-body picture. Henry emphasizes the spread of sin through the community, from its ordinary members to its leaders. The NABRE notes published by the USCCB emphasize the historical disaster that has wounded the nation because of its sins. Gill discusses both the calamities suffered and the corruption of the people. The following description of the land’s devastation keeps the suffering of Judah in view.

These readings agree that the passage exposes serious rebellion and credits God with preserving survivors. The picture concerns the people’s condition as a whole; it is not a diagnosis explaining every individual’s physical illness.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Paul explicitly quotes verse 9 in Romans 9:22–33. He uses Isaiah’s words about descendants being left to show that Israel’s story continues through God’s mercy, then discusses righteousness received through faith.

The connection is made by the New Testament itself: the survivors Isaiah describes become part of Paul’s explanation of God’s faithful purposes. The hope rests in the Lord who preserves a people, rather than in the assumption that belonging to the nation makes judgment impossible.
