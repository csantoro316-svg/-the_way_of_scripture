Passage

Isaiah 9:18-21

How does wickedness turn a people against itself?

Berean Standard Bible

18 For wickedness burns like a fire that consumes the thorns and briers and kindles the forest thickets, which roll upward in billows of smoke. 19 By the wrath of the LORD of Hosts the land is scorched, and the people are fuel for the fire. No man even spares his brother.

20 They carve out what is on the right, but they are still hungry; they eat what is on the left, but they are still not satisfied. Each one devours the flesh of his own offspring. 21 Manasseh devours Ephraim, and Ephraim Manasseh; together they turn against Judah. Despite all this, His anger is not turned away; His hand is still upraised.

What is the context?

Wickedness spreads like fire from thorns and briers into the forest. The picture then includes the wrath of the Lord: the land is scorched and the people themselves become fuel. Human wrongdoing and God’s judgment meet in the destruction described here.

The violence reaches the closest relationships. No one spares his brother; grabbing and eating bring no satisfaction, and the language even speaks of devouring one’s own offspring. The closing verse names Manasseh and Ephraim consuming one another, then joining against Judah.

This is a community destroying itself from within while also turning against its neighbor. The repeated words about the Lord’s anger leave the warning unresolved: the damage has not brought the people back to Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Gill emphasize the fighting and plundering through which people consume their own relatives and neighbors. Gill connects the devouring language with the conflict between Manasseh and Ephraim in the final verse.

The NABRE notes also identify civil conflict in the northern kingdom as the setting of the warning. Across these readings, the fire and devouring images expose how wrongdoing destroys the bonds that should hold a people together.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A related warning appears in Galatians 5:13–26. Paul warns believers that biting and devouring one another can end in mutual destruction. The verbal picture resembles Isaiah’s warning, though Paul does not identify it as a quotation.

Paul also sets out a different way of life: people called to freedom in Christ are to serve one another in love and walk by the Spirit. Love, patience, kindness and self-control replace the rivalries that consume a community. The connection carries the theme from the ruin caused by sin to the life God produces among His people.
