Passage

1 Timothy 6:11-16

What should Timothy pursue while he waits for Christ?

Berean Standard Bible

11 But you, O man of God, flee from these things and pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, perseverance, and gentleness. 12 Fight the good fight of the faith. Take hold of the eternal life to which you were called when you made the good confession before many witnesses.

13 I charge you in the presence of God, who gives life to all things, and of Christ Jesus, who made the good confession in His testimony before Pontius Pilate: 14 Keep this commandment without stain or reproach until the appearance of our Lord Jesus Christ, 15 which the blessed and only Sovereign One—the King of kings and Lord of lords—will bring about in His own time. 16 He alone is immortal and dwells in unapproachable light. No one has ever seen Him, nor can anyone see Him. To Him be honor and eternal dominion! Amen.

What is the context?

Paul turns directly to Timothy: leave greed behind and pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, perseverance and gentleness. Fighting the good fight belongs with those qualities. It is a call to faithful endurance, rather than another invitation to the quarrels just condemned.

Timothy has publicly confessed his faith. Paul now charges him before the life-giving God and Christ Jesus, who bore witness before Pilate, to keep his calling faithfully until Christ appears.

The closing praise lifts his eyes beyond present pressures to God’s rule and life. Christ’s appearing will come in God’s own time. God’s unapproachable light and unique immortality emphasize His greatness beyond human power or sight.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley suggests Timothy’s public confession may have occurred at baptism. Calvin understands it more broadly as the witness of Timothy’s ministry. The occasion is not named in the passage. Both read the charge as a call to persevere in faith and faithful service.

Major Christian readings agree that God is the source of life. Calvin and Wesley understand His unique immortality to mean that He has life in Himself; whatever life creatures have comes from Him.

Where traditions differ is what happens to a person at death. The Catholic Church teaches that the soul survives bodily death and will be reunited with the body at the resurrection (Catechism 366). The Reformed Westminster Confession 32.1–2 likewise teaches conscious life after death and a future bodily resurrection. Seventh-day Adventists instead teach that death is an unconscious state until resurrection, when God gives eternal life to His redeemed (Belief 26). These readings all depend on God giving life; they differ over the soul’s condition between death and resurrection.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The passage itself connects Timothy’s endurance with Jesus’ witness before Pilate and His future appearing. In John 18:33–37, Jesus speaks of His kingship and His coming into the world to bear witness to the truth. Timothy serves under that King while he waits for the King’s return.

2 Timothy 1:8–10 places suffering for the gospel within God’s gracious purpose and Christ’s victory over death. The life Timothy is called to hold onto has its foundation in what God has done through Christ, rather than in the security wealth can offer.

What is our position?

In Who is The Angel of the Lord?, we understand the unseen One in this passage as the Father. Read alongside John 1:18, this helps explain how people in the Old Testament could encounter God visibly: our study identifies those appearances with the eternal Son, who makes the Father known. This is the position developed in that study, rather than a conclusion supplied by the word light on its own.
