Passage

Ezekiel 10:18-22

Where does the Lord’s glory go next?

Berean Standard Bible

18 Then the glory of the LORD moved away from the threshold of the temple and stood above the cherubim. 19 As I watched, the cherubim lifted their wings and rose up from the ground, with the wheels beside them as they went. And they stopped at the entrance of the east gate of the house of the LORD, with the glory of the God of Israel above them.

20 These were the living creatures I had seen beneath the God of Israel by the River Kebar, and I knew that they were cherubim. 21 Each had four faces and four wings, with what looked like human hands under their wings. 22 Their faces looked like the faces I had seen by the River Kebar. Each creature went straight ahead.

What is the context?

The Lord’s glory moves away from the temple threshold and stands above the cherubim. They lift their wings and rise with the wheels, then stop at the entrance of the temple’s east gate.

The movement carries the vision toward departure. The glory has not simply vanished; Ezekiel is shown it moving from the sanctuary toward the edge of the temple grounds.

He again identifies the creatures as those he saw beneath the God of Israel at the River Kebar. He now knows that they are cherubim. Their faces, wings, hands and straight movement join this scene to the first vision.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley emphasize that the Lord’s withdrawal takes place in stages. They see the pauses as expressing His patience toward a people whose conduct has provoked judgment.

The scene also challenges the assumption that possessing the temple guarantees God’s protection. His glory moves beyond the building while remaining above the cherubim. The God who judged its corruption is free to leave it.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John 1:9–18 brings the theme of God dwelling among people to Jesus: the Word becomes flesh, and His witnesses see the Son’s glory. Hebrews 1:1–4 describes the Son as the radiance of God’s glory and the exact representation of His nature.

These passages do not explicitly identify every feature of Ezekiel’s vision with a feature of Jesus’ earthly life. They do explain where the larger theme leads: God makes Himself known through His Son. The departure from a corrupted sanctuary is one part of Scripture’s longer account of God dwelling with His people.
