Passage

Isaiah 1:10-20

Why does God reject worship that leaves injustice untouched?

Berean Standard Bible

10 Hear the word of the LORD, you rulers of Sodom; listen to the instruction of our God, you people of Gomorrah! 11 “What good to Me is your multitude of sacrifices?” says the LORD. “I am full from the burnt offerings of rams and the fat of well-fed cattle; I take no delight in the blood of bulls and lambs and goats. 12 When you come to appear before Me, who has required this of you— this trampling of My courts? 13 Bring your worthless offerings no more; your incense is detestable to Me. New Moons, Sabbaths, and convocations— I cannot endure iniquity in a solemn assembly. 14 I hate your New Moons and your appointed feasts. They have become a burden to Me; I am weary of bearing them. 15 When you spread out your hands in prayer, I will hide My eyes from you; even though you multiply your prayers, I will not listen. Your hands are covered with blood.

16 Wash and cleanse yourselves. Remove your evil deeds from My sight. Stop doing evil! 17 Learn to do right; seek justice and correct the oppressor. Defend the fatherless and plead the case of the widow.”

18 “Come now, let us reason together,” says the LORD. “Though your sins are like scarlet, they will be as white as snow; though they are as red as crimson, they will become like wool. 19 If you are willing and obedient, you will eat the best of the land. 20 But if you resist and rebel, you will be devoured by the sword.” For the mouth of the LORD has spoken.

What is the context?

The names Sodom and Gomorrah now address Judah’s own rulers and people. They escaped those cities’ destruction, yet their conduct invites the comparison. God rejects their many sacrifices, incense, feasts, sacred gatherings and prayers while their hands remain covered with blood.

The problem is made concrete in the call that follows: stop doing evil, learn to do right, seek justice, correct oppression and defend the fatherless and widow. The worshipers are being called to change how they treat people, especially those who lack protection.

God also invites them to bring the matter before Him. Scarlet and crimson become white as snow or wool, a picture of deep guilt being cleansed. The invitation is followed by the choice between willing obedience and continued rebellion, with enjoyment of the land set against destruction by the sword.

The passage holds mercy and responsibility together. Costly religious activity cannot make ongoing injustice acceptable, and the seriousness of the charges does not mean God has closed the door to forgiveness.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the sacrifices as outward religion being offered without a changed heart and life. The NABRE notes likewise connect the rejected worship with oppression and understand the invitation as God offering to settle His dispute with Israel on the basis of the change demanded.

Gill gives verse 18 a more distinct place within the passage: he reads it as a comforting promise to the preserved remnant and explains its cleansing through Christ. Henry also connects cleansing with God’s pardoning mercy. The difference concerns how the promise relates to its immediate audience and surrounding commands, while both see hope for people conscious of serious sin.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The same concern for worship expressed in daily conduct appears in James 1:19–27. James calls for doing the word, controlling the tongue, caring for orphans and widows, and remaining unstained by the world.

Titus 3:3–8 joins cleansing and changed conduct with their source in God’s mercy. Salvation comes through new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit, poured out through Jesus Christ; those who believe are then to devote themselves to good deeds. The wider purpose is a people made clean by God whose lives show His goodness toward others.
