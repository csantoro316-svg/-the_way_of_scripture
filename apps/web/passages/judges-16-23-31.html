Passage

Judges 16:23-31

How does Samson’s last prayer change the celebration?

Berean Standard Bible

23 Now the lords of the Philistines gathered together to offer a great sacrifice to their god Dagon. They rejoiced and said, “Our god has delivered Samson our enemy into our hands.” 24 And when the people saw him, they praised their god, saying: “Our god has delivered into our hands our enemy who destroyed our land and multiplied our dead.” 25 And while their hearts were merry, they said, “Call for Samson to entertain us.” So they called Samson out of the prison to entertain them. And they stationed him between the pillars.

26 Samson said to the servant who held his hand, “Lead me where I can feel the pillars supporting the temple, so I can lean against them.” 27 Now the temple was full of men and women; all the lords of the Philistines were there, and about three thousand men and women were on the roof watching Samson entertain them. 28 Then Samson called out to the LORD: “O Lord GOD, please remember me. Strengthen me, O God, just once more, so that with one vengeful blow I may pay back the Philistines for my two eyes.”

29 And Samson reached out for the two central pillars supporting the temple. Bracing himself against them with his right hand on one pillar and his left hand on the other, 30 Samson said, “Let me die with the Philistines.” Then he pushed with all his might, and the temple fell on the lords and all the people in it. So in his death he killed more than he had killed in his life. 31 Then Samson’s brothers and his father’s family came down, carried him back, and buried him between Zorah and Eshtaol in the tomb of his father Manoah. And he had judged Israel twenty years.

What is the context?

The Philistine lords gather to sacrifice to Dagon and celebrate Samson’s capture. They credit their god with delivering the enemy who devastated their land. Samson is brought from prison to entertain them and placed between the building’s supporting pillars.

He asks the servant guiding him to let him feel the pillars. The building is full, all the Philistine lords are present, and about three thousand men and women watch from the roof. Samson prays for God to remember and strengthen him once more, asking to avenge the loss of his eyes.

He braces himself against the two central pillars and pushes. The building falls on the lords and the people inside, and Samson dies with them. The narrator says he kills more in his death than during his life. His brothers and his father’s family recover his body and bury him in Manoah’s tomb, closing the account of his twenty years as judge.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Augustine, in City of God 1.21, explains Samson’s death as an exceptional act that can be justified only by special direction from God. His reasoning does not turn Samson’s action into general permission to kill oneself or others. The special direction is Augustine’s explanation; Judges records the prayer and outcome without quoting an additional command.

Wesley and Henry also understand this as a judge acting for God’s honor and Israel’s deliverance, with the answered prayer showing divine approval. They distinguish that purpose from ordinary personal revenge, even though Samson’s recorded request specifically mentions his eyes. These are their readings of the event; the passage supplies both the public setting and his personal words.

Both Protestant commentators also see a comparison with Christ: death becomes the means of defeating an enemy. They regard the final prayer as renewed dependence on God. The story ends with Samson seeking strength from the Lord rather than assuming that past strength guarantees what he can do.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The comparison with Jesus is strongest in the outcome: a death that looks like defeat brings victory. Hebrews 2:14–18 states the Christian claim directly—Jesus shares our humanity and through death breaks the devil’s power, releasing people held in fear of death.

The differences matter as well. Samson asks for vengeance for his eyes and dies while destroying his captors. In John 10:11–18, Jesus describes willingly laying down His life for His sheep and taking it up again. Samson’s death brings the account of his service to an end; Jesus’ death and resurrection bring His people lasting rescue. This is a comparison drawn from the two accounts, not a claim that the New Testament explains every detail of Samson’s death as a prediction.
