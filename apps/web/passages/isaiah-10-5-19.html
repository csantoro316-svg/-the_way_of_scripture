Passage

Isaiah 10:5-19

How can God use Assyria and still judge its king?

Berean Standard Bible

5 Woe to Assyria, the rod of My anger; the staff in their hands is My wrath. 6 I will send him against a godless nation; I will dispatch him against a people destined for My rage, to take spoils and seize plunder, and to trample them down like clay in the streets. 7 But this is not his intention; this is not his plan. For it is in his heart to destroy and cut off many nations.

8 “Are not all my commanders kings?” he says. 9 “Is not Calno like Carchemish? Is not Hamath like Arpad? Is not Samaria like Damascus? 10 As my hand seized the idolatrous kingdoms whose images surpassed those of Jerusalem and Samaria, 11 and as I have done to Samaria and its idols, will I not also do to Jerusalem and her idols?”

12 So when the Lord has completed all His work against Mount Zion and Jerusalem, He will say, “I will punish the king of Assyria for the fruit of his arrogant heart and the proud look in his eyes. 13 For he says: ‘By the strength of my hand I have done this, and by my wisdom, for I am clever. I have removed the boundaries of nations and plundered their treasures; like a mighty one I subdued their rulers. 14 My hand reached as into a nest to seize the wealth of the nations. Like one gathering abandoned eggs, I gathered all the earth. No wing fluttered, no beak opened or chirped.’”

15 Does an axe raise itself above the one who swings it? Does a saw boast over him who saws with it? It would be like a rod waving the one who lifts it, or a staff lifting him who is not wood! 16 Therefore the Lord GOD of Hosts will send a wasting disease among Assyria’s stout warriors, and under his pomp will be kindled a fire like a burning flame. 17 And the Light of Israel will become a fire, and its Holy One a flame. In a single day it will burn and devour Assyria’s thorns and thistles. 18 The splendor of its forests and orchards, both soul and body, it will completely destroy, as a sickness consumes a man. 19 The remaining trees of its forests will be so few that a child could count them.

What is the context?

Assyria is called the rod of God’s anger, an instrument He uses in judgment. But verse 7 immediately distinguishes God’s purpose from the king’s intention. The king aims to destroy nations and enlarge his own power.

His boast treats conquered cities as proof that Jerusalem must fall as well. He speaks as though the Lord can be measured alongside the idols of defeated kingdoms. Then God declares that, after completing His work concerning Zion and Jerusalem, He will punish the king’s arrogance.

The axe and saw expose the mistake. A tool cannot rightly boast over the person using it, yet the king credits his own strength and wisdom with everything he has gained. The image does not remove his responsibility; his proud words and destructive purpose are precisely what bring judgment.

The army’s strength is then pictured as a forest that will burn away, leaving so few trees that a child could count them. The empire that appears unstoppable remains subject to the Lord.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley both distinguish God’s purpose in correcting His people from the conqueror’s ambition. They understand Assyria as serving a purpose it neither recognizes nor shares.

Gill likewise says that God’s use of the king does not mean the king consciously intends to obey Him. Across these readings, being used in God’s judgment gives the king no excuse for cruelty or pride. The Lord who governs his success can also bring him down.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God’s purpose can move forward even through people whose intentions are evil. A major example appears in Acts 4:23–31: the believers name the rulers and peoples who gathered against Jesus, then acknowledge that the events of His death carried out what God had determined beforehand.

This is a shared biblical theme, rather than a claim that Assyria’s king represents Jesus or that Acts directly quotes this passage. In the account of Christ, human opposition becomes part of the saving work it seeks to stop. God’s rule over events does not turn the opponents’ hostility into faithful obedience.
