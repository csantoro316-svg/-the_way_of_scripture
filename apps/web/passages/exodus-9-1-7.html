Passage

Exodus 9:1-7

What did Pharaoh learn when the livestock were struck?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then the LORD said to Moses, “Go to Pharaoh and tell him that this is what the LORD, the God of the Hebrews, says: ‘Let My people go, so that they may worship Me. 2 But if you continue to restrain them and refuse to let them go, 3 then the hand of the LORD will bring a severe plague on your livestock in the field—on your horses, donkeys, camels, herds, and flocks. 4 But the LORD will make a distinction between the livestock of Israel and the livestock of Egypt, so that no animal belonging to the Israelites will die.’” 5 The LORD set a time, saying, “Tomorrow the LORD will do this in the land.” 6 And the next day the LORD did just that. All the livestock of the Egyptians died, but not one animal belonging to the Israelites died. 7 Pharaoh sent officials and found that none of the livestock of the Israelites had died. But Pharaoh’s heart was hardened, and he would not let the people go.

What is the context?

God again demands the release of His people for worship and warns of a severe plague on Egypt’s livestock in the field. Horses, donkeys, camels, herds and flocks are named. The judgment reaches the animals upon which the people’s work and households depend.

A specific time and distinction are announced: the plague will come the next day, and no animal belonging to Israel will die. The event occurs as declared. Pharaoh sends officials to check and learns that Israel’s livestock have indeed been spared.

The investigation confirms what Moses said, but Pharaoh still refuses to release the people. This is another step in the confrontation: he has checked the evidence of the distinction and continues to resist.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The shared reading sees the timing and preservation of Israel’s livestock as demonstrating God’s deliberate action. The description that all the Egyptian livestock died is explained in more than one way, since animals remain in the later account. Wesley understands it as all those in the field, following the scope of the warning in verse 3. Calvin takes it as a broad description of devastating losses throughout the herds, rather than the death of every animal in Egypt.
