Passage

James 5:14-16

What are prayer, healing and confession doing together?

Berean Standard Bible

14 Is any one of you sick? He should call the elders of the church to pray over him and anoint him with oil in the name of the Lord. 15 And the prayer offered in faith will restore the one who is sick. The Lord will raise him up. If he has sinned, he will be forgiven. 16 Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous man has great power to prevail.

What is the context?

James joins elders’ prayer, anointing, the Lord’s raising up, forgiveness and the confession of sins. The person in need is not left to carry the condition alone, and the believers are told to pray for each other.

The wording if he has sinned keeps the connection with personal sin conditional. James calls for honesty and restoration without making every sick person guilty of a particular hidden offense.

The words for sickness and restoration allow bodily as well as spiritual concerns. The promise cannot be explained simply by declaring that the Greek rules physical healing out.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The Catholic Church understands the anointing as a sacrament (Catechism, 1510–1513), bringing spiritual help and bodily healing if God wills (1520). It also teaches a priestly ministry of reconciliation in which God forgives (1441–1448).

Calvin connects the oil with the early church’s temporary gift of healing, while Wesley understands the practice as intended to continue. Both see mutual confession as prompting believers to pray for one another; Wesley explicitly refuses to confine that confession to elders.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Mark 6:12–13 places anointing and healing beside repentance in the disciples’ mission. John 9:1–3 also shows why care must not become an automatic accusation of personal sin.

2 Corinthians 12:7–10 brings another part of Christ’s care into view: strength given through continuing weakness. His saving purposes encompass more than the immediate removal of every painful condition.

What is our position?

Our study Prayer and Faith reads this passage as a call to seek spiritual restoration and renewed fellowship with God, with physical recovery remaining in His hands. It understands faithful prayer as dependence on God’s will, rather than an unconditional promise of healing produced by the strength of someone’s belief. Our study Baptism understands the mutual confession here as accountability and healing among believers, rather than a priestly requirement for receiving God’s forgiveness.
