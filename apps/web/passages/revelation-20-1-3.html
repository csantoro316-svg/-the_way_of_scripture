Passage

Revelation 20:1-3

What does the binding of Satan accomplish?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I saw an angel coming down from heaven with the key to the Abyss, holding in his hand a great chain. 2 He seized the dragon, that ancient serpent who is the devil and Satan, and bound him for a thousand years. 3 And he threw him into the Abyss, shut it, and sealed it over him, so that he could not deceive the nations until the thousand years were complete. After that, he must be released for a brief period of time.

What is the context?

After the beast and false prophet are defeated, John sees an angel descend with a key and a great chain. The angel seizes the dragon, whose identity is made explicit: the ancient serpent, the devil and Satan. The enemy behind the beast’s power is now restrained.

The angel throws him into the Abyss, closes it and seals it over him. The passage states the purpose: Satan cannot deceive the nations until the thousand years are complete. The Abyss is his prison in this scene. His final fate in the lake of fire comes later, in verse 10.

The restraint has a stated limit. After the thousand years, Satan must be released for a brief time. John announces that release before describing the reign of those who belong to Christ. The vision presents the enemy as subject to a limit on both his activity and his time.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians differ over when the thousand years occur and what the binding prevents. Walvoord reads this as a future confinement after Christ’s return, with Satan removed from activity in the world for a literal thousand years. He points to the sequence after chapter 19 and to the repeated statements about confinement and its duration.

The Catholic USCCB notes understand the thousand years as the period between Christ’s resurrection victory and the end of the world. Augustine, City of God 20.7–8, explains the binding as a restraint that allows people among the nations to be brought to Christ. His reading allows continuing temptation and persecution during this period; he does not understand the binding as an end to every activity of evil.

Wesley also places the binding in the future, after the beast’s overthrow, though his account of the following reign differs from Walvoord’s. He is cautious about deciding exactly how literally to take every feature of the chain and seal. These differences concern the vision’s timing and imagery; each reading recognizes God’s authority over Satan.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The binding belongs within the Bible’s larger account of Christ overcoming the enemy. In Mark 3:22–27, Jesus answers an accusation about His authority over demons by describing a strong man who must be bound before his house can be plundered. The picture connects the defeat of Satan’s hold with the rescue Jesus brings.

Colossians 2:13–15 connects forgiveness and the cross with Christ’s triumph over hostile powers. Revelation carries the story through restraint, a final release and eventual judgment. Readers connect these stages differently, but the larger purpose is consistent: evil does not keep its dominion, and God brings His work of rescue to completion.
