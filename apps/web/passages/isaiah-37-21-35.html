Passage

Isaiah 37:21-35

Why can Assyria’s success never make its king greater than God?

Berean Standard Bible

21 Then Isaiah son of Amoz sent a message to Hezekiah: “This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: Because you have prayed to Me concerning Sennacherib king of Assyria, 22 this is the word that the LORD has spoken against him: ‘The Virgin Daughter of Zion despises you and mocks you; the Daughter of Jerusalem shakes her head behind you. 23 Whom have you taunted and blasphemed? Against whom have you raised your voice and lifted your eyes in pride? Against the Holy One of Israel! 24 Through your servants you have taunted the Lord, and you have said: “With my many chariots I have ascended to the heights of the mountains, to the remote peaks of Lebanon. I have cut down its tallest cedars, the finest of its cypresses. I have reached its farthest heights, the densest of its forests. 25 I have dug wells and drunk foreign waters. With the soles of my feet I have dried up all the streams of Egypt.”

26 Have you not heard? Long ago I ordained it; in days of old I planned it. Now I have brought it to pass, that you should crush fortified cities into piles of rubble. 27 Therefore their inhabitants, devoid of power, are dismayed and ashamed. They are like plants in the field, tender green shoots, grass on the rooftops, scorched before it is grown. 28 But I know your sitting down, your going out and coming in, and your raging against Me. 29 Because your rage and arrogance against Me have reached My ears, I will put My hook in your nose and My bit in your mouth; I will send you back the way you came.’

30 And this will be a sign to you, O Hezekiah: This year you will eat what grows on its own, and in the second year what springs from the same. But in the third year you will sow and reap; you will plant vineyards and eat their fruit. 31 And the surviving remnant of the house of Judah will again take root below and bear fruit above. 32 For a remnant will go forth from Jerusalem, and survivors from Mount Zion. The zeal of the LORD of Hosts will accomplish this.

33 So this is what the LORD says about the king of Assyria: ‘He will not enter this city or shoot an arrow into it. He will not come before it with a shield or build up a siege ramp against it. 34 He will go back the way he came, and he will not enter this city,’ declares the LORD. 35 ‘I will defend this city and save it for My own sake and for the sake of My servant David.’”

What is the context?

Isaiah sends the Lord’s answer because Hezekiah has prayed concerning Sennacherib. The king’s boasts about conquest are exposed as a challenge against the Holy One of Israel. Jerusalem’s threatened people are pictured answering their tormentor with contempt.

The Lord then states what the boast ignores: He planned long ago what has now happened. The fortified cities did not fall because Assyria possessed power independent of Him. He knows the king’s movements and rage, and will turn him back as an animal is controlled by a hook and bit.

A sign of renewed ordinary life is given to Hezekiah. The people will eat what grows on its own for two years, then sow, reap and plant vineyards in the third. The surviving remnant of Judah will take root and bear fruit again.

The final promise is specific. The Assyrian king will not enter Jerusalem or carry the announced attack through there. The Lord will defend the city for His own sake and for the sake of His servant David.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry’s treatment of the parallel passage and Gill emphasize that Assyria’s actions remain under God’s knowledge and government. Military success gives the king no ground to exalt himself against the One who allowed it.

Both connect the harvest sign with recovery after disruption. Henry considers whether a Sabbath year also explains the second year’s produce; Gill discusses that possibility alongside the interruption caused by invasion. The promise itself describes provision and the return of sowing and reaping.

Gill connects the reference to David with God’s promise concerning David’s descendants and, ultimately, the Messiah. Henry likewise places the rescue in God’s purpose and glory, rather than treating it as a reward earned by Jerusalem’s inhabitants.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The reference to David reaches back to 2 Samuel 7:12–16, where the Lord promises an enduring house and kingdom. Isaiah’s rescue promise therefore belongs within a history longer than the immediate siege.

Luke 1:30–33 announces that Jesus will receive the throne of His father David and reign over the house of Jacob forever. His kingdom will not end.

This connection follows the promise named in Isaiah and its New Testament continuation. Jerusalem’s rescue is a real event in Judah’s history; the greater hope is the lasting reign God brings through David’s promised Son.
