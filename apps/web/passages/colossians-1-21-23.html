Passage

Colossians 1:21-23

What does reconciliation mean for the life ahead?

Berean Standard Bible

21 Once you were alienated from God and were hostile in your minds, engaging in evil deeds. 22 But now He has reconciled you by Christ’s physical body through death to present you holy, unblemished, and blameless in His presence— 23 if indeed you continue in your faith, established and firm, not moved from the hope of the gospel you heard, which has been proclaimed to every creature under heaven, and of which I, Paul, have become a servant.

What is the context?

After describing Christ’s supremacy and the peace made through His cross, Paul addresses the believers’ own change. They were once alienated, hostile in mind and doing evil. Now God has reconciled them through Christ’s physical body in death.

The purpose is to present them holy, unblemished and blameless in His presence. Reconciliation is directed toward that completed life with Him.

Paul attaches a serious appeal: continue established and firm in the faith, holding to the gospel’s hope. He places their local hearing of that message within its worldwide proclamation and names himself as its servant. The next paragraph explains the work and suffering this service involves.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that the passage calls believers to remain in the gospel’s hope. Traditions differ over how this condition relates to the possibility of finally falling away.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 17, teaches that those truly accepted in Christ will persevere because God preserves them. It also allows for serious falls into sin. On that account, continuing faith belongs to the life God sustains, rather than earning reconciliation.

The Catholic Catechism, paragraph 162, teaches that faith is God’s free gift and can be lost; it calls believers to nourish faith through God’s word, prayer and love.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, article XII, teach that people who have received the Spirit may depart from grace, while also affirming repentance and restoration by grace. Wesley’s note on verse 23 reads the condition as a real warning about losing blessings already received. These readings all take the appeal seriously, but understand its relationship to final salvation differently.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ephesians 5:25–27 describes Christ giving Himself for the church so that He may present her holy and without blemish. It supplies a close parallel to the purpose of His death named here: a people made ready for life with Him.

Romans 8:29–30 likewise describes God’s purpose of likeness to His Son and glory. That is the future toward which Paul directs this call to remain in faith.
