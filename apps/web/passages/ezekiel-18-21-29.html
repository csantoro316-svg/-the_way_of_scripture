Passage

Ezekiel 18:21-29

Does turning back still matter after years of wrongdoing?

Berean Standard Bible

21 But if the wicked man turns from all the sins he has committed, keeps all My statutes, and does what is just and right, he will surely live; he will not die. 22 None of the transgressions he has committed will be held against him. Because of the righteousness he has practiced, he will live. 23 Do I take any pleasure in the death of the wicked? declares the Lord GOD. Wouldn’t I prefer that he turn from his ways and live? 24 But if a righteous man turns from his righteousness and practices iniquity, committing the same abominations as the wicked, will he live? None of the righteous acts he did will be remembered. Because of the unfaithfulness and sin he has committed, he will die.

25 Yet you say, ‘The way of the Lord is not just.’ Hear now, O house of Israel: Is it My way that is unjust? Is it not your ways that are unjust? 26 If a righteous man turns from his righteousness and practices iniquity, he will die for this. He will die because of the iniquity he has committed. 27 But if a wicked man turns from the wickedness he has committed and does what is just and right, he will save his life. 28 Because he considered and turned from all the transgressions he had committed, he will surely live; he will not die. 29 Yet the house of Israel says, ‘The way of the Lord is not just.’ Are My ways unjust, O house of Israel? Is it not your ways that are unjust?

What is the context?

The Lord now considers change within a person’s own life. If a wicked person turns from sin and practices justice, the earlier transgressions will not be held against him. The Lord asks whether He delights in the wicked person’s death, rather than that person turning and living.

The warning also runs the other way. Earlier righteous conduct is no shield for someone who turns to the same abominations as the wicked. The person cannot rely on a former way of life while choosing its opposite.

The people call the Lord’s way unjust. He answers by repeating both examples: the person who turns to wrongdoing answers for it, and the person who considers and turns away from wrongdoing will live.

The call therefore refuses both despair and presumption. A sinful past does not make repentance pointless, and a good past does not make present unfaithfulness safe.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Gill reads the person who abandons righteousness as someone outwardly righteous in human judgment, rather than someone who loses a true saving relationship with Christ. Henry also distinguishes appearing righteous for a time from the faith that continues to the end. These are their explanations of the warning, not words the passage itself adds to its description.

Christians’ wider teachings differ here. The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 17, and the 1689 Baptist Confession, chapter 17, teach that those God truly calls and sanctifies will be preserved to the end. They also teach that serious sin can bring grave harm and discipline.

The Catholic Church teaches that grave sin committed with full knowledge and deliberate consent can result in loss of the state of grace, and calls for repentance and God’s forgiveness; see the Catechism, paragraphs 1857–1861. Methodist Article XII teaches that people may depart from grace after receiving the Holy Spirit and may rise again through God’s grace and repentance.

These documents state broader beliefs about grace and falling away, rather than giving official commentary on every verse of Ezekiel. Across these different explanations, the passage’s demand remains urgent: turn from wrongdoing instead of treating either one’s past sins or past goodness as a reason not to respond.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Luke 15:11–24, Jesus tells of a son who squanders what he received, comes to his senses and returns to his father. The father receives him with compassion and celebrates the return of the one who was lost.

This is a parallel in the call to return and the welcome of mercy, rather than an explicit interpretation of Ezekiel 18. It shows why repentance need not be treated as hopeless: the invitation to turn belongs within God’s purpose to restore people to life with Him.
