Passage

Judges 7:9-14

How does God encourage Gideon before the battle?

Berean Standard Bible

9 That night the LORD said to Gideon, “Get up and go down against the camp, for I have delivered it into your hand. 10 But if you are afraid to do so, then go down to the camp with your servant Purah 11 and listen to what they are saying. Then your hands will be strengthened to attack the camp.” So he went with Purah his servant to the outposts where armed men were guarding the camp. 12 Now the Midianites, Amalekites, and all the other people of the east had settled in the valley like a swarm of locusts, and their camels were as countless as the sand on the seashore.

13 And as Gideon arrived, a man was telling his friend about a dream. “Behold, I had a dream,” he said, “and I saw a loaf of barley bread come tumbling into the Midianite camp. It struck the tent so hard that the tent overturned and collapsed.” 14 His friend replied: “This is nothing less than the sword of Gideon son of Joash, the Israelite. God has delivered Midian and the whole camp into his hand.”

What is the context?

That night, the LORD tells Gideon to go against the camp because He has given it into his hand. He also provides reassurance: if Gideon is afraid, he may take his servant Purah down to listen to the enemy. What he hears will strengthen him. God’s response acknowledges his fear and gives him help to act.

The great enemy camp remains in view, with people compared to locusts and camels to the sand on the seashore. At its edge, Gideon hears one man describe a dream in which a barley loaf overturns a tent. The man’s companion interprets it as Gideon’s victory, saying that God has given Midian and the whole camp into his hand.

This exchange confirms the promise Gideon has already received. The assurance comes from an unexpected place: the people he is about to face. The next passage records its effect on him—worship followed by action.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the dream and its interpretation as encouragement God provides through people outside Israel. Henry emphasizes God’s care in bringing Gideon to hear words that strengthen his confidence. The event is read in light of the LORD’s prior promise that this visit would encourage him, rather than as a dream whose meaning Gideon simply invents.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The need for confidence in God’s promise reaches beyond Gideon’s immediate battle. Second Corinthians 4:13–18 grounds Christian courage in the God who raised Jesus and will raise His people with Him. Paul can speak of suffering honestly while looking toward a lasting glory.

Gideon receives reassurance about a particular rescue. Through Jesus’ resurrection, believers receive hope that reaches even beyond death. The larger biblical theme is trust in God’s ability to fulfill what He has promised, with our eyes on the future He has made known.
