Passage

Matthew 12:22-30

The Stronger One Sets People Free

Berean Standard Bible

22 Then a demon-possessed man who was blind and mute was brought to Jesus, and He healed the man so that he could speak and see. 23 The crowds were astounded and asked, “Could this be the Son of David?” 24 But when the Pharisees heard this, they said, “Only by Beelzebul, the prince of demons, does this man drive out demons.”

25 Knowing their thoughts, Jesus said to them, “Every kingdom divided against itself will be laid waste, and every city or household divided against itself will not stand. 26 If Satan drives out Satan, he is divided against himself. How then can his kingdom stand? 27 And if I drive out demons by Beelzebul, by whom do your sons drive them out? So then, they will be your judges.

28 But if I drive out demons by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you. 29 Or again, how can anyone enter a strong man’s house and steal his possessions, unless he first ties up the strong man? Then he can plunder his house. 30 He who is not with Me is against Me, and he who does not gather with Me scatters.

What is the context?

Jesus heals a man described as demon-possessed, blind, and mute. The man can now speak and see. The crowds ask whether Jesus could be the Son of David, while the Pharisees attribute His work to Beelzebul, whom they call the prince of demons. The disagreement concerns the source and meaning of His power.

Jesus answers the accusation through the picture of a divided kingdom or household. If Satan is driving out Satan, he is undoing his own rule. Jesus also asks His opponents to apply their reasoning to the people among them who drive out demons. Their accusation cannot be treated as a fair standard only when it is used against Him.

His positive explanation is that He acts by God's Spirit and that God's kingdom has come upon them. The Greek verb is φθάνω, phthanō (FTHAH-noh), to reach or arrive. Thayer's entry discusses this verse, including the sense of arrival earlier than expected. Jesus directs attention to God's rule already at work in the release they have witnessed.

The strong man's house gives another picture of what is happening. Someone must first overcome the strong man before taking his possessions. In this setting, Jesus is overcoming Satan's hold and setting people free. Luke 11:20–22 makes the comparison explicit through someone stronger defeating the armed man.

The final saying calls for a response. Being with Jesus means joining His work of gathering; opposing Him means scattering. His opponents have seen release and called it evil. Jesus presses them to recognize what God is doing through Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the healing and strong-man picture as evidence that Jesus defeats Satan through God's power. Wesley on Matthew 12:27–30 reads His work as taking people from Satan's rule and calls for allegiance to Him. The Catholic Catechism, 550, similarly connects His freeing people from demons with the arrival of God's kingdom and the victory accomplished through His cross. The shared emphasis is Christ's victory and the response His work calls for.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The victory reaches beyond this one healing. Hebrews 2:14–15 says that Jesus shares human life and goes through death to defeat the devil's power and free people enslaved by fear of death. The One who releases the man here will give Himself to bring a deeper freedom.

Colossians 2:13–15 joins forgiveness with Christ's triumph over the powers through the cross. These passages extend the theme of the stronger Savior: His victory includes release from sin's debt and the fear of death, rather than ending with the outward restoration of one person.
