Passage

Luke 23:32-38

What kind of King remains on the cross?

Berean Standard Bible

32 Two others, who were criminals, were also led away to be executed with Jesus. 33 When they came to the place called The Skull, they crucified Him there, along with the criminals, one on His right and the other on His left. 34 Then Jesus said, “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing.” And they divided up His garments by casting lots.

35 The people stood watching, and the rulers sneered at Him, saying, “He saved others; let Him save Himself if He is the Christ of God, the Chosen One.” 36 The soldiers also mocked Him and came up to offer Him sour wine. 37 “If You are the King of the Jews,” they said, “save Yourself!” 38 Above Him was posted an inscription: THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS.

What is the context?

Jesus is led away with two criminals and crucified between them at the place called The Skull. The Greek word κρανίον, kranion (krah-NEE-on), means skull. Thayer lists its use here and in the other Gospel accounts of this place. (Thayer, Greek 2898)

He asks the Father to forgive those acting in ignorance while His garments are divided by casting lots. The BSB includes this prayer and notes that some manuscripts omit it. (BSB text and footnotes)

The rulers and soldiers challenge Him to save Himself if He is God’s Christ or the King of the Jews. Above Him, the inscription identifies Him as that King. Their challenge measures His identity by escape from the cross, while the account presents Him enduring it.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see the contrast between Jesus’ mercy and the mockery surrounding Him. Wesley notices that the rulers ridicule His claim as Messiah while the soldiers ridicule His kingship; His prayer seeks the good of the people doing Him harm. (Wesley on Luke 23:34–38)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus has already applied Isaiah’s description of the Servant counted among wrongdoers to His own coming suffering. Now He is executed between criminals. Isaiah also describes the Servant bearing others’ sins and interceding for wrongdoers, which provides a supported connection with the prayer offered here. (Luke 22:37; Isaiah 53:11–12)

The dividing of His garments recalls the suffering speaker in Psalm 22, whose clothing is divided by lot and whose trust in God is mocked. The psalm moves from distress toward God’s deliverance and worship among the nations. That larger movement helps place the humiliation within the hope of God’s saving work. (Psalm 22:7–8, 18, 22–31)

Hebrews explicitly connects Jesus’ suffering outside the city gate with the sin-offering bodies burned outside Israel’s camp. Leviticus distinguishes those sacrificed animals from the live goat that carries away the people’s sins. Both images help readers see the cost of cleansing and the removal of sin, while Hebrews itself makes the outside-the-gate connection. It then calls believers to go to Jesus and share His disgrace. (Hebrews 13:10–16; Leviticus 16:20–28)

What is our position?

Our study What Does Prayer Look Like? reads Jesus’ prayer for forgiveness as intercession entrusted to the Father. Jesus brings those responsible for His death before God in mercy. The lesson we draw is to pray for others while leaving the outcome with the Father. This position concerns the prayer in verse 34; it does not supply a separate conclusion on every detail of the crucifixion scene.
