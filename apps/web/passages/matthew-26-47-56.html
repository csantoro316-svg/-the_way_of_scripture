Passage

Matthew 26:47-56

The Arrest: Jesus Puts Away the Sword and Accepts the Cup

Berean Standard Bible

47 While Jesus was still speaking, Judas, one of the Twelve, arrived, accompanied by a large crowd armed with swords and clubs, sent from the chief priests and elders of the people. 48 Now the betrayer had arranged a signal with them: “The One I kiss is the man; arrest Him.” 49 Going directly to Jesus, he said, “Greetings, Rabbi!” and kissed Him. 50 “Friend,” Jesus replied, “do what you came for.” Then the men stepped forward, seized Jesus, and arrested Him.

51 At this, one of Jesus’ companions drew his sword and struck the servant of the high priest, cutting off his ear. 52 “Put your sword back in its place,” Jesus said to him. “For all who draw the sword will die by the sword. 53 Are you not aware that I can call on My Father, and He will at once put at My disposal more than twelve legions of angels? 54 But how then would the Scriptures be fulfilled that say it must happen this way?”

55 At that time Jesus said to the crowd, “Have you come out with swords and clubs to arrest Me as you would an outlaw? Every day I sat teaching in the temple courts, and you did not arrest Me. 56 But this has all happened so that the writings of the prophets would be fulfilled.” Then all the disciples deserted Him and fled.

What is the context?

Judas arrives with an armed crowd sent by the chief priests and elders. He has arranged a kiss as the signal identifying Jesus. The familiar greeting becomes an act of betrayal, and the men seize Him.

One of Jesus' companions draws a sword and cuts off the high priest's servant's ear. Matthew leaves both men unnamed; John 18:1–12 identifies them as Peter and Malchus. Luke 22:47–53 adds that Jesus stops the violence and heals the injured man.

Jesus orders the sword put away and warns about the outcome of taking up the sword. He says He could appeal to the Father for more than twelve legions of angels. His acceptance of arrest is therefore not presented as a lack of help or power: He connects it with the fulfillment of Scripture.

He also challenges the crowd's manner of arresting Him. He had taught openly in the temple courts, yet they have come armed as though He were an outlaw. Again He points to the prophets' writings being fulfilled. The disciples then desert Him and flee, just as He has warned them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly recognize that Jesus refuses an armed attempt to prevent His arrest and willingly accepts the path toward the cross. Churches differ over how this example relates to all uses of force by Christians.

Mennonite Church USA's Confession of Faith, article 22, directly cites Matthew 26:52–53 in teaching that followers of Jesus should resist evil without violence, including when facing warfare. It joins this with love of enemies, reconciliation, and active peacemaking.

The Catholic Catechism, 2263–2265, distinguishes wrongful killing from proportionate defense of oneself or those entrusted to one's care. This is a wider teaching about defense rather than permission to stop Jesus' saving work by force. The traditions differ about the reach of nonviolence, while both treat this particular attack as contrary to Jesus' direction.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus' words here fit His explanation of His mission in John 10:14–18: the shepherd knows His sheep and lays down His life willingly, with authority to take it up again. The arrest leads toward that self-giving rather than defeating it. Hebrews 2:14–18 explains the larger victory: through sharing human life and undergoing death, He breaks the devil's power and frees people held by fear of death.

The refusal of retaliation also has a place in the life of His people. Romans 12:17–21 calls believers to seek peace, leave vengeance to God, and answer an enemy's need with practical care. In Luke's account, Jesus' healing of the man wounded during His arrest supplies a concrete example of doing good amid hostility. His saving purpose and His treatment of an enemy belong together.
