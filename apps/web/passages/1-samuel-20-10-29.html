Passage

1 Samuel 20:10-29

What does Jonathan ask David to remember beyond this crisis?

Berean Standard Bible

10 Then David asked Jonathan, “Who will tell me if your father answers you harshly?” 11 “Come,” he replied, “let us go out to the field.” So the two of them went out into the field, 12 and Jonathan said, “By the LORD, the God of Israel, I will sound out my father by this time tomorrow or the next day. If he is favorable toward you, will I not send for you and tell you? 13 But if my father intends to bring evil on you, then may the LORD punish me, and ever so severely, if I do not tell you and send you on your way in safety. May the LORD be with you, just as He has been with my father. 14 And as long as I live, treat me with the LORD’s loving devotion, that I may not die, 15 and do not ever cut off your loving devotion from my household—not even when the LORD cuts off every one of David’s enemies from the face of the earth.” 16 So Jonathan made a covenant with the house of David, saying, “May the LORD hold David’s enemies accountable.” 17 And Jonathan had David reaffirm his vow out of love for him, for Jonathan loved David as he loved himself.

18 Then Jonathan said to David, “Tomorrow is the New Moon, and you will be missed, because your seat will be empty. 19 When you have stayed three days, hurry down to the place you hid on the day this trouble began, and remain beside the stone Ezel. 20 I will shoot three arrows to the side of it, as if I were aiming at a target. 21 Then I will send a boy and say, ‘Go, find the arrows!’ Now, if I expressly say to him, ‘Look, the arrows are on this side of you; bring them,’ then come, because as surely as the LORD lives, it is safe for you, and there is no danger. 22 But if I say to the young man, ‘Look, the arrows are beyond you,’ then you must go, for the LORD has sent you away. 23 And as for the matter you and I have discussed, the LORD is a witness between you and me forever.”

24 So David hid in the field, and when the New Moon had come, the king sat down to eat. 25 He sat in his usual place by the wall, opposite Jonathan and beside Abner, but David’s place was empty. 26 Saul said nothing that day because he thought, “Something has happened to David to make him ceremonially unclean—surely he is unclean.” 27 But on the day after the New Moon, the second day, David’s place was still empty, and Saul asked his son Jonathan, “Why hasn’t the son of Jesse come to the meal either yesterday or today?” 28 Jonathan answered, “David urgently requested my permission to go to Bethlehem, 29 saying, ‘Please let me go, because our clan is holding a sacrifice in the city, and my brother has told me to be there. So now, if I have found favor in your eyes, please let me go and see my brothers.’ That is why he has not come to the king’s table.”

What is the context?

David asks how he will learn Saul’s answer if it is dangerous for Jonathan to approach him openly. They go into the field, where Jonathan promises before the LORD to investigate and send him away safely if necessary.

Jonathan also asks something for the future: David must continue showing kindness to him and his household, even after David’s enemies are defeated. Their promise now expressly reaches beyond the two friends to their families. Jonathan looks toward David’s future while asking that his own family not be forgotten.

The word translated loving devotion is hesed (KHEH-sed). Brown–Driver–Briggs gives kindness and goodness, and specifically includes these verses among acts of kindness between people. Here the covenant gives that kindness a lasting shape: it must continue when the immediate need has passed.

Jonathan arranges an arrow signal. Arrows on the near side will mean safety; arrows beyond the boy will mean David must leave. At the meal, David’s seat is empty. Saul first assumes he is ceremonially unclean, but on the second day he asks where he is. Jonathan gives the explanation about Bethlehem. Saul’s response comes in the next passage.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill see the covenant extending from the two friends to their households. Their emphasis is on promises that continue beyond a change in power. Jonathan is doing more than securing an immediate escape for David; he asks for kindness that will protect his family’s future.

Gill explains the LORD’s kindness as kindness pleasing to God, modeled on His care and promised before Him. The language holds affection and faithfulness together. It does not reduce the agreement to a useful bargain that either friend can forget once he no longer needs help.

Henry sees Jonathan’s loyalty as love overcoming ambition and self-interest. His future might seem to depend on David failing, yet he binds his family’s hopes to friendship with David. The LORD is repeatedly named as witness, giving the promises a seriousness beyond private convenience.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise bears fruit in 2 Samuel 9:1–13. David seeks someone from Saul’s household to whom he can show kindness for Jonathan’s sake. He restores land to Jonathan’s son Mephibosheth and welcomes him to eat at his table like one of his own sons. A promise made while David was in danger becomes lasting care when he has power.

There is a helpful parallel with God’s gracious welcome in Ephesians 2:1–10. God makes people alive with Christ and displays His kindness through Him; salvation is His gift, rather than something earned by works. David’s care gives us a human picture of kindness received because of another, while God’s grace in Christ reaches further than any human king’s generosity.

This comparison does not make Mephibosheth’s disability a symbol of personal sin or make every part of the story a hidden code. It draws attention to the welcome itself: faithful love makes room at the table for someone who did not win it through achievement.
