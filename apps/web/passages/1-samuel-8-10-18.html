Passage

1 Samuel 8:10-18

What will the king take from the people?

Berean Standard Bible

10 So Samuel spoke all the words of the LORD to the people who were asking him for a king. 11 He said, “This will be the manner of the king who will reign over you: He will take your sons and appoint them to serve his own chariots and horses, and to run in front of his chariots. 12 He will appoint some for himself as commanders of thousands and of fifties, and others to plow his ground, to reap his harvest, and to make his weapons of war and equipment for his chariots. 13 And he will take your daughters to be perfumers, cooks, and bakers.

14 He will take the best of your fields and vineyards and olive groves and give them to his servants. 15 He will take a tenth of your grain and grape harvest and give it to his officials and servants. 16 And he will take your menservants and maidservants and your best cattle and donkeys and put them to his own use. 17 He will take a tenth of your flocks, and you yourselves will become his slaves. 18 When that day comes, you will beg for relief from the king you have chosen, but the LORD will not answer you on that day.”

What is the context?

Samuel passes on the warning God has given. Its repeated action is taking: the king will take sons for military service and work, daughters for his household, productive land for his servants, and portions of the harvest and flocks. People who hope for a protector will also bear the cost of his power.

The warning moves from particular demands to the people themselves becoming his slaves. The threat is larger than an inconvenient change in administration. Their households, work and possessions will be drawn into serving the ruler they have chosen.

The final warning anticipates their later cry for relief. God says He will not answer them on that day. The words address the consequences of this choice; they should not be detached from the setting and turned into a claim that God never hears anyone who has made a wrong request.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the warning as describing oppressive demands made for the king’s own advantage. Gill distinguishes the king’s assumed practices from a divine grant of unlimited rights. In this reading, predicting what rulers will do does not make every act of taking morally lawful.

Henry emphasizes the burden of the government the people imagine will solve their problems. He also draws a moral comparison with the harsh mastery of sinful desires. That application extends the warning; the direct subject remains the human king’s claims on Israel’s families, labor and property.

These readings fit the restraint required of the king in Deuteronomy 17:16–20: he must not accumulate power and wealth without limit or consider himself above his fellow Israelites. Samuel’s warning and that instruction should be considered together.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The ruler who keeps taking provides a strong contrast with Jesus’ account of His own mission. In Mark 10:42–45, Jesus describes rulers who dominate others and tells His disciples to pursue service instead. He gives His own life as a ransom for many.

This is a comparison between ways of exercising authority. Samuel warns of people being used to sustain a ruler; Jesus gives Himself for the people He serves. The New Testament directs the hope of faithful leadership toward His self-giving work, while also shaping the conduct of those who follow Him.
