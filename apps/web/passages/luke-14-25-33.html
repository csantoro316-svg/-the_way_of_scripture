Passage

Luke 14:25-33

Why does Jesus tell the crowd to count the cost of following Him?

Berean Standard Bible

25 Now large crowds were traveling with Jesus, and He turned and said to them, 26 “If anyone comes to Me and does not hate his father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters—yes, even his own life—he cannot be My disciple. 27 And whoever does not carry his cross and follow Me cannot be My disciple.

28 Which of you, wishing to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost to see if he has the resources to complete it? 29 Otherwise, if he lays the foundation and is unable to finish the work, everyone who sees it will ridicule him, 30 saying, ‘This man could not finish what he started to build.’

31 Or what king on his way to war with another king will not first sit down and consider whether he can engage with ten thousand men the one coming against him with twenty thousand? 32 And if he is unable, he will send a delegation while the other king is still far off, to ask for terms of peace. 33 In the same way, any one of you who does not give up everything he has cannot be My disciple.

What is the context?

Large crowds are traveling with Jesus when He turns and speaks about the demands of following Him. His words reach family relationships, one’s own life, carrying the cross and giving up possessions.

Two pictures call for honest consideration. A builder must know whether he can finish a tower before laying its foundation. A king must consider whether his army can face a greater force and seek terms if it cannot.

Jesus ends by applying the point to the hearers: someone who does not give up everything he has cannot be His disciple. The demand is not left at admiring His teaching from within a crowd. It reaches the claims and attachments that could stand above Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly understand the passage as a call to put Christ above every competing attachment. Wesley reads the family language by comparison with loyalty to Christ, and the Catholic bishops’ notes likewise understand family and possessions as unable to displace the commitment He requires. (Wesley on Luke 14:26–33; USCCB notes on Luke 14:25–33)

Luke uses the strong Greek verb μισέω, miseō (mih-SEH-oh), meaning to hate. Matthew gives the parallel demand in terms of loving family more than Jesus. The readings above understand Luke’s language through that comparison while retaining the real cost of putting Him first. (Thayer, Greek 3404; Matthew 10:37–39)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Paul describes counting former gains as loss for the sake of knowing Christ, sharing His sufferings and pressing toward the hope of resurrection. His own account gives a concrete example of a life whose values have been reordered around the Lord. (Philippians 3:7–14)

Hebrews calls believers to lay aside what entangles them and run with endurance while looking to Jesus. He endured the cross for the joy before Him. The cost of following belongs within His own path through suffering toward glory, rather than being an isolated test of determination. (Hebrews 12:1–3)
