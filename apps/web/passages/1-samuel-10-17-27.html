Passage

1 Samuel 10:17-27

How does Saul’s private calling become a public appointment?

Berean Standard Bible

17 After this, Samuel summoned the people to the LORD at Mizpah 18 and said to the Israelites, “This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: ‘I brought Israel up out of Egypt, and I rescued you from the hands of the Egyptians and of all the kingdoms that oppressed you.’ 19 But today you have rejected your God, who saves you from all your troubles and afflictions, and you have said to Him, ‘No, set a king over us.’ Now therefore present yourselves before the LORD by your tribes and clans.”

20 Thus Samuel had all the tribes of Israel come forward, and the tribe of Benjamin was selected. 21 Then he had the tribe of Benjamin come forward by its clans, and the clan of Matri was selected. Finally, Saul son of Kish was selected. But when they looked for him, they could not find him. 22 So again they inquired of the LORD, “Has the man come here yet?” And the LORD replied, “Behold, he has hidden himself among the baggage.” 23 So they ran and brought Saul, and when he stood among the people, he was a head taller than any of the others. 24 Samuel said to all the people, “Do you see the one the LORD has chosen? There is no one like him among all the people.” And all the people shouted, “Long live the king!”

25 Then Samuel explained to the people the rights of kingship. He wrote them on a scroll and laid it up before the LORD. And Samuel sent all the people away, each to his own home. 26 Saul also went to his home in Gibeah, and the men of valor whose hearts God had touched went with him. 27 But some worthless men said, “How can this man save us?” So they despised him and brought him no gifts; but Saul remained silent about it.

What is the context?

Samuel gathers Israel at Mizpah and first recalls God’s rescue of His people. The appointment does not erase the rebuke: in demanding a king, they have rejected the God who saves them from their troubles. Both the warning and the selection remain part of this public event.

The selection narrows from Benjamin to the clan of Matri, then to Saul. When he cannot be found, the people inquire of the LORD and learn that he is hiding among the baggage. They bring him forward, Samuel identifies him as the LORD’s chosen man, and the people acclaim him as king.

Samuel explains the rights of kingship, writes them on a scroll, and places it before the LORD. The exact document is not reproduced here. The scene presents an ordered appointment with stated responsibilities, rather than merely a celebration of Saul’s imposing height.

Saul returns to Gibeah with men whose hearts God has moved to support him. Others despise him and question whether he can save them. Saul remains silent. His public appointment therefore begins with support and resistance side by side.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the public selection as confirmation of God’s choice that should prevent disputes about Samuel choosing for himself. He reads Saul’s hiding as a sense of unworthiness. Henry also sees reluctance to assume power, while noting that awareness of inadequacy can go too far when someone has been called to a task.

On the written rights of kingship, Wesley connects the rules with the restraints in Deuteronomy 17. Gill discusses the view that Samuel is repeating the abuses warned of in chapter 8, but prefers a distinction: this concerns how the kingdom is to be governed and the duties involved. The document’s full contents are not available in this passage, so these explanations should not be treated as a recovered transcript.

Henry makes a Christian comparison between the different responses to Saul and the different responses to Jesus. Wesley also compares Saul’s restraint with Christ’s silence under opposition. These applications concern particular features of the scene, not a claim that every later act of Saul resembles Christ.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Henry’s comparison can be considered alongside John 1:10–13, which describes the world failing to recognize the Word and some receiving Him through faith. The shared theme is a division in response to one whom God has sent; the New Testament makes claims about Jesus that go far beyond Saul’s appointment.

Philippians 2:5–11 directs the larger royal hope toward Jesus’ obedience, death and exaltation, ending with every knee bowing and every tongue confessing Him as Lord. Saul receives a disputed beginning to an earthly reign. Jesus’ final honor rests on the saving path of humble obedience described in that passage.
