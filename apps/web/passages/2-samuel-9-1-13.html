Passage

2 Samuel 9:1-13

Why does David welcome Jonathan’s son to his table?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then David asked, “Is there anyone left from the house of Saul to whom I can show kindness for the sake of Jonathan?” 2 And there was a servant of the house of Saul named Ziba. They summoned him to David, and the king inquired, “Are you Ziba?” “I am your servant,” he replied. 3 So the king asked, “Is there anyone left of the house of Saul to whom I can show the kindness of God?” Ziba answered, “There is still Jonathan’s son, who is lame in both feet.” 4 “Where is he?” replied the king. And Ziba said, “Indeed, he is in Lo-debar at the house of Machir son of Ammiel.”

5 So King David had him brought from the house of Machir son of Ammiel in Lo-debar. 6 And when Mephibosheth son of Jonathan, the son of Saul, came to David, he fell facedown in reverence. Then David said, “Mephibosheth!” “I am your servant,” he replied. 7 “Do not be afraid,” said David, “for surely I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan. I will restore to you all the land of your grandfather Saul, and you will always eat at my table.” 8 Mephibosheth bowed down and said, “What is your servant, that you should show regard for a dead dog like me?”

9 Then the king summoned Saul’s servant Ziba and said to him, “I have given to your master’s grandson all that belonged to Saul and to all his house. 10 You and your sons and servants are to work the ground for him and bring in the harvest, so that your master’s grandson may have food to eat. But Mephibosheth, your master’s grandson, is always to eat at my table.” Now Ziba had fifteen sons and twenty servants. 11 And Ziba said to the king, “Your servant will do all that my lord the king has commanded.” So Mephibosheth ate at David’s table like one of the king’s own sons. 12 And Mephibosheth had a young son named Mica, and all who dwelt in the house of Ziba were servants of Mephibosheth. 13 So Mephibosheth lived in Jerusalem, because he always ate at the king’s table, and he was lame in both feet.

What is the context?

David asks whether anyone remains from Saul’s house to whom he can show kindness for Jonathan’s sake. Ziba, a servant of Saul’s household, tells him about Jonathan’s son Mephibosheth, who is living with Machir in Lo-debar and is lame in both feet.

David has Mephibosheth brought to him, calls him by name and tells him not to fear. He promises to restore Saul’s land and give him a continuing place at the royal table. Mephibosheth responds with astonished humility, referring to himself as a dead dog. Those are his words about himself, not a judgment the narrator asks us to make about his worth.

David assigns Ziba, his fifteen sons and twenty servants to cultivate the land. Mephibosheth lives in Jerusalem and eats at the king’s table like one of David’s own sons. His young son Mica is also mentioned. The chapter ends by again noting his disability; his welcome has not depended on its removal.

The word translated kindness is hesed (KHEH-sed). Brown–Driver–Briggs includes kindness expressed in favors and benefits, and specifically lists this chapter. Here it takes practical form: property, provision and a place at the table.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads David’s action as faithfulness to his friendship and promises to Jonathan. Gill also connects it with the pledge not to cut off kindness from Jonathan’s household. That promise is recorded in 1 Samuel 20:12–17. The passage presents David seeking an opportunity for kindness rather than a reason to eliminate a rival.

Gill understands eating like one of the king’s sons as a high honor and a continuing expression of friendship. The land’s produce supports Mephibosheth’s household even while he regularly eats with David. Both provision and personal welcome matter.

His disability is stated plainly, but the account does not equate it with guilt, uselessness or a lesser human value. David’s kindness is tied to Jonathan and the promise he made, not to a test of Mephibosheth’s physical ability.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Henry draws a limited comparison between David’s kindness and the kindness of God our Savior. Mephibosheth does not arrive with an achievement that purchases his place. The king takes the initiative and gives him a welcome rooted in a relationship and promise that came before this meeting.

Ephesians 2:4–10 describes God’s mercy and kindness in Christ: salvation is His gift, received through faith rather than earned by works, and it leads into the good works He prepares. The connection is the generosity of the giver, not a claim that a physical disability represents sin.

The table also offers a meaningful parallel of welcome. In Luke 22:28–30, Jesus promises His faithful disciples a place at His table in His kingdom. That is a different promise in a different setting, but both scenes help us see the honor of being received by the king.
