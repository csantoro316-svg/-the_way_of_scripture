Passage

Matthew 26:39

Not as I Will, but as You Will

Gethsemane and surrendered faith: not as I will, but as You will.

Berean Standard Bible

39 Going a little farther, He fell facedown and prayed, “My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me. Yet not as I will, but as You will.”

What is the context?

This verse belongs inside Jesus' prayer in Gethsemane, after He has spoken of His overwhelming sorrow and before His arrest. Matthew 26:36–46 gives the full scene, including the disciples' sleep and Jesus' three periods of prayer.

Falling facedown, Jesus addresses the Father and asks whether the cup can pass from Him. He immediately places that desire under the Father's will. The prayer expresses both the reality of the suffering He faces and the obedience with which He faces it.

Cup is ποτήριον, potērion (poh-TAY-ree-on). Thayer's lexicon explains its figurative use here as the suffering Christ must undergo. John 18:11 returns to the same picture when Jesus speaks of drinking the cup given by the Father.

The surrounding verses matter: Jesus prays again and again, always accepting the Father's will, then rises to meet His betrayer. This one sentence is not a promise that the right wording will remove every trial, or a rebuke to someone who feels genuine fear or grief.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as real human anguish joined with Jesus' willing obedience, rather than either pretend suffering or sinful rebellion. The Catholic Catechism, 612, and the Westminster Confession, 8.4, both affirm His willing acceptance of suffering for our salvation.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 5:7–10 connects Jesus' earthly prayers, suffering, obedience, and saving work. His tears are part of His real human life, and the passage presents Him as the source of eternal salvation. The cross that He approaches is more than an example of courage; it belongs to what He does for those He saves.

That saving work also opens a way for weak people to approach God. Hebrews 4:14–16 describes Jesus as the high priest who understands weakness without sin and directs His people to seek mercy and grace in their need. His surrender in suffering stands alongside His continuing care for those who come through Him.

What is our position?

What Does Prayer Look Like? reads Gethsemane as a model of honest surrender. Jesus brings the real cost of His suffering before the Father and places the outcome entirely in His hands. Expressing grief is not treated as a failure of faith.

The study also emphasizes that Jesus prays three times. Continued asking can belong with surrender when the Father's will governs it. Prayer and Faith likewise reads this scene as prayer rooted in trust and submission to God, rather than a way to secure the outcome we choose. These studies supply our position on prayer here; they do not make every detail of Jesus' suffering a claim about what a believer will experience.
