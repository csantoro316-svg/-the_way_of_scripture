Passage

Matthew 26:31-35

The Shepherd Will Be Struck, but He Will Meet Them Again

Berean Standard Bible

31 Then Jesus said to them, “This very night you will all fall away on account of Me. For it is written: ‘I will strike the Shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered.’ 32 But after I have risen, I will go ahead of you into Galilee.”

33 Peter said to Him, “Even if all fall away on account of You, I never will.” 34 “Truly I tell you,” Jesus declared, “this very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny Me three times.” 35 Peter replied, “Even if I have to die with You, I will never deny You.” And all the other disciples said the same thing.

What is the context?

On the way to the Mount of Olives, Jesus tells His disciples that they will all fall away because of Him that very night. He supports the warning with Zechariah's picture of a shepherd struck and sheep scattered. He then immediately promises that after His resurrection He will go ahead of them into Galilee.

Peter insists that even if everyone else falls away, he never will. Jesus answers with a specific warning: before the rooster crows that night, Peter will deny Him three times. Peter replies that he would die with Jesus rather than deny Him, and the other disciples say the same.

Matthew will show the disciples fleeing at the arrest and Peter denying Jesus in the courtyard. Their declarations of loyalty are sincere-sounding, but do not show how they will act under pressure. Jesus knows the coming failure and speaks of a meeting beyond it.

Luke 22:28–34 adds Jesus' prayer for Peter's faith and His instruction that, after turning back, Peter should strengthen his brothers. This supplies another side of the warning: Jesus addresses both the danger and the return.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readers commonly see a warning against overconfidence together with hope in Jesus' promised meeting after the resurrection. Wesley on 26:31–32 stresses that their forsaking Him will not make Him forsake them.

Churches differ in how they relate a believer's serious failure to salvation. The Westminster Confession, 17, and 1689 Baptist Confession, 17, teach that believers may fall into grievous sins but will finally persevere through God's preserving grace. Peter's promised return fits that understanding of failure followed by restoration.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, XII, allow departure from grace and restoration through repentance. Catholic teaching also says faith can be lost and urges perseverance (Catechism, 162). These traditions also recognize forgiveness and restoration; they do not teach that every lapse leaves a person beyond mercy. The different explanations should not obscure what Jesus promises these disciples here.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus explicitly applies Zechariah 13:7 to the coming scattering. The shepherd's suffering does not end with a permanently abandoned flock: He also announces His resurrection and their meeting in Galilee. God's purpose includes bringing the scattered disciples back together after the cross.

That hope takes personal shape in John 21:15–19, where the risen Jesus calls Peter to care for His sheep and follow Him. 1 Peter 1:3–9 later describes new birth into a living hope through Jesus' resurrection, an enduring inheritance, and God's protection through faith amid real trials. These connections retain the comfort of Christ's care while taking both failure and faithful response seriously.
