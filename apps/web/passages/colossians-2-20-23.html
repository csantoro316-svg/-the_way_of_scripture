Passage

Colossians 2:20-23

Can strict rules give us a changed heart?

Berean Standard Bible

20 If you have died with Christ to the spiritual forces of the world, why, as though you still belonged to the world, do you submit to its regulations: 21 “Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch!”? 22 These will all perish with use, because they are based on human commands and teachings. 23 Such restrictions indeed have an appearance of wisdom, with their self-prescribed worship, their false humility, and their harsh treatment of the body; but they are of no value against the indulgence of the flesh.

What is the context?

Paul carries forward the believers’ death with Christ. If they have died with Him to the spiritual forces of the world, why submit as though they still belong to that world?

He names restrictive commands about handling, tasting and touching. Their objects are used up and perish, and the regulations rest on human commands and teachings.

Such practices can look wise and humble. Yet Paul says that self-made worship and harsh treatment of the body do not overcome the indulgence of the flesh. An impressive appearance of discipline is not the same as the inward change he seeks.

The next chapter supplies the positive direction: seek the things above with Christ and put away the old way of life. Paul’s criticism of these restrictions leads toward a changed life, not indifference to how people live.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read these verses as a warning that outward severity cannot produce the holy life it claims to achieve. Wesley’s notes on verses 20–23 describe the danger of religious rules that feed self-will and pride even while appearing to restrain them. The warning does not turn every practice of fasting or self-discipline into the condemned system; it concerns the commands and claims Paul is addressing.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Galatians 5:13–26 gives the positive counterpart: freedom serves love, and life by the Spirit produces fruit including patience, gentleness and self-control. The change God seeks grows from His Spirit’s work rather than the appearance of strictness.

That life belongs within the new humanity described in Colossians 3:9–11, renewed in the Creator’s image and centered on Christ across old divisions. God’s purpose reaches beyond a list of forbidden objects to a people made new in Him.
