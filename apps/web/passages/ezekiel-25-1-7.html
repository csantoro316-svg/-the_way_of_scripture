Passage

Ezekiel 25:1-7

Why does Ammon answer for celebrating Judah’s ruin?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, set your face against the Ammonites and prophesy against them. 3 Tell the Ammonites to hear the word of the Lord GOD, for this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Because you exclaimed, “Aha!” when My sanctuary was profaned, when the land of Israel was laid waste, and when the house of Judah went into exile, 4 therefore I will indeed give you as a possession to the people of the East. They will set up their camps and pitch their tents among you. They will eat your fruit and drink your milk. 5 I will make Rabbah a pasture for camels, and Ammon a resting place for sheep. Then you will know that I am the LORD.’

6 For this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Because you clapped your hands and stomped your feet and rejoiced over the land of Israel with a heart full of contempt, 7 therefore I will indeed stretch out My hand against you and give you as plunder to the nations. I will cut you off from the peoples and exterminate you from the countries. I will destroy you, and you will know that I am the LORD.’

What is the context?

The focus turns from Jerusalem to its neighbors. Ammon is accused of rejoicing when the Lord’s sanctuary is profaned, the land is devastated and Judah goes into exile. The clapping and stamping express contempt rather than compassion.

The Lord announces a reversal. Ammon will become plunder, and people from the East will occupy its land and consume its produce. Rabbah will become pasture for camels, and the land a resting place for sheep.

The judgment against Judah does not make delight in its destruction acceptable. The Lord who confronts His own people also holds their neighbors accountable. Ammon too will know that He is the Lord.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley understand the charge as gloating over a calamity that should have prompted pity. The warning does not excuse Judah’s sins; it confronts the contempt of those watching Judah suffer.

The USCCB notes identify the people from the East as desert nomads. Wesley connects Arabian groups with Nebuchadnezzar’s campaign. These explanations fill out the broad description differently; Ezekiel’s announcement itself names people from the East.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The wider promise to Abraham includes the Lord’s response to those who curse him, while also looking toward blessing for all families of the earth: Genesis 12:1–3. Deuteronomy 30:1–10 joins Israel’s restoration and changed hearts with judgment on its persecutors.

There is also a promise concerning Ammon itself. After announcing its judgment, Jeremiah 49:1–6 says that the Lord will restore the Ammonites from captivity. Reading the passages together keeps both accountability and mercy in view. Ezekiel’s warning does not supply the whole account of everything God will do for these people.
