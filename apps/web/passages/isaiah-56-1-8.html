Passage

Isaiah 56:1-8

Is there a place for people who fear they will be excluded?

Berean Standard Bible

1 This is what the LORD says: “Maintain justice and do what is right, for My salvation is coming soon, and My righteousness will be revealed. 2 Blessed is the man who does this, and the son of man who holds it fast, who keeps the Sabbath without profaning it and keeps his hand from doing any evil.”

3 Let no foreigner who has joined himself to the LORD say, “The LORD will utterly exclude me from His people.” And let the eunuch not say, “I am but a dry tree.” 4 For this is what the LORD says: “To the eunuchs who keep My Sabbaths, who choose what pleases Me and hold fast to My covenant— 5 I will give them, in My house and within My walls, a memorial and a name better than that of sons and daughters. I will give them an everlasting name that will not be cut off.

6 And the foreigners who join themselves to the LORD to minister to Him, to love the name of the LORD, and to be His servants— all who keep the Sabbath without profaning it and who hold fast to My covenant— 7 I will bring them to My holy mountain and make them joyful in My house of prayer. Their burnt offerings and sacrifices will be accepted on My altar, for My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations.” 8 Thus declares the Lord GOD, who gathers the dispersed of Israel: “I will gather to them still others besides those already gathered.”

What is the context?

The approaching salvation of God calls for justice and right conduct. Keeping the Sabbath and turning away from evil are named as marks of faithfulness.

The Lord then addresses two fears. A foreigner who has joined himself to Him must not assume he will be excluded, and a eunuch must not describe himself as a dry tree with no future. Those who choose what pleases God and hold to His covenant are promised a lasting name in His house, better than sons and daughters.

The foreigners who love and serve the Lord are promised welcome on His holy mountain and joy in His house of prayer. Their offerings will be accepted. His house is to be a house of prayer for all nations.

The One who gathers Israel’s dispersed people declares that He will gather others as well. The repeated emphasis is on belonging to the Lord and receiving a place through His promise.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley emphasize the welcome offered to people who fear that their background or condition leaves them outside. They connect the gathering of foreigners with Gentiles sharing in God’s people.

Their explanations of Sabbath language have different emphases. Henry stresses the duty of keeping a holy day; Wesley treats Sabbath observance here as standing for the worship of God more broadly. Both join the promise of acceptance with faithful service rather than treating belonging as merely a matter of ancestry.

The enduring name answers the eunuch’s fear of having no lasting place. The promise gives a future that does not depend on having biological descendants.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus directly quotes the description of a house of prayer for all nations in Mark 11:15–18, as He challenges what is happening in the temple courts. His appeal brings Isaiah’s stated purpose for God’s house into His own teaching and action.

Ephesians 2:11–22 develops the larger theme of welcome: Gentiles once far away are brought near through Christ’s blood. Jewish and Gentile believers have access to the Father by one Spirit and belong to one household.

The Ethiopian eunuch’s baptism in Acts 8:34–40 is a fitting parallel with the promise of welcome. He hears about Jesus from Isaiah, receives baptism and goes on his way rejoicing. Acts does not explicitly quote Isaiah 56 or state that this chapter prompted his question about baptism; the connection is a parallel within the wider story of God gathering people.
