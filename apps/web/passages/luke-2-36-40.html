Passage

Luke 2:36-40

How does Anna respond to the promised redemption?

Berean Standard Bible

36 There was also a prophetess named Anna, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher, who was well along in years. She had been married for seven years, 37 and then was a widow to the age of eighty-four. She never left the temple, but worshiped night and day, fasting and praying. 38 Coming forward at that moment, she gave thanks to God and spoke about the Child to all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem.

39 When Jesus’ parents had done everything required by the Law of the Lord, they returned to Galilee, to their own town of Nazareth. 40 And the Child grew and became strong. He was filled with wisdom, and the grace of God was upon Him.

What is the context?

Anna is introduced as a prophetess, a woman who speaks for God, from the tribe of Asher and well along in years. Luke describes her life of worship, fasting, and prayer at the temple. Her devotion places her among the people waiting for God’s promised help.

She comes forward, thanks God, and speaks about the child to those waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem. Alongside Simeon, she gives public testimony that this child belongs to the hope they have been awaiting.

After the family completes the Law’s requirements, Luke takes the account back to Nazareth. He summarizes Jesus’ growth in strength and wisdom under God’s grace. His story includes both the remarkable recognition at the temple and an ordinary human childhood.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian traditions commonly see Anna as a faithful witness who responds to the coming of the Messiah with thanks and testimony. The return to Nazareth and the child’s growth continue Luke’s presentation of Jesus as truly sharing human life. (USCCB notes on Luke 2:36–40; Catechism 531)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Anna belongs to the scriptural pattern of women bearing witness to God’s work. At Pentecost, Peter explains the Spirit’s outpouring through Joel’s promise that sons and daughters will prophesy. Anna’s earlier witness does not depend on being part of the later Pentecost event; the connection is the broader purpose of God making His work known through people He calls. (Acts 2:16–18)

There is also a helpful parallel in faithful waiting. Anna looks for Jerusalem’s redemption, while Paul describes believers waiting with hope and patience for the redemption of their bodies. These are distinct moments in the saving story, but both direct hope toward what God has promised rather than treating the waiting as empty time. (Romans 8:22–25)
