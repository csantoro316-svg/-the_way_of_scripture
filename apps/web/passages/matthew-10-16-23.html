Passage

Matthew 10:16-23

Facing Opposition with Wisdom and Faith

Berean Standard Bible

16 Behold, I am sending you out like sheep among wolves; therefore be as shrewd as snakes and as innocent as doves. 17 But beware of men, for they will hand you over to their councils and flog you in their synagogues. 18 On My account you will be brought before governors and kings as witnesses to them and to the Gentiles.

19 But when they hand you over, do not worry about how to respond or what to say. In that hour you will be given what to say. 20 For it will not be you speaking, but the Spirit of your Father speaking through you.

21 Brother will betray brother to death, and a father his child; children will rise against their parents and have them put to death. 22 You will be hated by everyone because of My name, but the one who perseveres to the end will be saved. 23 When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next. Truly I tell you, you will not reach all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes.

What is the context?

Jesus prepares His messengers for opposition. The picture changes from a harvest needing workers to sheep surrounded by wolves. He does not tell them to become like the threat around them. They are to combine practical wisdom with innocence as they carry out His mission.

The word for shrewd is φρόνιμος, phronimos (FROH-nee-mos), prudent or sensible. The word for innocent is ἀκέραιος, akeraios (ah-KEH-rye-os), free from evil or deceit. Thayer's entries for the first word and the second include this verse. The two instructions belong together: care and discernment must be joined with integrity.

The dangers are concrete. Jesus names councils, flogging, governors, kings, and betrayal within families. Yet He also describes those appearances before rulers as opportunities for testimony, even to Gentiles. Their message will reach beyond the setting of the first journey to Israel.

His promise about words to speak addresses being handed over for trial. The Spirit of their Father will help them in that hour. They are not left to carry the witness in their own strength. The promise concerns this moment of pressure; it is not a direction to stop learning His teaching.

The call to persevere comes alongside the warning that witnesses may be put to death. Being saved therefore cannot be reduced to a guarantee that every faithful witness will avoid death. Jesus also tells them to flee to another town when persecuted. Continuing the mission can involve leaving a dangerous place.

He closes with the saying that they will not reach all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes. Christian interpreters differ over the meaning and timing of that coming, as well as how to explain the promise joined to perseverance.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand this as a call to remain faithful to Jesus under opposition, relying on the Spirit while acting with wisdom and integrity. Traditions differ over whether a believer can finally turn away from Christ.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 17.1–3, and the 1689 Baptist Confession, 17.1–3, teach that those God has brought into saving faith will not finally be lost: God preserves them, even though they may fall into serious sins for a time.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, XII, say that those who have received the Holy Spirit may depart from grace and fall into sin, yet by God's grace return and change how they live. The Catholic Catechism, 161–162, likewise calls faith a free gift that can be lost and urges believers to keep growing in faith and remain with Christ.

Verse 23 also receives different readings. Wesley on Matthew 10:23 understands the coming as judgment on the temple and nation. C. I. Scofield's 1917 note on 10:16–23 looks ahead to a faithful group in Israel witnessing during a final period of distress before Christ's return. The US Catholic bishops' note on 10:23 considers connections with the risen Jesus' mission or judgment on Jerusalem while acknowledging the difficulty of the saying. These commentators' readings should not be treated as a single view held by everyone in their traditions.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts shows the promised witness continuing under pressure. In Acts 4:8–13, Peter speaks before the authorities while filled with the Holy Spirit, announcing Jesus' resurrection and the salvation found in Him. The trial becomes a setting for the message of life through Christ.

Acts 8:1–4 describes believers scattered by persecution continuing to proclaim the word. The apostles remain in Jerusalem at that point, while others carry the message into new places. Opposition does not have the final say over God's purpose to make Christ known.
