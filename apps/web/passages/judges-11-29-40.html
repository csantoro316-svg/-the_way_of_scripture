Passage

Judges 11:29-40

What happens after Jephthah makes his vow?

Berean Standard Bible

29 Then the Spirit of the LORD came upon Jephthah, and he passed through Gilead and Manasseh, then through Mizpah of Gilead. And from there he advanced against the Ammonites. 30 Jephthah made this vow to the LORD: “If indeed You will deliver the Ammonites into my hand, 31 then whatever comes out the door of my house to greet me on my triumphant return from the Ammonites will belong to the LORD, and I will offer it up as a burnt offering.” 32 So Jephthah crossed over to the Ammonites to fight against them, and the LORD delivered them into his hand. 33 With a great blow he devastated twenty cities from Aroer to the vicinity of Minnith, as far as Abel-keramim. So the Ammonites were subdued before the Israelites.

34 And when Jephthah returned home to Mizpah, there was his daughter coming out to meet him with tambourines and dancing! She was his only child; he had no son or daughter besides her. 35 As soon as Jephthah saw her, he tore his clothes and said, “No! Not my daughter! You have brought me to my knees! You have brought great misery upon me, for I have given my word to the LORD and cannot take it back.” 36 “My father,” she replied, “you have given your word to the LORD. Do to me as you have said, for the LORD has avenged you of your enemies, the Ammonites.”

37 She also said to her father, “Let me do this one thing: Let me wander for two months through the mountains with my friends and mourn my virginity.” 38 “Go,” he said. And he sent her away for two months. So she left with her friends and mourned her virginity upon the mountains. 39 After two months, she returned to her father, and he did to her as he had vowed. And she had never had relations with a man. So it has become a custom in Israel 40 that each year the young women of Israel go out for four days to lament the daughter of Jephthah the Gileadite.

What is the context?

The Spirit of the LORD comes upon Jephthah before the vow is made. He then promises that, if God gives victory, whatever comes from his house to greet him will belong to the LORD and be offered as a burnt offering. The Hebrew עֹלָה, olah (oh-LAH), is the usual word for a whole burnt offering, as explained by Brown–Driver–Briggs. The LORD gives victory over Ammon, but the account records no divine command to make this vow.

When Jephthah returns, his only child comes out with tambourines and dancing. He tears his clothes in distress and tells her of his promise. She asks for two months with her friends to mourn her virginity. After she returns, the text says he does to her as he vowed, notes that she had never had relations with a man, and records the annual lament for her.

The account does not describe the act in detail, which has led to different readings of what happened. One boundary is clear from the wider law: God forbids the sacrifice of sons and daughters (Deuteronomy 12:29–32; 18:9–13). A military victory and the Spirit’s empowering do not themselves tell us that every action of the deliverer is approved.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The Jewish historian Josephus, in Antiquities 5.7.10, reads the account as Jephthah sacrificing his daughter as a burnt offering. He expressly judges it contrary to the law and unacceptable to God. The scholarly notes published by the USCCB also read the story as a fatal sacrifice. These are interpretations of the account, not a claim that every Jewish or Catholic reader holds the same view.

Wesley takes the other main reading: she is dedicated to remaining unmarried for life. He emphasizes the mourning of her virginity and the statement that she knew no man. The Jewish commentator Radak likewise describes her living separately, with Israelite women visiting her yearly. Henry and Gill discuss the uncertainty and also favor an unmarried dedication rather than her death.

The disagreement concerns what Jephthah did, rather than whether God commanded human sacrifice. Rashi treats the vow as a tragedy that could have been prevented by seeking release from it. The account therefore needs care: Jephthah’s conviction that he cannot take back his words is part of the story, not by itself a declaration that God required a forbidden act.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The wider biblical account of sacrifice directs us to what God provides through Christ. Hebrews 10:5–14 presents Jesus willingly doing God’s will and offering Himself once for all. His sacrifice accomplishes the cleansing that repeated offerings cannot secure.

That is the revealed saving purpose to keep before us when reading this painful account. John 10:14–18 also emphasizes Jesus’ willing gift of His own life. Scripture does not identify Jephthah’s daughter as a required sacrifice for Israel’s salvation. The gospel’s assurance rests in Christ’s completed offering, rather than in a bargain a human leader makes to obtain God’s help.
