Passage

2 Samuel 24:1-9

Why does David insist on counting the troops?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Again the anger of the LORD burned against Israel, and He stirred up David against them, saying, “Go and take a census of Israel and Judah.” 2 So the king said to Joab the commander of his army, who was with him, “Go now throughout the tribes of Israel from Dan to Beersheba and register the troops, so that I may know their number.” 3 But Joab replied to the king, “May the LORD your God multiply the troops a hundred times over, and may the eyes of my lord the king see it. But why does my lord the king want to do such a thing?” 4 Nevertheless, the king’s word prevailed against Joab and against the commanders of the army. So Joab and the commanders of the army departed from the presence of the king to register the troops of Israel.

5 They crossed the Jordan and camped near Aroer, south of the town in the middle of the valley, and proceeded toward Gad and Jazer. 6 Then they went to Gilead and the land of Tahtim-hodshi, and on to Dan-jaan and around to Sidon. 7 They went toward the fortress of Tyre and all the cities of the Hivites and Canaanites. Finally, they went on to the Negev of Judah, to Beersheba. 8 At the end of nine months and twenty days, having gone through the whole land, they returned to Jerusalem. 9 And Joab reported to the king the total number of the troops. In Israel there were 800,000 men of valor who drew the sword, and in Judah there were 500,000.

What is the context?

The final chapter opens with the LORD’s anger against Israel and David being stirred to take a census. The reason for the anger against Israel is not specified. David orders Joab to register the troops throughout the land.

Joab wishes that God would multiply the people but questions why David wants the count. The king overrules him and the commanders. They travel through the land and return after nine months and twenty days.

The report gives eight hundred thousand men of valor in Israel and five hundred thousand in Judah. The following verses show David recognizing the census as a serious wrong.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry understands David’s sin as pride and reliance on military strength. This reading focuses on the confidence behind the count, rather than treating every census as forbidden.

The USCCB notes place another emphasis on royal power: a count equips a king to impose demands on the people. The people belong to God, and royal control over them has limits. This is an explanation of the census’s significance, rather than a motive David explicitly states.

Gill reads the opening alongside 1 Chronicles 21:1, which names Satan as the one inciting David. Henry describes David as being left to act wrongly under God’s judgment. These readings distinguish God’s rule over events from David’s responsibility for the act.

James 1:13–15 also matters when reading this difficult opening: God does not tempt anyone to evil, and people are accountable for the desires that lead them into sin.
