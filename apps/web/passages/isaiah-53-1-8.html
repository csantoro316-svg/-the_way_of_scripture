Passage

Isaiah 53:1-8

Why does the innocent Servant suffer for people who rejected Him?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Who has believed our message? And to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed? 2 He grew up before Him like a tender shoot, and like a root out of dry ground. He had no stately form or majesty to attract us, no beauty that we should desire Him. 3 He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief. Like one from whom men hide their faces, He was despised, and we esteemed Him not.

4 Surely He took up our infirmities and carried our sorrows; yet we considered Him stricken, struck down by God, and afflicted. 5 But He was pierced for our transgressions, He was crushed for our iniquities; the punishment that brought us peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed. 6 We all like sheep have gone astray, each one has turned to his own way; and the LORD has laid upon Him the iniquity of us all.

7 He was oppressed and afflicted, yet He did not open His mouth. He was led like a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is silent, so He did not open His mouth. 8 By oppression and judgment He was taken away, and who can recount His descendants? For He was cut off from the land of the living; He was stricken for the transgression of My people.

What is the context?

The account opens with a question about who has believed the message. The Servant grows like a tender shoot in dry ground. He has none of the outward splendor that would make people naturally desire Him; instead, He is despised and rejected.

The speakers then correct their earlier judgment. They had taken His affliction as evidence that God was striking Him down. Now they describe Him carrying their infirmities and sorrows and suffering because of their transgressions and iniquities. The people are the wandering sheep; He bears what their wandering has brought.

The Hebrew word חֳלִי, choli (kho-LEE), used in the description of infirmities, means sickness or disease. Brown–Driver–Briggs also lists its use for distress pictured as sickness. The passage gives the Servant’s suffering a deeply personal character: He carries the burdens of others.

He does not answer oppression with self-defense. The image is a lamb led to slaughter or a sheep silent before its shearers. The closing lines describe His being taken away through oppression and judgment and cut off from the land of the living for the transgression of the people.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The major Christian traditions read the Servant’s saving suffering through Jesus Christ. The Catholic Catechism, paragraphs 615–616, the Westminster Confession, chapter 8.5 and the Methodist Articles II and XX all teach that His obedient, sacrificial death deals with sin and brings reconciliation with God. That shared reading does not mean their accounts of every aspect of salvation are identical.

Henry and Wesley emphasize the reversal in the speakers’ understanding: the Servant is suffering for their sins, rather than for wrongdoing of His own. They connect the sheep going their own way with humanity’s departure from God and the Servant’s suffering with the peace He secures.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 8:26–35 gives an explicit connection. The Ethiopian official is reading the lines about the sheep led to slaughter and the life taken from the earth. Philip begins with that very Scripture and explains the good news about Jesus.

The New Testament also shows two connected uses of the burden-bearing and healing language. Matthew 8:14–17 cites Isaiah when Jesus heals the sick. First Peter 2:21–25 describes Christ bearing sins, enabling people to live righteously, and bringing wandering sheep back to their Shepherd.

These uses keep both His compassion for suffering people and His rescue from sin in view. Matthew describes actual healings in Jesus’ ministry; Peter explains what His suffering means for people who turn from sin to Him. Together they show why this passage is central to the New Testament’s explanation of His saving work.
