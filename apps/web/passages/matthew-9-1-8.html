Passage

Matthew 9:1-8

Jesus Has Authority to Forgive

Berean Standard Bible

1 Jesus got into a boat, crossed over, and came to His own town. 2 Just then some men brought to Him a paralytic lying on a mat. When Jesus saw their faith, He said to the paralytic, “Take courage, son; your sins are forgiven.” 3 On seeing this, some of the scribes said to themselves, “This man is blaspheming!”

4 But Jesus knew what they were thinking and said, “Why do you harbor evil in your hearts? 5 Which is easier: to say, ‘Your sins are forgiven,’ or to say, ‘Get up and walk’? 6 But so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins...” Then He said to the paralytic, “Get up, pick up your mat, and go home.”

7 And the man got up and went home. 8 When the crowds saw this, they were filled with awe and glorified God, who had given such authority to men.

What is the context?

After the people across the sea ask Jesus to leave, He crosses back to His own town. Matthew 4:13 has already identified Capernaum as His home. Some men bring a paralyzed man on a mat. Jesus sees their faith and begins with an assurance of forgiveness, before saying anything about the man's ability to walk.

Mark 2:1–12 and Luke 5:17–26 describe the crowded house and the men lowering their friend through the roof. They also make the scribes' objection explicit: forgiving sins is God's work. The dispute concerns who Jesus is and what authority He has, rather than whether the man's physical need matters.

The word translated forgiven comes from ἀφίημι, aphiēmi (ah-FEE-ay-mee). Thayer's Greek Lexicon includes both releasing a debt and forgiving sins among its uses, and lists this passage. Jesus announces a release from guilt before God. The word does not identify a particular sin as the cause of the man's paralysis.

Jesus answers the hidden objection with something the witnesses can see. A claim to forgive could be spoken without a visible result, but telling a paralyzed man to stand and walk can be tested immediately. Jesus states the purpose of the healing: they are to know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins. The man rises and goes home, and the crowd glorifies God for giving such authority to men.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the healing as confirming Jesus' authority to forgive sins, with the man's deeper need addressed alongside his bodily need. Wesley on Matthew 9:3–8 sees the healing as evidence of divine power, and the Catholic Catechism, 1441, likewise explains Jesus' forgiveness through His identity as the Son of God.

Readers also consider how the closing reference to authority given to men relates to the Church's later ministry. The US Catholic bishops' note on Matthew 9:8 connects it with the Church's authority to forgive, and the Catholic Catechism, 1441–1442, describes Christ entrusting the forgiveness pronounced in confession to the apostles' ministry.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 30.2, describes the Church's authority through proclaiming the gospel, exercising discipline, and restoring repentant people to fellowship. These traditions describe that ministry differently, while locating its authority in Christ rather than in a person's independent power to cancel another's offense against God.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The forgiveness announced in this house belongs to Jesus' wider saving mission. After His resurrection, in Luke 24:44–49, He explains that the Scriptures point to His suffering and rising, and that repentance and forgiveness will be proclaimed in His name to all nations. What is given to this man reaches far beyond this one room.

His healing is real, but the hope offered here is larger than being able to walk again. Jesus' death and resurrection open the way for the forgiveness that His followers are sent to announce. The episode reveals the authority of the Savior whose mission brings people back to God.
