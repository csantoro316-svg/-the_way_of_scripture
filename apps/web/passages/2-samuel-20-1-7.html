Passage

2 Samuel 20:1-7

Why does another rebellion begin as David returns?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now a worthless man named Sheba son of Bichri, a Benjamite, happened to be there, and he blew the ram’s horn and shouted: “We have no share in David, no inheritance in Jesse’s son. Every man to his tent, O Israel!” 2 So all the men of Israel deserted David to follow Sheba son of Bichri. But the men of Judah stayed by their king all the way from the Jordan to Jerusalem. 3 When David returned to his palace in Jerusalem, he took the ten concubines he had left to take care of the palace, and he placed them in a house under guard. He provided for them, but he no longer slept with them. They were confined until the day of their death, living as widows.

4 Then the king said to Amasa, “Summon the men of Judah to come to me within three days, and be here yourself.” 5 So Amasa went to summon Judah, but he took longer than the time allotted him. 6 And David said to Abishai, “Now Sheba the son of Bichri will do us more harm than Absalom. Take your lord’s servants and pursue him, or he will find fortified cities and elude us.” 7 So Joab’s men, along with the Cherethites, the Pelethites, and all the mighty men, marched out of Jerusalem in pursuit of Sheba son of Bichri.

What is the context?

The quarrel at the end of chapter 19 becomes an opening for another revolt. Sheba, a Benjamite, sounds the ram’s horn and calls Israel away from David. Israel follows him, while Judah remains with the king on the journey to Jerusalem.

The Hebrew word behind the description of Sheba as worthless is beliyyaal, pronounced beh-lee-YAH-al. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes this verse among its examples of a base or wicked person. The description concerns his character and conduct.

Back in Jerusalem, David places the ten concubines under guard and provides for them, but no longer sleeps with them. Their confinement lasts for the rest of their lives. The consequences of Absalom’s actions in chapter 16 continue after his death.

David orders Amasa to gather Judah’s men within three days. When he takes longer, David sends Abishai and the troops already available. He fears Sheba will reach fortified cities and become an even greater danger than Absalom.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry connects the revolt with the pride and angry exchanges that came before it. A dispute among the king’s supporters creates an opportunity for someone who wants to reject the king altogether.

Wesley understands the statement about all Israel as referring to the general body of Israelites present. The scene describes the division at David’s return, rather than giving a separate account of every individual in every tribe.

Gill leaves the reason for Amasa’s delay uncertain: he considers Amasa’s own slowness, reluctance among the people and insufficient time. The text reports the delay without explaining its cause.

The women’s situation also deserves attention. They receive material support, but lose their freedom and their former life. The account records what David does; it does not record their consent to Absalom’s actions or tell us what each woman thought.
