Passage

1 Samuel 2:1-11

Why does Hannah’s thanksgiving reach beyond her own family?

Berean Standard Bible

1 At that time Hannah prayed: “My heart rejoices in the LORD; my horn is exalted in the LORD. My mouth speaks boldly against my enemies, for I rejoice in Your salvation. 2 There is no one holy like the LORD. Indeed, there is no one besides You! And there is no Rock like our God. 3 Do not boast so proudly, or let arrogance come from your mouth, for the LORD is a God who knows, and by Him actions are weighed.

4 The bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble are equipped with strength. 5 The well-fed hire themselves out for food, but the starving hunger no more. The barren woman gives birth to seven, but she who has many sons pines away. 6 The LORD brings death and gives life; He brings down to Sheol and raises up. 7 The LORD sends poverty and wealth; He humbles and He exalts. 8 He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap. He seats them among princes and bestows on them a throne of honor. For the foundations of the earth are the LORD’s, and upon them He has set the world.

9 He guards the steps of His faithful ones, but the wicked perish in darkness; for by his own strength shall no man prevail. 10 Those who oppose the LORD will be shattered. He will thunder from heaven against them. The LORD will judge the ends of the earth and will give power to His king. He will exalt the horn of His anointed.” 11 Then Elkanah went home to Ramah, but the boy began ministering to the LORD before Eli the priest.

What is the context?

Hannah’s prayer turns from the son she has received to the God who gave him. She praises His holiness, knowledge and power to overturn human expectations. The strong can be brought low; the hungry and poor can be raised up. People cannot make themselves secure simply by being powerful.

The repeated horn image may sound unfamiliar. The Hebrew קֶרֶן, qeren (KEH-ren), literally means a horn, but here pictures strength and honor. Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically identifies this use in verses 1 and 10. Hannah begins by thanking God for lifting her up and ends by speaking of His king being strengthened.

The word translated anointed is מָשִׁיחַ, mashiach (mah-SHEE-akh). It describes someone set apart by anointing, including kings and priests; the lexicon lists several such uses. The word alone does not mean every person called anointed is Jesus. Here it introduces a royal hope that reaches beyond Hannah’s immediate circumstances.

The song’s pictures are broader than a report of her family. The seven children of verse 5 are not a claim that she already has seven sons; verse 21 later names three more sons and two daughters. The scene closes with Samuel beginning his service under Eli while Elkanah returns home.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands Hannah’s raised horn as restored strength and honor. He reads the song’s reversals through her own deliverance while also seeing its closing kingly hope as reaching toward Christ. He treats seven as an expression of abundance rather than a literal count of children already born.

Henry likewise emphasizes that Hannah’s joy rests in God Himself, extending beyond the gift of Samuel. He reads the king and anointed language in light of Christ’s kingdom. These are Christian readings of the song within the larger Bible; they do not require every contrast in the poem to describe an event already completed in Hannah’s household.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hannah’s song has a close parallel in Mary’s praise before Jesus’ birth. In Luke 1:46–55, Mary rejoices in God, praises His holiness, and describes the proud brought low, the humble lifted up, and the hungry fed. She connects these reversals with God remembering His promise to Abraham.

This is a shared pattern of praise, rather than a claim that Luke explicitly quotes every line from Hannah. The same chapter’s prophecy speaks of a horn of salvation in David’s house (Luke 1:68–75). Hannah’s thanksgiving opens toward the royal hope; Luke announces God’s promised rescue arriving in the story of Jesus.

What is our position?

Our study [Prayer and Faith](../studies/prayer-and-faith.html) points to Hannah’s thanksgiving as evidence of what shaped her request. Her praise centers on God’s holiness, His rule over life and death, and His power to bring down the proud and lift the weak. She responds by worshiping God for who He is, rather than presenting the answer as proof that she had prayed hard enough.
