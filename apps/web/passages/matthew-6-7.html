Passage

Matthew 6:7

Words That Mean Something

Berean Standard Bible

7 And when you pray, do not babble on like pagans, for they think that by their many words they will be heard.

What is the context?

This verse belongs to Jesus' teaching about prayer in Matthew 6:5–13. He has just warned against praying to gain an audience's admiration. He now warns against another mistake: supposing that using many words is what makes God listen.

The verb for babbling is βαττολογέω, battologeō (bat-toh-loh-GEH-oh). Thayer's Greek Lexicon explains it here as using many idle words or repeating things emptily. The end of the verse explains the problem. The speakers trust the amount they say as the reason they will be heard.

Jesus is not setting a maximum prayer length or treating every repeated request as meaningless. In Matthew 26:39–44, He returns to the same request in prayer, always submitting to His Father's will. Luke 18:1–8 calls for persistence. A person can return sincerely to a need without treating repetition as a way to force God's hand.

The next verse says the Father already knows what is needed. The model prayer that follows is direct and purposeful. God's name, kingdom, and will guide the requests; the person praying depends on Him for provision, forgiveness, and protection.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand these verses as rejecting prayer offered for show or as an empty stream of words. Wesley on Matthew 6:6–9, the Catholic Catechism, 2700–2704, and the Reformed Westminster Larger Catechism, question 187, all emphasize prayer that is meant from the heart; they allow sincere use of the Lord's Prayer while rejecting words used without understanding or faith.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God's help in prayer reaches into human weakness. Romans 8:26–27 says people do not know how to pray as they should, yet the Spirit helps and intercedes according to God's will. The passage places their hope in His help, not in an ability to produce impressive speech.

Hebrews 4:14–16 gives another reason for confidence: Jesus is the great High Priest who understands human weakness. Through Him, believers can approach God's grace for mercy and help. God's saving provision opens the way for sincere prayer even when the person praying struggles to find words.

What is our position?

Our study Prayer and Faith rejects treating prayer as a formula for obtaining the result we want. It understands Jesus' model as teaching dependence on God and submission to His will. The warning against mechanical repetition fits that reading: a quantity of words cannot replace trust in the Father.

Our companion study What Does Prayer Look Like? points to Jesus' repeated prayer in Gethsemane and explains that continued asking can remain consistent with surrender. It is the Father's will that must govern the asking.
