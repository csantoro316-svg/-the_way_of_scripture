Passage

1 Kings 22:13-18

Why does Micaiah first repeat the promise of victory?

Berean Standard Bible

13 Then the messenger who had gone to call Micaiah instructed him, “Behold now, with one accord the words of the prophets are favorable to the king. So please let your words be like theirs, and speak favorably.” 14 But Micaiah said, “As surely as the LORD lives, I will speak whatever the LORD tells me.” 15 When Micaiah arrived, the king asked him, “Micaiah, should we go to war against Ramoth-gilead, or should we refrain?” “Go up and triumph,” Micaiah replied, “for the LORD will deliver it into the hand of the king.” 16 But the king said to him, “How many times must I make you swear not to tell me anything but the truth in the name of the LORD?”

17 So Micaiah declared: “I saw all Israel scattered on the hills like sheep without a shepherd. And the LORD said, ‘These people have no master; let each one return home in peace.’” 18 Then the king of Israel said to Jehoshaphat, “Did I not tell you that he never prophesies good for me, but only bad?”

What is the context?

The messenger urges Micaiah to agree with the other prophets and give the king a favorable answer. Micaiah replies that he will speak what the LORD tells him.

His first answer repeats their promise of success. Ahab immediately demands the truth, showing that he does not receive this as Micaiah’s serious message. Micaiah then describes Israel scattered on the hills like sheep without a shepherd, with permission for each person to return home.

The picture points to the loss of the king rather than the promised triumph. Ahab responds by treating the warning as further evidence that Micaiah is against him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill read Micaiah’s initial answer as irony: he echoes the answer Ahab wants before making the true warning clear. Their reading follows the contrast between that first response and the king’s immediate demand to hear the truth.

Henry emphasizes that warning someone of danger is an act of kindness. An unwelcome message is not necessarily an unkind one, and the hearer’s dislike does not make it false.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The image of people without a shepherd continues through Scripture. In Mark 6:30–44, Jesus sees the crowd in that condition, has compassion, teaches them and provides food.

This is a broader biblical connection, rather than a claim that Micaiah is directly predicting that scene. Here the people face the loss of their king. In John 10:11–18, Jesus describes Himself as the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep. His care supplies a striking contrast to leadership that leaves people exposed.
