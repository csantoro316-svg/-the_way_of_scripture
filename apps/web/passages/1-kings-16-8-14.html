Passage

1 Kings 16:8-14

How does the judgment on Baasha’s house unfold?

Berean Standard Bible

8 In the twenty-sixth year of Asa’s reign over Judah, Elah son of Baasha became king of Israel, and he reigned in Tirzah two years. 9 However, while Elah was in Tirzah getting drunk in the house of Arza the steward of his household there, Elah’s servant Zimri, the commander of half his chariots, conspired against him. 10 So in the twenty-seventh year of Asa’s reign over Judah, Zimri went in, struck Elah down, and killed him. And Zimri reigned in his place.

11 As soon as Zimri began to reign and was seated on the throne, he struck down the entire household of Baasha. He did not leave a single male, whether a kinsman or friend. 12 So Zimri destroyed the entire household of Baasha, according to the word that the LORD had spoken against Baasha through Jehu the prophet. 13 This happened because of all the sins Baasha and his son Elah had committed and had caused Israel to commit, provoking the LORD, the God of Israel, to anger with their worthless idols. 14 As for the rest of the acts of Elah, along with all his accomplishments, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel?

What is the context?

Elah son of Baasha reigns two years in Tirzah. While he is getting drunk in his steward Arza’s house, Zimri, commander of half the royal chariots, conspires against him and kills him.

Zimri takes the throne and destroys Baasha’s household, including its male relatives and friends. The narrator connects this with the word spoken through Jehu.

The reason given includes the sins of both Baasha and Elah and their influence on Israel. Another violent transfer of power ends a royal house without first ending the worship that brought it under judgment.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill explain the description of the idols as worthless: they cannot provide the help their worshippers seek. Henry likewise connects the destruction with the kings’ continuing religious choices.

The repeated judgment shows the failure of a dynasty to learn from its predecessor. Zimri’s seizure of the throne will itself be judged in the next part of the account.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The wider Bible addresses more than the repetition of one sinful pattern after another. In Romans 6:15–23, Paul contrasts slavery to sin with the freedom God gives for wholehearted obedience and a life of righteousness.

This is a connection in theme, not Paul’s explanation of Elah’s assassination. The history shows the destructive course of entrenched sin; the New Testament announces the possibility of a different allegiance through God’s gift of life in Christ.
