Passage

1 Samuel 11:1-11

Who will rescue Jabesh-gilead from Nahash?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then Nahash the Ammonite came up and laid siege to Jabesh-gilead. All the men of Jabesh said to him, “Make a treaty with us, and we will serve you.” 2 But Nahash the Ammonite replied, “I will make a treaty with you on one condition, that I may put out everyone’s right eye and bring reproach upon all Israel.” 3 “Hold off for seven days,” replied the elders of Jabesh, “and let us send messengers throughout Israel. If there is no one to save us, we will surrender to you.” 4 When the messengers came to Gibeah of Saul and relayed these words in the hearing of the people, they all wept aloud. 5 Just then Saul was returning from the field, behind his oxen. “What troubles the people?” asked Saul. “Why are they weeping?” And they relayed to him the words of the men from Jabesh.

6 When Saul heard their words, the Spirit of God rushed upon him, and he burned with great anger. 7 He took a pair of oxen, cut them into pieces, and sent them by messengers throughout the land of Israel, proclaiming, “This is what will be done to the oxen of anyone who does not march behind Saul and Samuel.” Then the terror of the LORD fell upon the people, and they came out together as one man. 8 And when Saul numbered them at Bezek, there were 300,000 Israelites and 30,000 men of Judah. 9 So they said to the messengers who had come, “Tell the men of Jabesh-gilead: ‘Deliverance will be yours tomorrow by the time the sun is hot.’” And when the messengers relayed this to the men of Jabesh, they rejoiced.

10 Then the men of Jabesh said to Nahash, “Tomorrow we will come out, and you can do with us whatever seems good to you.” 11 The next day Saul organized the troops into three divisions, and during the morning watch they invaded the camp of the Ammonites and slaughtered them, until the hottest part of the day. And the survivors were so scattered that no two of them were left together.

What is the context?

Nahash the Ammonite besieges Jabesh-gilead. The men offer to serve him under a treaty, but he demands the loss of everyone’s right eye as a humiliation of all Israel. The elders obtain seven days to seek help. If no rescuer comes, they will surrender.

The report reaches Gibeah and causes public weeping. Saul is returning from the field when he hears it. The Spirit of God comes upon him, and he burns with anger. He cuts up a pair of oxen and sends the pieces with a summons: anyone refusing to follow Saul and Samuel will face the same loss of his own oxen.

The narrator connects the people’s united response with the terror of the LORD falling on them. A force of 300,000 Israelites and 30,000 men of Judah gathers. The threatened city receives the promise of deliverance by the next day’s heat and rejoices.

Saul divides the troops into three groups and attacks during the morning watch. The Ammonites are defeated and scattered. His private anointing and public appointment are now followed by action on behalf of people facing a cruel enemy.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the rescue as the first fruit of Saul’s rule and emphasizes the courage and readiness that accompany the Spirit’s work. Wesley sees Saul joining Samuel’s name to the summons as reinforcing its authority. Both readings credit God’s enabling while recognizing Saul’s active response.

Wesley understands the threatened mutilation as both humiliation and a means of weakening the people. The explicit reason Nahash gives is reproach upon Israel. The passage does not explain every military effect of losing an eye or establish that this was a universal practice in ancient warfare.

The unity of the response is central to the scene. It contrasts with the fear and helplessness of the opening report. The narrative gives the troop numbers and the outcome without requiring a separate symbolic meaning for each number.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The Spirit’s enabling is directed toward rescuing threatened people, rather than merely enhancing the king’s reputation. This belongs to the Bible’s wider theme of God appointing a deliverer for those in need. In Luke 4:16–21, Jesus announces the Spirit’s anointing for good news, liberty and release.

The comparison concerns divine appointment serving a rescuing purpose. Saul’s battle is a particular event in Israel’s history; Jesus’ saving mission is greater and is not reduced to military victory. The connection should not turn this campaign into a standing instruction for Christians to repeat Saul’s methods.
