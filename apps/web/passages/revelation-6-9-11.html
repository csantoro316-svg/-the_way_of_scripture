Passage

Revelation 6:9-11

Does God hear those who have suffered for His word?

Berean Standard Bible

9 And when the Lamb opened the fifth seal, I saw under the altar the souls of those who had been slain for the word of God and for the testimony they had upheld. 10 And they cried out in a loud voice, “How long, O Lord, holy and true, until You judge those who dwell upon the earth and avenge our blood?” 11 Then each of them was given a white robe and told to rest a little while longer until the full number of their fellow servants, their brothers, were killed, just as they had been killed.

What is the context?

The fifth seal changes the scene. There is no new horse. John sees beneath the altar the souls of people killed because of God’s word and the testimony they maintained. Their deaths have not placed them beyond God’s care.

They ask the holy and true Lord how long it will be before He judges and avenges their blood. This is an appeal for justice to the One entitled to give it. The vision lets their suffering be named and their question be heard.

Each receives a white robe and is told to rest a little longer. Others will still suffer as they have. The answer assures them of a place with God while acknowledging that the wrongs on earth have not yet all been brought to an end. The passage does not name the victims or give a calendar date for the end of their waiting.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readings differ over the historical setting. Gill includes the earlier Christian martyrs, especially those associated with Diocletian’s persecution, and allows later martyrs within the continuing account. Wesley also reads earlier and later periods of persecution. Walvoord instead places these deaths in a future tribulation after the church has been gathered to Christ. The identification depends in part on how each reads the book’s sequence.

Wesley and Gill both distinguish the cry for justice from malicious personal revenge. They understand the white robes as honor, acceptance and joy given to those who suffered. Their explanations point toward confidence in God’s judgment while leaving its execution with Him. The scene does not instruct the church to carry out violence against opponents.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The cry reaches back to Genesis 4:9–10, where Abel’s blood cries out to God. Scripture’s story does not treat a victim’s death as the disappearance of the wrong done. The God who heard then also hears here.

Jesus addresses persistent appeals for justice in Luke 18:1–8, encouraging His people to keep praying and not lose heart. Romans 12:17–21 entrusts vengeance to God and calls believers to overcome evil with good. Read together, these passages give a larger frame for the martyrs’ waiting: God’s people can appeal to Him for wrongs to be put right while trusting Him with the outcome.
