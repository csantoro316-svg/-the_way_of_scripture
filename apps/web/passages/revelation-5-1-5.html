Passage

Revelation 5:1-5

Who is worthy to open the sealed scroll?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I saw a scroll in the right hand of the One seated on the throne. It had writing on both sides and was sealed with seven seals. 2 And I saw a mighty angel proclaiming in a loud voice, “Who is worthy to break the seals and open the scroll?” 3 But no one in heaven or on earth or under the earth was able to open the scroll or look inside it. 4 And I began to weep bitterly, because no one was found worthy to open the scroll or look inside it.

5 Then one of the elders said to me, “Do not weep! Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has triumphed to open the scroll and its seven seals.”

What is the context?

The throne-room vision continues. The One seated on the throne holds a scroll written on both sides and sealed with seven seals. A mighty angel asks who is worthy to open it. No one in the realms named can do so, and John weeps.

The picture recalls Ezekiel 2:8–10, where the prophet receives a scroll written on the front and back. That scroll contains lamentation, mourning and woe. The connection helps place John’s scroll within the biblical pattern of God revealing what He will bring about.

The Greek word biblion can mean a book, scroll or written document; see Thayer’s entry. The word alone does not tell us the precise kind of document John sees. Its meaning must be considered through its place in the vision and what follows as the seals are opened.

An elder interrupts John’s grief with news that someone has triumphed: the Lion of Judah, the Root of David. The focus moves from the inability of others to the unique worthiness of Christ. The next verses show Him taking the scroll as the slain yet living Lamb.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill broadly agree that the scroll represents God’s purposes and the events unfolded through the rest of the vision, which Christ alone is worthy to reveal and bring about. Both connect the titles Lion of Judah and Root of David with the promised Messiah. Their wider accounts of the timing of the events differ, but the central emphasis here is shared: hope rests on Christ’s victory.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The titles reach back to promises already given. Genesis 49:8–10 joins Judah with the image of a lion and the expectation of rule. Isaiah 11:1–2, 10 speaks of the shoot from Jesse and the Root of Jesse whom the nations seek. The elder identifies the victorious One through those promises.

The next scene shows how the victory has come: the Lion is seen as a Lamb who has been slain, and His blood has purchased people for God. The movement from royal titles to sacrificial death ties God’s promised rule to the work of Christ. Revelation presents His suffering and triumph within the same saving purpose.
