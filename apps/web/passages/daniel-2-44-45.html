Passage

Daniel 2:44-45

What makes the kingdom God establishes different?

Berean Standard Bible

44 In the days of those kings, the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that will never be destroyed, nor will it be left to another people. It will shatter all these kingdoms and bring them to an end, but will itself stand forever. 45 And just as you saw a stone being cut out of the mountain without human hands, and it shattered the iron, bronze, clay, silver, and gold, so the great God has told the king what will happen in the future. The dream is true, and its interpretation is trustworthy.”

What is the context?

The God of heaven will establish a kingdom that cannot be destroyed or transferred to another people. It will end the preceding kingdoms and stand forever.

This is the meaning of the stone cut from the mountain without human hands. Its origin and lasting victory come from God rather than the workmanship that produced the statue.

Daniel closes with assurance that the dream is true and its interpretation trustworthy. Earthly rulers succeed one another; the kingdom God establishes is not simply another temporary link in that chain.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read the stone through Christ and His kingdom, beginning in the Roman period and reaching its full victory. Gaebelein places the decisive crushing of the statue at Christ’s future return and the establishment of His earthly kingdom.

Cambridge, following its Greek identification of the fourth kingdom, understands the passage as expecting God’s kingdom after the fall of Antiochus Epiphanes. The difference over the earthly kingdoms therefore carries into the expected timing of this final change.

These are the named interpreters’ readings. The passage’s own emphasis is that God’s kingdom ends the succession of perishable powers and will itself endure.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A close parallel appears in Luke 20:17–18, where Jesus speaks of the rejected stone and of those on whom it falls being crushed. This connects the stone’s judgment imagery with Jesus, while the announcement in Luke 1:30–33 identifies Him as the promised ruler whose kingdom will not end.

Revelation 11:15–19 carries the hope to the kingdom of the world becoming that of the Lord and His Christ, with judgment and reward accompanying His reign. Together these passages give the lasting kingdom its New Testament focus in Christ without settling every disputed detail of the statue’s historical sequence.
