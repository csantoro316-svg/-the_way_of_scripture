Passage

Luke 22:47-53

How does Jesus respond when betrayal turns into arrest?

Berean Standard Bible

47 While He was still speaking, a crowd arrived, led by the man called Judas, one of the Twelve. He approached Jesus to kiss Him. 48 But Jesus asked him, “Judas, are you betraying the Son of Man with a kiss?”

49 Those around Jesus saw what was about to happen and said, “Lord, should we strike with our swords?” 50 And one of them struck the servant of the high priest, cutting off his right ear. 51 But Jesus answered, “No more of this!” And He touched the man’s ear and healed him.

52 Then Jesus said to the chief priests, temple officers, and elders who had come for Him, “Have you come out with swords and clubs as you would against an outlaw? 53 Every day I was with you in the temple courts, and you did not lay a hand on Me. But this hour belongs to you and to the power of darkness.”

What is the context?

A crowd arrives while Jesus is still speaking. Judas, one of the Twelve, leads them and approaches with a kiss. Jesus exposes the contradiction between that familiar gesture and the betrayal it is being used to carry out.

The disciples ask whether they should strike with their swords, and one cuts off the right ear of the high priest’s servant. Jesus stops the violence and heals him. John’s account identifies the disciple as Peter and the servant as Malchus. (John 18:10–11)

Jesus then addresses the chief priests, temple officers and elders who have come against Him. They bring swords and clubs as if He were leading a rebellion, although He taught openly in the temple without being seized. He identifies this moment as their hour and the power of darkness.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see Jesus refusing an armed rescue while continuing in willing obedience to the Father. His healing of the injured servant shows mercy even during His arrest. The Catholic Catechism describes His self-offering as freely given, and Wesley’s notes on this scene emphasize the final act of mercy and the limited hour granted to the powers opposing Him. (Catechism §614; Wesley on Luke 22:51–53)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John’s account makes the connection with the garden prayer clear: Jesus tells Peter to put away his sword and accepts the cup the Father has given Him. His arrest begins the path He has already said fulfills Isaiah’s picture of the Servant counted among wrongdoers. The injustice done to Him and His willing obedience are both present. (John 18:10–11; Luke 22:37; Isaiah 53:7–12)

The wider New Testament shows where the apparent victory of darkness leads. Hebrews says that through death Jesus breaks the devil’s power over those held by fear of death. Colossians describes believers being rescued from the dominion of darkness into the Son’s kingdom, where there is redemption and forgiveness. These passages supply the larger connection: the darkness surrounding His arrest does not have the final word over His saving work. (Hebrews 2:14–15; Colossians 1:12–14)
