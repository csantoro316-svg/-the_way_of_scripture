Passage

Isaiah 35:1-10

What does God’s promised rescue look like for the weary?

Berean Standard Bible

1 The wilderness and the dry land will be glad; the desert will rejoice and blossom like a rose. 2 It will bloom profusely and rejoice with joy and singing. The glory of Lebanon will be given to it, the splendor of Carmel and Sharon. They will see the glory of the LORD, the splendor of our God. 3 Strengthen the limp hands and steady the feeble knees! 4 Say to those with anxious hearts: “Be strong, do not fear! Behold, your God will come with vengeance. With divine retribution He will come to save you.”

5 Then the eyes of the blind will be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped. 6 Then the lame will leap like a deer and the mute tongue will shout for joy. For waters will gush forth in the wilderness, and streams in the desert. 7 The parched ground will become a pool, the thirsty land springs of water. In the haunt where jackals once lay, there will be grass and reeds and papyrus.

8 And there will be a highway called the Way of Holiness. The unclean will not travel it— only those who walk in the Way— and fools will not stray onto it. 9 No lion will be there, and no vicious beast will go up on it. Such will not be found there, but the redeemed will walk upon it. 10 So the redeemed of the LORD will return and enter Zion with singing, crowned with everlasting joy. Gladness and joy will overtake them, and sorrow and sighing will flee.

What is the context?

The barren landscape of judgment gives way to a desert blooming and singing. The people will see the Lord’s glory, and the weak are called to take courage because God is coming to save them.

The rescue reaches people and land together. Blind eyes open, deaf ears hear, the lame leap and the mute sing. Water breaks out in the wilderness, and thirsty ground becomes a place of springs and growing plants.

A highway called the Way of Holiness leads the redeemed toward Zion. Uncleanness and threatening beasts do not govern this road. The chapter ends with the Lord’s redeemed returning in song, with everlasting joy replacing sorrow and sighing.

The movement is from fear and exhaustion to a secure journey home. The promise is not that the weary must create the way themselves; the God who comes to save is the source of their hope.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry sees the kingdom of Christ as the chapter’s great subject, while also noticing Judah’s historical relief. He connects the opened senses with people made able to receive God’s word and the highway with a life directed toward Him.

Wesley particularly emphasizes peoples receiving God’s grace where there had been spiritual barrenness. Gill includes the actual healings in Jesus’ ministry, changed understanding through the gospel, later restoration and the final joy of God’s redeemed people.

These explanations differ in how they distribute the promises across historical relief, the spread of the gospel and future completion. They share the conviction that the Lord brings restoration, holiness and joy; the images are not merely a call to feel more optimistic.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus points to the blind seeing, the deaf hearing and the lame walking when He answers John the Baptist’s question in Matthew 11:2–6. His works give concrete expression to the rescue Isaiah describes and help John’s messengers recognize the One who has come.

Hebrews 12:12–14 also takes up Isaiah’s call to strengthen limp hands and weak knees. It joins that encouragement with making straight paths and pursuing peace and holiness.

Together these uses keep the promise centered on God’s saving work and the life it opens. Jesus brings the promised help, and weary believers are encouraged to continue toward the holiness and joy of life with God.
