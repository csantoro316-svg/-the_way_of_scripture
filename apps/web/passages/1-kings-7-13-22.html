Passage

1 Kings 7:13-22

Who makes the bronze pillars, and why are they named?

Berean Standard Bible

13 Now King Solomon sent to bring Huram from Tyre. 14 He was the son of a widow from the tribe of Naphtali, and his father was a man of Tyre, a craftsman in bronze. Huram had great skill, understanding, and knowledge for every kind of bronze work. So he came to King Solomon and carried out all his work.

15 He cast two pillars of bronze, each eighteen cubits high and twelve cubits in circumference. 16 He also made two capitals of cast bronze to set on top of the pillars, each capital five cubits high. 17 For the capitals on top of the pillars he made a network of lattice, with wreaths of chainwork, seven for each capital. 18 Likewise, he made the pillars with two rows of pomegranates around each grating to cover each capital atop the pillars. 19 And the capitals atop the pillars in the portico were shaped like lilies, four cubits high. 20 On the capitals of both pillars, just above the rounded projection next to the network, were the two hundred pomegranates in rows encircling each capital. 21 Thus he set up the pillars at the portico of the temple. The pillar to the south he named Jachin, and the pillar to the north he named Boaz. 22 And the tops of the pillars were shaped like lilies. So the work of the pillars was completed.

What is the context?

Solomon brings Huram, a skilled bronze craftsman, from Tyre. He is distinct from the king of Tyre who arranged the supplies. His mother is identified here as a widow from Naphtali and his father as a Tyrian bronze worker.

Huram makes two decorated bronze pillars for the temple entrance. Their capitals carry chainwork, pomegranates and lily shapes. The pillar to the south is named Jachin, and the one to the north Boaz.

Jachin, pronounced YAH-keen, means he will establish, according to Brown–Driver–Briggs. The account preserves the names as well as the pillars’ size and workmanship, but gives no separate speech explaining their dedication.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Henry connect the strength and stability of the pillars with dependence on God to establish and sustain His people.

Gill also develops that comparison, while rejecting the idea that the name Boaz must commemorate David’s ancestor. The exact meaning of the pillar’s name is less certain than such a connection might suggest: Brown–Driver–Briggs records several proposals.

The immediate emphasis is the skilled work that marks the entrance to the LORD’s house. The further lessons about stability should be recognized as comparisons drawn from that setting.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The temple-pillar picture appears in Jesus’ promise in Revelation 3:12. The one who overcomes is made a pillar in God’s temple, never again to leave, and receives the names associated with God and His city.

Gill sees an allusion to the named pillars here. That offers a supported comparison: strength and an established place at the temple help express lasting belonging in God’s presence.

Revelation gives the promise its own meaning and setting. The connection encourages confidence in Christ’s keeping, rather than making the two bronze pillars a code for every detail of the vision.
