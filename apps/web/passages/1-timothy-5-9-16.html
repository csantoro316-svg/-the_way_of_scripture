Passage

1 Timothy 5:9-16

What does Paul ask of widows enrolled for support?

Berean Standard Bible

9 A widow should be enrolled if she is at least sixty years old, faithful to her husband, 10 and well known for good deeds such as bringing up children, entertaining strangers, washing the feet of the saints, imparting relief to the afflicted, and devoting herself to every good work.

11 But refuse to enroll younger widows. For when their passions draw them away from Christ, they will want to marry, 12 and thus will incur judgment because they are setting aside their first faith. 13 At the same time they will also learn to be idle, going from house to house and being not only idle, but also gossips and busybodies, discussing things they should not mention.

14 So I advise the younger widows to marry, have children, and manage their households, denying the adversary occasion for slander. 15 For some have already turned aside to follow Satan. 16 If any believing woman has dependent widows, she must assist them and not allow the church to be burdened, so that it can help the widows who are truly in need.

What is the context?

Paul moves from the community’s care for widows to enrollment on a list. He gives an age threshold of sixty and describes a woman known for marital faithfulness, hospitality, help for those in distress and other good works.

He cautions against enrolling younger widows, describing a conflict between the commitment involved and the desire to marry. The warning also addresses idleness, gossip and interference in matters that do not concern them.

Paul advises the younger widows under discussion to marry, raise children and manage their households. He says some have already turned aside, then returns to the family responsibility to support dependent widows so the church can help those truly in need.

The paragraph connects the use of the community’s support with faithful, active life. It does not give a complete description of the list’s organization or the wording of the commitment associated with it.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Several commentators understand enrollment as organized service alongside support. Wesley’s note on verse 9 calls these women deaconesses serving others. Calvin’s comments on verses 9–10 describe widows supported by the church and engaged in caring for the poor and sick. These are interpretations of the list, rather than a complete surviving rulebook for it.

The qualifications receive different applications. Wesley allows successive lawful marriages in his reading of the wife-of-one-husband phrase. The USCCB note instead understands it as married only once. The BSB main text renders the requirement faithful to her husband.

First faith in verse 12 is also read differently. Thayer’s entry for faith lists the verse under pledged faith or faithfulness. Calvin’s comment on verse 12 expressly rejects limiting it to a promise of remaining unmarried and reads it as departure from Christian faith. Wesley describes abandoning trust in God and the earlier commitment to dedicated service. The precise nature of the warning should not be assumed from one reading alone.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 9:36–42 gives a concrete parallel in Tabitha’s acts of charity and the widows who show the clothing she made. The account joins practical care within the believing community with a work of God that leads others to faith. It does not identify Tabitha as a member of this later enrollment list.

Galatians 6:9–10 places continuing good works within the hope of a harvest and calls for good toward all, especially the household of faith. The ordered care described here belongs within that wider life of doing good together.
