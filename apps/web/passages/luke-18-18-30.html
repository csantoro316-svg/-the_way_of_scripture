Passage

Luke 18:18-30

What can wealth give us, and what can only God give?

Berean Standard Bible

18 Then a certain ruler asked Him, “Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?” 19 “Why do you call Me good?” Jesus replied. “No one is good except God alone. 20 You know the commandments: ‘Do not commit adultery, do not murder, do not steal, do not bear false witness, honor your father and mother.’” 21 “All these I have kept from my youth,” he said.

22 On hearing this, Jesus told him, “You still lack one thing: Sell everything you own and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. Then come, follow Me.” 23 But when the ruler heard this, he became very sad, because he was extremely wealthy.

24 Seeing the man’s sadness, Jesus said, “How hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom of God! 25 Indeed, it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God.” 26 Those who heard this asked, “Who then can be saved?” 27 But Jesus said, “What is impossible with man is possible with God.”

28 “Look,” said Peter, “we have left all we had to follow You.” 29 “Truly I tell you,” Jesus replied, “no one who has left home or wife or brothers or parents or children for the sake of the kingdom of God 30 will fail to receive many times more in this age—and in the age to come, eternal life.”

What is the context?

A ruler asks what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus first questions his use of good and points to God’s goodness, then names commandments concerning how people treat others. The ruler says he has kept them from his youth.

Jesus tells him to sell everything, give to the poor and follow Him. Luke records his deep sadness and explains that he is extremely wealthy. The demand brings his possessions and Jesus’ invitation together at a point of decision.

Jesus then compares a rich person’s entrance into the kingdom with a camel passing through a needle’s eye. The hearers ask who can be saved. His answer places the possibility with God where human ability fails.

Peter responds that the disciples have left their possessions and followed Jesus. Jesus assures them that loss for the kingdom’s sake will be met with far more in this age and eternal life in the age to come. This assurance belongs with the costly invitation and the warning about wealth, rather than being a separate promise of effortless prosperity.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see wealth as a serious obstacle when it holds a person’s allegiance, and salvation as something that depends on God. Wesley takes the command to sell everything as the particular remedy for this ruler’s love of wealth, while directing other believers to use their possessions for God’s glory. (Wesley on Luke 18:22)

The Catholic Catechism likewise teaches that disciples must prefer Christ to everything and be detached from riches. The emphasis is on the claim possessions make on the heart and the need to put Christ first. These sources do not treat having money as a substitute for faithfulness or giving it away as an automatic purchase of salvation. (Catechism §§2544–2547)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Paul makes Christ’s own self-giving the reason for generosity: He became poor for the sake of others. Paul then describes one community’s abundance meeting another’s need, and Acts records believers sharing possessions so that people among them did not lack what they needed. This is a supported connection with both the call to give and the wider family life of those who follow Jesus. (2 Corinthians 8:8–15; Acts 4:32–35)

First Timothy tells wealthy believers to put their hope in God, do good and be ready to share, laying up treasure for the future. That instruction shows the wider response the gospel calls for: present resources serve others, while lasting hope rests with God. (1 Timothy 6:17–19)
