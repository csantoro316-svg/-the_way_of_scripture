Passage

1 Samuel 1:1-8

What makes Hannah’s sorrow so painful?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now there was a man named Elkanah who was from Ramathaim-zophim in the hill country of Ephraim. He was the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite. 2 He had two wives, one named Hannah and the other Peninnah. And Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none. 3 Year after year Elkanah would go up from his city to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of Hosts at Shiloh, where Eli’s two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests to the LORD.

4 And whenever the day came for Elkanah to present his sacrifice, he would give portions to his wife Peninnah and to all her sons and daughters. 5 But to Hannah he would give a double portion, for he loved her even though the LORD had closed her womb. 6 Because the LORD had closed Hannah’s womb, her rival would provoke her viciously to taunt her. 7 And this went on year after year. Whenever Hannah went up to the house of the LORD, her rival taunted her until she wept and would not eat. 8 “Hannah, why are you crying?” her husband Elkanah asked. “Why won’t you eat? Why is your heart so grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?”

What is the context?

The book opens inside a troubled household. Elkanah has two wives: Peninnah has children, and Hannah has none. Each year he travels to Shiloh to worship and sacrifice. Eli’s sons, Hophni and Phinehas, serve there as priests. These names introduce the family and the place of worship that will shape Samuel’s early life.

Elkanah loves Hannah and gives her a double portion at the meal. Peninnah turns Hannah’s childlessness into a reason to hurt her. This is repeated cruelty, continuing year after year even during the journey to worship. Hannah reaches the point of weeping and being unable to eat.

The narrator says that the LORD had closed Hannah’s womb. He does not say that she had committed a particular sin or deserved Peninnah’s taunts. Elkanah’s questions draw attention to his love, but they do not remove her grief. His affection and her longing for a child are both real.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The central events are not a major dividing point between Christian traditions: Hannah suffers from childlessness and a rival’s cruelty while her husband continues to love her. Wesley treats Elkanah’s love as something that should remain steady through an affliction Hannah cannot help. He reads the question about ten sons as an appeal to the value of that love.

Henry also emphasizes the harm of adding to someone’s sorrow. He sees Elkanah’s continuing worship amid family conflict as significant. These readings highlight the comfort available to Hannah without making her longing disappear or treating Peninnah’s conduct as justified.
