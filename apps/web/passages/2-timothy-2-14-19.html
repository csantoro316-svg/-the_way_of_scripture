Passage

2 Timothy 2:14-19

How does faithful teaching differ from destructive disputes?

Berean Standard Bible

14 Remind the believers of these things, charging them before God to avoid quarreling over words, which succeeds only in leading the listeners to ruin. 15 Make every effort to present yourself approved to God, an unashamed workman who accurately handles the word of truth.

16 But avoid irreverent, empty chatter, which will only lead to more ungodliness, 17 and the talk of such men will spread like gangrene. Among them are Hymenaeus and Philetus, 18 who have deviated from the truth. They say that the resurrection has already occurred, and they undermine the faith of some. 19 Nevertheless, God’s firm foundation stands, bearing this seal: “The Lord knows those who are His,” and, “Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord must turn away from iniquity.”

What is the context?

Paul asks Timothy to remind people of the teaching just given and stop destructive disputes over words. He contrasts that conduct with the careful work of someone who seeks God’s approval and handles the message of truth faithfully.

The Greek word orthotomeo (or-thoh-toh-MEH-oh) has the picture of cutting straight. In this verse, Thayer explains it as teaching the truth correctly and directly. The point is faithful handling of the message, rather than a license to force a preferred meaning into it.

Paul gives an example of the damage false teaching causes: Hymenaeus and Philetus say the resurrection has already happened and undermine people’s faith. Against that instability, God’s foundation stands. The two sayings on its seal join God’s knowledge of His people with their responsibility to turn from wrongdoing.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin and Wesley read verse 15 as a call to explain and apply the truth faithfully, with God’s approval in view. Both see a real contrast between teaching that helps people and disputes that damage them.

The exact account of the false resurrection teaching is not supplied. Wesley suggests it may have reduced resurrection to a spiritual change already experienced. The USCCB note also explains it as denying a future bodily resurrection in favor of an already realized spiritual rising. These are explanations of the error; the passage itself states its claim and harmful effect without giving a full system.

On the foundation, Calvin particularly emphasizes God preserving His chosen people despite others’ desertion. Wesley emphasizes God’s unchanging truth together with the necessity of turning from sin. Both keep the assurance and the call to holiness together.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The first saying on the seal echoes Numbers 16:1–7, where a challenge to Moses and Aaron is met by God’s declaration that He will show who belongs to Him. In that same account, the people are told to move away from the rebels’ tents (16:23–27). God’s knowledge and His people’s response belong together in both settings.

This is a connection with that event, not a promise that every later dispute will end with the same judgment. 1 Corinthians 15:20–26 sets the hope threatened by the false teaching in its wider setting: Christ has risen, His people will be raised, and death will finally be defeated.

What is our position?

In The Berean Standard, we understand this verse as a call to honest, daily and humble engagement with Scripture. We should examine words in their context, test what we are taught and remain willing to be corrected by the text. The goal is a faith that knows what it believes and why, rather than correctness for its own sake.
