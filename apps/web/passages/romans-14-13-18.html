Passage

Romans 14:13-18

When does using our freedom stop being loving?

Berean Standard Bible

13 Therefore let us stop judging one another. Instead, make up your mind not to put any stumbling block or obstacle in your brother’s way. 14 I am convinced and fully persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean in itself. But if anyone regards something as unclean, then for him it is unclean. 15 If your brother is distressed by what you eat, you are no longer acting in love. Do not by your eating destroy your brother, for whom Christ died.

16 Do not allow what you consider good, then, to be spoken of as evil. 17 For the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking, but of righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. 18 For whoever serves Christ in this way is pleasing to God and approved by men.

What is the context?

Paul redirects judgment toward a practical decision: do not put an obstacle in another believer’s way. He is persuaded that food is not unclean in itself, yet recognizes the seriousness of someone eating what they believe to be wrong.

The freedom to eat does not excuse harming another person’s faith. Paul makes the value of that person clear by naming Christ’s death for them. A meal is too small a thing to set above someone for whom Christ gave His life.

The kingdom of God is characterized by righteousness, peace and joy in the Holy Spirit. Paul changes the measure of success from whether a preferred food is served to whether the community’s life serves Christ and reflects those qualities.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the passage as placing love above displaying one’s freedom. The warning concerns serious harm to another’s faith, rather than winning a disagreement about food.

The warning also touches a wider disagreement about perseverance. Wesley’s note on verse 15 understands it as a real possibility of destruction for one for whom Christ died. Reformed churches following the Westminster Confession, chapter 17, teach that those God truly calls will not finally fall from grace. That doctrinal difference does not remove the passage’s direct demand to protect another person rather than use liberty carelessly.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

1 Corinthians 8:7–13 gives a close parallel. Paul again describes a weak conscience, a freedom that can become a stumbling block, and a brother for whom Christ died. He connects wounding that person with sinning against Christ.

The value of the person is measured by Christ’s saving work. Care for another believer is therefore part of honoring the Lord who gave Himself for them. The connection turns knowledge and freedom toward the preservation and good of God’s people.
