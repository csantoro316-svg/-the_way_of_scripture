Passage

Ezekiel 27:28-36

Why do the sailors and merchants mourn so bitterly for Tyre?

Berean Standard Bible

28 The countryside will shake when your sailors cry out. 29 All who handle the oars will abandon their ships. The sailors and all the captains of the sea will stand on the shore. 30 They will raise their voices for you and cry out bitterly. They will throw dust on their heads and roll in ashes. 31 They will shave their heads for you and wrap themselves in sackcloth. They will weep over you with anguish of soul and bitter mourning.

32 As they wail and mourn over you, they will take up a lament for you: ‘Who was ever like Tyre, silenced in the middle of the sea? 33 When your wares went out to sea, you satisfied many nations. You enriched the kings of the earth with your abundant wealth and merchandise. 34 Now you are shattered by the seas in the depths of the waters; your merchandise and the people among you have gone down with you.

35 All the people of the coastlands are appalled over you. Their kings shudder with fear; their faces are contorted. 36 Those who trade among the nations hiss at you; you have come to a horrible end and will be no more.’”

What is the context?

The shipwreck produces an answering cry around the coast. Sailors leave their ships and stand on shore, throwing dust on their heads, rolling in ashes and clothing themselves in sackcloth.

Their lament remembers what Tyre supplied: its wares satisfied nations, and its trade enriched kings. Now merchandise and people have gone down together. The loss reaches those who depended on the city as well as those within it.

The closing reactions mix grief, fear and hissing. The beautiful city of the opening verses has become an object of horror. The long celebration of everything it possessed ends in silence and loss.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads the maritime scene as a picture of the city’s fall, with the sea that seemed its protection becoming its grave. Henry draws the lesson toward responsible use of wealth and hope in God.

The lament makes the disaster personal: people are lost with the goods, and those left on shore grieve. Recognizing the judgment does not require treating the suffering as insignificant.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The commercial mourning has a closely supported parallel in Revelation 18:11–24. Merchants mourn the end of their market, a long list of cargo includes enslaved human lives, and seafarers stand at a distance lamenting a city whose wealth enriched them.

The parallel carries the critique of a powerful trading center into Revelation’s judgment on Babylon. The issue reaches beyond lost luxury: Revelation names the blood of prophets and saints among the reasons for judgment. The shared pictures call readers to see the human cost and moral character of prosperity, rather than assume that a thriving market proves all is well.
