Passage

Isaiah 43:1-13

Why does God call a judged people His own?

Berean Standard Bible

1 But now, this is what the LORD says— He who created you, O Jacob, and He who formed you, O Israel: “Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; you are Mine! 2 When you pass through the waters, I will be with you; and when you go through the rivers, they will not overwhelm you. When you walk through the fire, you will not be scorched; the flames will not set you ablaze. 3 For I am the LORD your God, the Holy One of Israel, your Savior; I give Egypt for your ransom, Cush and Seba in your place. 4 Because you are precious and honored in My sight, and because I love you, I will give men in exchange for you and nations in place of your life.

5 Do not be afraid, for I am with you; I will bring your offspring from the east and gather you from the west. 6 I will say to the north, ‘Give them up!’ and to the south, ‘Do not hold them back!’ Bring My sons from afar, and My daughters from the ends of the earth— 7 everyone called by My name and created for My glory, whom I have indeed formed and made.”

8 Bring out a people who have eyes but are blind, and who have ears but are deaf. 9 All the nations gather together and the peoples assemble. Who among them can declare this, and proclaim to us the former things? Let them present their witnesses to vindicate them, so that others may hear and say, “It is true.” 10 “You are My witnesses,” declares the LORD, “and My servant whom I have chosen, so that you may consider and believe Me and understand that I am He. Before Me no god was formed, and after Me none will come. 11 I, yes I, am the LORD, and there is no Savior but Me. 12 I alone decreed and saved and proclaimed— I, and not some foreign god among you. So you are My witnesses,” declares the LORD, “that I am God. 13 Even from eternity I am He, and none can deliver out of My hand. When I act, who can reverse it?”

What is the context?

The first words turn from the previous rebuke to the Lord’s claim on Jacob and Israel. He created and formed them, redeemed them and called them by name. The repeated assurance is that He is with them as they pass through waters, rivers and fire.

Egypt, Cush and Seba are named in the language of ransom, and the Lord declares His people precious, honored and loved. Sons and daughters are to be brought from every direction, everyone called by His name and created for His glory.

The scene then returns to a hearing before the nations. Witnesses are called to show who can announce and accomplish what happens. The Lord appoints His servant and people to witness that He alone is God and Savior.

The promise and the task belong together. God’s people are not only gathered and preserved; they are to know Him and bear witness to what He has declared and done.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Gill understand the waters and fire as encouragement through affliction. Gill applies the promise to the trials of God’s people, with God’s presence and support sustaining them through what threatens them.

Commentators differ over the historical setting of the named nations given in exchange. Wesley connects it with Assyrian action redirected toward Egypt and nearby lands; the USCCB notes connect it with Persian conquests after Israel’s release. These are proposed explanations of how the ransom language relates to events.

For the witness scene, the USCCB notes emphasize Israel’s calling to testify to the Lord’s announced and accomplished works. Henry and Gill also extend the witness theme to believers making known the grace and faithfulness they have received. The shared emphasis is that the true Savior makes Himself known through His saving action.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The relationship between suffering, God’s presence and His glory is developed for Christians in 1 Peter 4:12–19. Peter speaks of a fiery trial, sharing in Christ’s sufferings and the Spirit of glory resting on those insulted for His name.

He calls them to entrust themselves to their faithful Creator and continue doing good. That is a shared theme with Isaiah’s assurance from the Creator who claims His people, rather than a direct quotation of Isaiah 43.

The larger purpose includes a faithful people bearing His name through hardship. His care is not confined to easy circumstances; it gives them reason to remain faithful while looking toward the revelation of Christ’s glory.
