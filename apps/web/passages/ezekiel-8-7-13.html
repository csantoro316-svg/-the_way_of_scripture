Passage

Ezekiel 8:7-13

What are the elders doing where they think no one sees?

Berean Standard Bible

7 Then He brought me to the entrance to the court, and I looked and saw a hole in the wall. 8 “Son of man,” He told me, “dig through the wall.” So I dug through the wall and discovered a doorway. 9 Then He said to me, “Go in and see the wicked abominations they are committing here.” 10 So I went in and looked, and engraved all around the wall was every kind of crawling creature and detestable beast, along with all the idols of the house of Israel.

11 Before them stood seventy elders of the house of Israel, with Jaazaniah son of Shaphan standing among them. Each had a censer in his hand, and a fragrant cloud of incense was rising. 12 “Son of man,” He said to me, “do you see what the elders of the house of Israel are doing in the darkness, each at the shrine of his own idol? For they are saying, ‘The LORD does not see us; the LORD has forsaken the land.’” 13 Again, He told me, “You will see them committing even greater abominations.”

What is the context?

A hole in a wall leads Ezekiel to a doorway. Inside, the walls are covered with images of crawling creatures, detestable animals and Israel’s idols. Seventy elders stand before the images, each offering incense. Jaazaniah son of Shaphan is named among them.

The scene exposes the conduct of leaders in a place hidden from ordinary view. Their incense rises before images while they say that the Lord does not see and has abandoned the land.

The vision directly challenges that claim. What they do in darkness is being shown to Ezekiel by the very God whose attention they deny. Once again, he is told that there is more to see.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read this as the exposure of concealed idolatry among Israel’s leaders. Henry emphasizes the contradiction between an outward religious appearance and hidden conduct; Wesley stresses the failure of leaders who should have set an example.

The elders’ claim that God has left them also serves as a reason to seek other objects of worship. The vision answers both parts of their claim: God sees what they are doing, and His coming judgment is a response to their conduct.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Romans 1:18–25 describes people exchanging God’s glory for images of humans, birds, animals and reptiles. Paul explains the underlying exchange: created things are worshiped in place of their Creator.

Paul does not explicitly quote this scene in Ezekiel. His explanation shows why the problem reaches beyond one hidden room or one group of elders. God makes Himself known through His workmanship, yet people can turn that workmanship into a substitute for Him. The gospel addresses this deep refusal to honor the God who made us.
