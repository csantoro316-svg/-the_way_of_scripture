Passage

1 Samuel 5:1-12

Has capturing the ark defeated Israel’s God?

Berean Standard Bible

1 After the Philistines had captured the ark of God, they took it from Ebenezer to Ashdod, 2 carried it into the temple of Dagon, and set it beside his statue. 3 When the people of Ashdod got up early the next morning, there was Dagon, fallen on his face before the ark of the LORD. So they took Dagon and returned him to his place. 4 But when they got up early the next morning, there was Dagon, fallen on his face before the ark of the LORD, with his head and his hands broken off and lying on the threshold. Only the torso remained. 5 That is why, to this day, the priests of Dagon and all who enter the temple of Dagon in Ashdod do not step on the threshold.

6 Now the hand of the LORD was heavy on the people of Ashdod and its vicinity, ravaging them and afflicting them with tumors. 7 And when the men of Ashdod saw what was happening, they said, “The ark of the God of Israel must not stay here with us, because His hand is heavy upon us and upon our god Dagon.” 8 So they called together all the rulers of the Philistines and asked, “What shall we do with the ark of the God of Israel?” “It must be moved to Gath,” they replied. So they carried away the ark of the God of Israel. 9 But after they had moved the ark to Gath, the LORD’s hand was also against that city, throwing it into great confusion and afflicting the men of the city, both young and old, with an outbreak of tumors.

10 So they sent the ark of God to Ekron, but as it arrived, the Ekronites cried out, “They have brought us the ark of the God of Israel in order to kill us and our people!” 11 Then the Ekronites called together all the rulers of the Philistines and said, “Send away the ark of the God of Israel. It must return to its place, so that it will not kill us and our people!” For a deadly confusion had pervaded the city; the hand of God was very heavy upon it. 12 Those who did not die were afflicted with tumors, and the outcry of the city went up to heaven.

What is the context?

The Philistines carry the captured ark to Ashdod and place it beside Dagon’s statue. The next morning the statue is facedown before the ark. Its worshipers must put it back in place. On the following morning it has fallen again, this time with its head and hands broken off. The captured ark needs no Israelite army to make the contrast visible.

The narrator connects the broken statue with a continuing practice: people entering Dagon’s temple avoid stepping on the threshold. He does not say they have abandoned the building altogether. The practice remembers the event while worship at the temple continues.

The trouble reaches beyond the statue. The LORD’s hand is described as heavy on Ashdod, then on Gath when the ark is moved there. People suffer tumors and great confusion. When it reaches Ekron, the inhabitants protest that it has been brought to kill them. The rulers are finally asked to send it back to its own place.

The chapter ends with suffering and an outcry reaching heaven. It makes the source of the disaster explicit: the hand of God is against these cities. It does not give a modern medical diagnosis, a statue’s exact height, or a detailed account of how each person dies.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley sees the ark’s placement as the display of a trophy honoring Dagon. Gill discusses both a trophy of victory and the addition of Israel’s ark alongside an existing deity. These are explanations of the Philistines’ purpose; the narrator directly states the placement and what follows without reporting their whole reasoning.

Henry reads the fallen Dagon as a picture of false worship giving way before God. He extends it into a Christian comparison with Christ overturning the idols people cherish in their hearts. That is his application across the Bible, rather than an explanation spoken by a character in the chapter. His reading also recognizes that removing one outward idol does not end the need to resist pride and selfish desire.

The older commentaries often identify the tumors more specifically as hemorrhoids. Brown–Driver–Briggs gives tumor for the written Hebrew term and discusses the alternate reading. The BSB uses tumors. The exact modern disease is not necessary to understand the account’s main point: the people cannot protect themselves merely by moving the ark from one city to another.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Henry’s Christian comparison can be tested against the New Testament’s own teaching about Jesus’ victory. Colossians 2:6–15 calls believers to remain rooted in Christ, identifies the fullness of God in Him, and describes Him disarming the powers and authorities through the cross.

The shared theme is God’s victory where human appearances might suggest defeat. The Philistines have captured the ark, yet they have not made God powerless. The New Testament places the decisive saving victory in Christ’s cross, where sins are forgiven and hostile powers are overcome. This is a comparison of the two accounts, rather than a claim that the ark’s movements supply a detailed prediction of every event in Jesus’ death.
