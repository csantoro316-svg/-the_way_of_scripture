Passage

1 Samuel 18:1-4

How does Jonathan welcome the man who could become his rival?

Berean Standard Bible

1 After David had finished speaking with Saul, the souls of Jonathan and David were knit together, and Jonathan loved him as himself. 2 And from that day Saul kept David with him and did not let him return to his father’s house. 3 Then Jonathan made a covenant with David because he loved him as himself. 4 And Jonathan removed the robe he was wearing and gave it to David, along with his tunic, his sword, his bow, and his belt.

What is the context?

David has just answered Saul’s question about his family after defeating Goliath. Jonathan, Saul’s son, forms a deep friendship with him. While Saul keeps David at court, Jonathan makes a covenant with him and gives him his robe, tunic and weapons. His love takes the form of a commitment and costly gifts.

The Hebrew verb behind the picture of their souls being knit together is qashar (kah-SHAR), meaning to bind or tie. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes this verse as an expression of strong affection. It helps us picture a close bond, rather than a casual acquaintance.

The reader already knows David has been anointed to replace Saul. That gives the friendship added weight: the king’s son welcomes the young man whose rise could threaten his own future. Jonathan’s generosity will stand in sharp contrast to his father’s growing hostility.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Henry and Gill read this as a sincere friendship expressed through a covenant. They emphasize Jonathan’s willingness to honor David rather than resent him. Henry especially sees God’s grace at work in love that is not controlled by the desire for status.

The gifts receive different emphasis. Gill sees Jonathan clothing and equipping David for life at court and in the army. The USCCB Bible notes also see the exchange pointing toward David as the future heir. The scene can carry that larger meaning without describing a formal transfer of the throne at this moment.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jonathan’s friendship gives a picture of love that willingly gives something up for another. In John 15:12–17, Jesus commands His disciples to love one another as He has loved them, and speaks of laying down one’s life for friends. He brings that love to its fullest expression through His own death.

This is a parallel in the character of love, rather than a claim that John directly interprets Jonathan’s gifts. The connection helps us see why friendship matters in God’s purposes: His people are called to seek another person’s good, even when generosity costs them comfort or standing.
