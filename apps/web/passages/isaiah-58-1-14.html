Passage

Isaiah 58:1-14

What kind of fasting does God actually welcome?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Cry aloud, do not hold back! Raise your voice like a ram’s horn. Declare to My people their transgression and to the house of Jacob their sins. 2 For day after day they seek Me and delight to know My ways, like a nation that does what is right and does not forsake the justice of their God. They ask Me for righteous judgments; they delight in the nearness of God.” 3 “Why have we fasted, and You have not seen? Why have we humbled ourselves, and You have not noticed?” “Behold, on the day of your fast, you do as you please, and you oppress all your workers. 4 You fast with contention and strife to strike viciously with your fist. You cannot fast as you do today and have your voice be heard on high. 5 Is this the fast I have chosen: a day for a man to deny himself, to bow his head like a reed, and to spread out sackcloth and ashes? Will you call this a fast and a day acceptable to the LORD?

6 Isn’t this the fast that I have chosen: to break the chains of wickedness, to untie the cords of the yoke, to set the oppressed free and tear off every yoke? 7 Isn’t it to share your bread with the hungry, to bring the poor and homeless into your home, to clothe the naked when you see him, and not to turn away from your own flesh and blood? 8 Then your light will break forth like the dawn, and your healing will come quickly. Your righteousness will go before you, and the glory of the LORD will be your rear guard. 9 Then you will call, and the LORD will answer; you will cry out, and He will say, ‘Here I am.’ If you remove the yoke from your midst, the pointing of the finger and malicious talk,

10 and if you give yourself to the hungry and satisfy the afflicted soul, then your light will go forth in the darkness, and your night will be like noonday. 11 The LORD will always guide you; He will satisfy you in a sun-scorched land and strengthen your frame. You will be like a well-watered garden, like a spring whose waters never fail. 12 Your people will rebuild the ancient ruins; you will restore the age-old foundations; you will be called Repairer of the Breach, Restorer of the Streets of Dwelling.

13 If you turn your foot from breaking the Sabbath, from doing as you please on My holy day, if you call the Sabbath a delight, and the LORD’s holy day honorable, if you honor it by not going your own way or seeking your own pleasure or speaking idle words, 14 then you will delight yourself in the LORD, and I will make you ride on the heights of the land and feed you with the heritage of your father Jacob.” For the mouth of the LORD has spoken.

What is the context?

The prophet is commanded to declare the people’s sins loudly. They appear eager to seek God, learn His ways and receive just decisions. Yet their complaint reveals a contradiction: they fast and humble themselves outwardly while wondering why He does not notice.

The Lord names what their observance conceals. They pursue their own desires, oppress workers, quarrel and strike one another. Bowed heads, sackcloth and ashes do not make that conduct acceptable.

The fast He chooses is described through freedom and care: break oppressive bonds, release those under the yoke, share bread, shelter the poor and homeless, clothe the naked and stop turning away from family. The promises of light, healing and an answered call accompany the removal of oppression and malicious speech.

The image of a watered garden describes His guidance and renewal, and the rebuilt ruins picture a community restored for people to inhabit. The final appeal concerns the Sabbath: they are to honor His holy day and find delight in Him rather than simply pursuing their own wishes.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read the chapter as a challenge to religious observance that leaves daily wrongdoing untouched. They emphasize repentance expressed in mercy and justice, rather than outward signs standing in for a changed life.

Both connect the care of those in need with a sincere response to God. They also treat honoring the Sabbath as more than a display: the day is to be set apart with willing delight in Him.

The promises of renewal belong to this call for a restored relationship and community. Fasting is not presented as a way to make God overlook cruelty or to purchase a desired answer.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The same concern appears in Jesus’ teaching on fasting in Matthew 6:16–18. He tells His followers to fast before their Father rather than arrange their appearance to win human notice. This is a parallel in teaching, not an explicit quotation of Isaiah.

Matthew 25:31–46 gives a further connection between devotion to the King and care for people. The acts named include feeding the hungry, welcoming a stranger and clothing the naked; Jesus identifies what is done for the least of His brothers with what is done for Him.

Together these passages show a continuing theme in God’s purpose for His people: worship directed toward Him must reach into how they treat others. The outward act and the life lived around it belong together.
