Passage

Acts 16:6-10

How is the journey directed toward Macedonia?

Berean Standard Bible

6 After the Holy Spirit had prevented them from speaking the word in the province of Asia, they traveled through the region of Phrygia and Galatia. 7 And when they came to the border of Mysia, they tried to enter Bithynia, but the Spirit of Jesus would not permit them. 8 So they passed by Mysia and went down to Troas. 9 During the night, Paul had a vision of a man of Macedonia standing and pleading with him, “Come over to Macedonia and help us.” 10 As soon as Paul had seen the vision, we got ready to leave for Macedonia, concluding that God had called us to preach the gospel to them.

What is the context?

The Holy Spirit prevents the travelers from speaking in the province of Asia, and the Spirit of Jesus does not permit their intended entry into Bithynia. They continue to Troas. Luke records the prohibitions without explaining exactly how they are communicated.

Paul then sees a nighttime vision of a Macedonian man asking for help. The group gets ready to cross to Macedonia, concluding that God has called them to preach there. Luke begins using we in describing the preparations, placing the narrator with the travelers.

The direction concerns a particular journey and the spread of the gospel. The passage does not say that Asia is permanently excluded from hearing, or that every later change of plan will carry the same kind of revelation.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the journey as directed by God toward a new place of witness. The text names the Spirit’s prohibitions and Paul’s vision but does not reduce either to a general method of interpreting feelings or inconvenient circumstances.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The response to the vision is a plan to preach, not merely to travel. The help sought from Macedonia is understood in connection with the good news about Jesus, and the next scenes will show that message reaching actual people and households.

This continues the outward witness described in Acts 1:8. The commission reaches beyond the places already visited, while the route unfolds through direction the messengers have not planned for themselves.

What is our position?

Should We Seek Signs From God? names Paul’s experience in Acts 16 as a specific biblical account of God directing His servants through a vision. The study distinguishes such recorded events from treating every dream, impression or coincidence as reliable divine communication. It calls for testing claims while leaving the broader question of continuing visions open.
