Passage

Isaiah 9:1-7

Who brings light into the darkness and peace that lasts?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Nevertheless, there will be no more gloom for those in distress. In the past He humbled the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali, but in the future He will honor the Way of the Sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the nations: 2 The people walking in darkness have seen a great light; on those living in the land of the shadow of death, a light has dawned.

3 You have enlarged the nation and increased its joy. The people rejoice before You as they rejoice at harvest time, as men rejoice in dividing the plunder. 4 For as in the day of Midian You have shattered the yoke of their burden, the bar across their shoulders, and the rod of their oppressor. 5 For every trampling boot of battle and every garment rolled in blood will be burned as fuel for the fire.

6 For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given, and the government will be upon His shoulders. And He will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. 7 Of the increase of His government and peace there will be no end. He will reign on the throne of David and over his kingdom, to establish and sustain it with justice and righteousness from that time and forevermore. The zeal of the LORD of Hosts will accomplish this.

What is the context?

The darkness at the end of chapter 8 gives way to a promise of light. Zebulun and Naphtali, lands that had known humiliation, will receive honor. The change reaches beyond private relief: the nation grows, joy returns, and the burden of oppression is broken.

The reference to the day of Midian recalls God’s deliverance through Gideon in Judges 6–7. Isaiah uses that remembered rescue to describe the defeat of an oppressor. Boots and bloodstained clothing become fuel for the fire; the equipment of war has no further use.

The reason for this hope is a child born and a son given. He will carry the responsibility of government, with names describing extraordinary wisdom, divine might, enduring fatherly care and peace. His rule on David’s throne will grow in justice and righteousness, without an end. The final assurance rests on the Lord’s determination to accomplish it.

The Hebrew פֶּלֶא, pele, pronounced PEH-leh, means a wonder. Brown–Driver–Briggs explains its use here as describing the promised king as a wonderful counselor. The title points to the exceptional character of His counsel, rather than simply giving Him an ordinary personal name.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read this as a promise of Christ and His kingdom. They connect the light with His coming and the release from oppression with His victory over sin. Henry includes both the peace Christ gives His people now and the final peace of His completed kingdom.

There is also discussion of the setting in which Isaiah first spoke. The NABRE notes suggest that the description of the child may reflect a king’s coronation rather than the moment of his birth. This concerns the immediate royal setting of the words; the New Testament’s application to Jesus is explicit in Matthew 4.

Gill explains Everlasting Father in terms of Christ’s lasting care for His people and His relation to eternal life. In this reading the title does not mean that the Son is the same person as God the Father. The title describes the promised ruler’s character and relationship to those He governs.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Matthew 4:12–17 directly identifies Jesus’ ministry in Galilee as the fulfillment of Isaiah’s promise of light in Zebulun and Naphtali. Matthew places the quotation beside Jesus’ announcement that the kingdom of heaven is near. The light arrives in a Person who calls people to repent.

The promise concerning David’s throne also appears in Gabriel’s announcement to Mary. Luke 1:30–33 says that her Son Jesus will receive David’s throne and reign forever. Together these passages identify the King and the lasting rule through which God carries His promise forward.
