Passage

2 Samuel 11:1-5

How does David use his power when he sees Bathsheba?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In the spring, at the time when kings march out to war, David sent out Joab and his servants with the whole army of Israel. They destroyed the Ammonites and besieged Rabbah, but David remained in Jerusalem. 2 One evening David got up from his bed and strolled around on the roof of the palace. And from the roof he saw a woman bathing—a very beautiful woman. 3 So David sent and inquired about the woman, and he was told, “This is Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam and the wife of Uriah the Hittite.” 4 Then David sent messengers to get her, and when she came to him, he slept with her. (Now she had just purified herself from her uncleanness.) Then she returned home. 5 And the woman conceived and sent word to David, saying, “I am pregnant.”

What is the context?

While Joab and Israel’s army besiege Rabbah, David remains in Jerusalem. One evening he rises from his bed, walks on the palace roof and sees a beautiful woman bathing. He inquires and learns that she is Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah the Hittite.

David sends messengers to get her and sleeps with her. The BSB notes that she had just purified herself from her uncleanness. She returns home, then later sends word that she is pregnant. David has acted despite being told that she is married to a man serving in his army.

The account tells us what David sees, asks and sends others to do. It does not report Bathsheba’s private intentions while bathing or describe the conversation when she is brought to him. Those gaps should remain gaps rather than become an invented explanation that she set out to tempt him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley see a warning in David’s remaining at home while the army is away and then pursuing what he sees. The danger develops through choices: learning that the woman is married does not cause him to stop.

Interpreters differ about Bathsheba’s part. Gill and Wesley infer that she was persuaded and consented. Bob Deffinbaugh challenges readings that assign her the role of seducer and emphasizes David’s abuse of royal power. These are individual readings, not official teachings shared by every church in their traditions.

The text itself does not give us Bathsheba’s consent or intentions. It does clearly make David the king who sends for a married woman. The note about purification describes ritual cleanness; it does not establish a plan by Bathsheba to make sexual wrongdoing acceptable through a later washing.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

James 1:13–15 describes desire drawing a person toward sin and sin growing toward death. It also says that God does not tempt people to evil. That distinction matters as we follow a story in which God’s chosen king acts so destructively.

This is a biblical pattern illustrated by David’s choices, not a claim that James directly comments on this encounter. God’s work through David does not excuse his pursuit of another man’s wife or make the harm part of a good example.

The story calls for attention to the point where desire is becoming deliberate action. David has the authority to send for people, but authority gives him responsibility to protect them rather than freedom to take what he wants.
