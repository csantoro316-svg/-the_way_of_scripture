Passage

Luke 13:22-30

Why does Jesus turn a question about numbers into a call to enter?

Berean Standard Bible

22 Then Jesus traveled throughout the towns and villages, teaching as He made His way toward Jerusalem. 23 “Lord,” someone asked Him, “will only a few people be saved?” Jesus answered, 24 “Make every effort to enter through the narrow door. For many, I tell you, will try to enter and will not be able.

25 After the master of the house gets up and shuts the door, you will stand outside knocking and saying, ‘Lord, open the door for us.’ But he will reply, ‘I do not know where you are from.’ 26 Then you will say, ‘We ate and drank with you, and you taught in our streets.’ 27 And he will answer, ‘I tell you, I do not know where you are from. Depart from me, all you evildoers.’

28 There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth when you see Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and all the prophets in the kingdom of God, but you yourselves are thrown out. 29 People will come from east and west and north and south, and will recline at the table in the kingdom of God. 30 And indeed, some who are last will be first, and some who are first will be last.”

What is the context?

Jesus continues teaching through towns and villages on His way to Jerusalem. Someone asks whether only a few will be saved. He answers by calling His hearers to make every effort to enter through the narrow door.

The picture becomes urgent when the householder closes the door. Those outside appeal to their familiarity with him: they ate and drank with him and heard him teach. But they are not received. Nearness to the activity is not presented as the same thing as a right response to the master.

Jesus describes Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and the prophets in the kingdom, while some who expect to belong find themselves outside. People from every direction come to its table. His final reversal unsettles assumptions about who is first and who is last.

The passage directs the question toward the hearer’s own response and the time available to make it. It does not supply a count of the final number saved.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly hear an urgent call to respond to Jesus rather than rely on familiarity or assumed privilege. Calvin emphasizes turning from curiosity about the number saved to the hearer’s own response; Wesley stresses the need to respond before the door closes through faith, prayer and a life directed toward God. (Calvin on Luke 13:23–24; Wesley on Luke 13:24–25)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah describes the Lord preparing a banquet for all peoples and overcoming death and tears. Jesus’ picture of people gathering from every direction around the kingdom’s table shares that wider hope of God’s saving welcome. This is a connection between the pictures, not a claim that every detail is being explained here. (Isaiah 25:6–9)

In John, Jesus explicitly identifies Himself as the gate through whom people enter and are saved. That gives a wider New Testament connection with the call to enter here: the welcome and life God offers are found through Him. (John 10:7–10)
