Passage

Isaiah 44:1-20

Who can give life: the God who formed His people, or an image they form?

Berean Standard Bible

1 But now listen, O Jacob My servant, Israel, whom I have chosen. 2 This is the word of the LORD, your Maker, who formed you from the womb and who will help you: “Do not be afraid, O Jacob My servant, Jeshurun, whom I have chosen. 3 For I will pour water on the thirsty land, and currents on the dry ground. I will pour out My Spirit on your descendants, and My blessing on your offspring. 4 They will sprout among the grass like willows by flowing streams. 5 One will say, ‘I belong to the LORD,’ another will call himself by the name of Jacob, and still another will write on his hand, ‘The LORD’s,’ and will take the name of Israel.”

6 Thus says the LORD, the King and Redeemer of Israel, the LORD of Hosts: “I am the first and I am the last, and there is no God but Me. 7 Who then is like Me? Let him say so! Let him declare his case before Me, since I established an ancient people. Let him foretell the things to come, and what is to take place. 8 Do not tremble or fear. Have I not told you and declared it long ago? You are My witnesses! Is there any God but Me? There is no other Rock; I know not one.”

9 All makers of idols are nothing, and the things they treasure are worthless. Their witnesses fail to see or comprehend, so they are put to shame. 10 Who fashions a god or casts an idol which profits him nothing? 11 Behold, all his companions will be put to shame, for the craftsmen themselves are only human. Let them all assemble and take their stand; they will all be brought to terror and shame. 12 The blacksmith takes a tool and labors over the coals; he fashions an idol with hammers and forges it with his strong arms. Yet he grows hungry and loses his strength; he fails to drink water and grows faint. 13 The woodworker extends a measuring line; he marks it out with a stylus; he shapes it with chisels and outlines it with a compass. He fashions it in the likeness of man, like man in all his glory, that it may dwell in a shrine.

14 He cuts down cedars or retrieves a cypress or oak. He lets it grow strong among the trees of the forest. He plants a laurel, and the rain makes it grow. 15 It serves as fuel for man. He takes some of it to warm himself, and he kindles a fire and bakes his bread. He also fashions it into a god and worships it; he makes an idol and bows down to it. 16 He burns half of it in the fire, and he roasts meat on that half. He eats the roast and is satisfied. Indeed, he warms himself and says, “Ah! I am warm; I see the fire.” 17 From the rest he makes a god, his graven image. He bows down to it and worships; he prays to it and says, “Save me, for you are my god.”

18 They do not comprehend or discern, for He has shut their eyes so they cannot see and closed their minds so they cannot understand. 19 And no one considers in his heart, no one has the knowledge or insight to say, “I burned half of it in the fire, and I baked bread on its coals; I roasted meat and I ate. Shall I make something detestable with the rest of it? Shall I bow down to a block of wood?” 20 He feeds on ashes. His deluded heart has led him astray, and he cannot deliver himself or say, “Is not this thing in my right hand a lie?”

What is the context?

The Lord again addresses Jacob and Israel as the servant He chose, formed and will help. The opening promises water for thirsty ground, His Spirit for their descendants and a people willing to say that they belong to Him.

The poetic name Jeshurun, pronounced yeh-shoo-ROON, adds a note of intended faithfulness. Brown–Driver–Briggs explains it as upright one, a name for Israel expressing its ideal character. The promise concerns a people shaped by the God who calls them His own.

The Lord declares that He is the first and last and the only God. His people are witnesses to what He has announced. That claim is set beside a detailed picture of craftsmen producing an idol.

The maker grows hungry, thirsty and weak. He depends on wood and rain, shapes the figure and gives it a place to stand. Most sharply, he uses part of the same wood to cook a meal and warm himself, then bows before the rest and asks it to save him.

The problem is deeper than poor workmanship. A deceived heart prevents the worshiper from recognizing the lie he holds. The contrast runs through the whole passage: God forms and gives life to His people, while people form an object that cannot give life back.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry, Wesley and Gill connect the promised water and Spirit with renewed life and belonging to God. Their explanations of the idol scene emphasize human dependence and the inability of a manufactured object to become its maker’s savior.

Where traditions differ is in applying the warning to Christian religious images. The Catholic Church, in Catechism 2129–2132, distinguishes the worship due to God alone from honor directed through an image toward the person represented. It grounds the permitted use of sacred images in the incarnation and the teaching of the Second Council of Nicaea.

The Reformed Westminster Confession 21.1 forbids worshiping God under a visible representation; the Larger Catechism, question 109, also rejects making representations of any person of the Trinity. The 1689 Baptist Confession 22.1 likewise excludes worship under visible representations.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, Article XIV, reject worship and adoration of images. The UMC’s published text also directs readers to its guidance on contemporary interpretation in light of its work toward Christian unity.

These statements explain a real difference over the religious use of images. They should not be turned into a claim that every member of a tradition practices identically, or that Catholic teaching treats a piece of wood as a god that can save.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Peter connects the gift of the Spirit with the risen and exalted Jesus in Acts 2:32–39. The promise reaches his hearers, their children and those far away whom God calls. This is a strong parallel with Isaiah’s promised Spirit and renewed people, rather than a claim that Peter directly quotes Isaiah 44.

The turn from idols also appears in 1 Thessalonians 1:8–10: people turn to serve the living God and wait for His risen Son. Colossians 3:1–5 extends the concern beyond carved objects by naming greed as idolatry, within a call to live as people raised with Christ.

Together these connections show the purpose of the change: God gives life and His Spirit so that His people belong to Him and serve Him. Replacing a powerless object is only part of the story; the heart’s allegiance and the life that follows are also changed.
