Passage

2 Peter 3:8-10

Why does God give people time to repent?

Berean Standard Bible

8 Beloved, do not let this one thing escape your notice: With the Lord a day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years are like a day. 9 The Lord is not slow in keeping His promise as some understand slowness, but is patient with you, not wanting anyone to perish but everyone to come to repentance.

10 But the Day of the Lord will come like a thief. The heavens will disappear with a roar, the elements will be destroyed by fire, and the earth and its works will be laid bare.

What is the context?

Peter now addresses his beloved readers directly. The comparison between a day and a thousand years recalls Psalm 90:1–6, where God’s enduring life is set beside human frailty. It corrects the way people measure His faithfulness; it supplies no conversion formula for calculating the date of Christ’s return.

The apparent delay is described as patience that gives room for repentance. The warning about the Day of the Lord remains: it will arrive unexpectedly, like a thief. God’s patience is therefore a reason to respond to Him now.

The BSB ends verse 10 with the earth and its works being laid bare. Other translations read that they will be burned up. The USCCB note records this difference in the surviving texts. The passage’s fire and judgment language remains clear; the wording in the Scripture panel follows the verified BSB.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The main difference concerns the scope of anyone and everyone in verse 9. Wesley understands God’s patience as expressing His desire that no human soul perish. The Catholic Church uses this verse in teaching that God predestines no one to hell and that the Church appeals to His mercy; see Catechism 1037. Neither source treats that desire as a prediction that everyone will finally be saved.

One Reformed reading, presented in Ligonier’s The Lord Is Patient, links anyone and everyone to the believing audience addressed as you. On this reading, the passage concerns God bringing all His chosen people to repentance. The broader Reformed teaching about God’s choice appears in Westminster Confession, chapter 3. Ligonier’s explanation is a particular application of that teaching to this verse.

Calvin’s own comments express the distinction differently: he sees an invitation to all humanity in the gospel while distinguishing that invitation from God’s hidden purpose in choosing people. Reformed writers therefore should not all be assigned one explanation of these words. Across these readings, God’s patience calls for repentance, and His promised coming remains certain.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Romans 2:1–5 also places God’s kindness and patience beside His coming judgment. His kindness leads toward repentance; a hard heart can instead store up judgment. Together the passages show that the time God gives has a saving purpose. The proper response is to turn toward Him rather than treat the passage of time as proof that He will never act.
