Passage

John 1:6-13

How do people become God’s children?

Berean Standard Bible

6 There came a man who was sent from God. His name was John. 7 He came as a witness to testify about the Light, so that through him everyone might believe. 8 He himself was not the Light, but he came to testify about the Light.

9 The true Light, who gives light to everyone, was coming into the world. 10 He was in the world, and though the world was made through Him, the world did not recognize Him. 11 He came to His own, and His own did not receive Him.

12 But to all who did receive Him, to those who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God— 13 children born not of blood, nor of the desire or will of man, but born of God.

What is the context?

The Gospel introduces John the Baptist as a man sent by God to bear witness to the Light. He is the witness whose testimony points people toward the One in whom they are to believe.

The true Light comes into the world He made, but the world does not recognize Him and His own do not receive Him. The account then turns toward those who do receive Him and believe in His name: they are given the right to become God’s children.

Their birth comes from God. Human ancestry, desire and will are not its source. The passage places receiving Christ and believing in His name alongside God’s action in giving this new life.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian traditions commonly agree that becoming God’s child depends on God’s grace and cannot be secured by ancestry or human effort alone. They differ in how they explain God’s initiative, the response of faith and the gift of new birth.

Reformed churches following Westminster and Baptists following the 1689 Confession teach that God renews and draws those He has chosen so they freely come to Christ. Their ability to respond follows the Spirit giving life and renewing their wills. (Westminster Confession 10.1–2; 1689 Baptist Confession 10.1–2)

The Catholic Church likewise teaches that grace is an undeserved gift and that even preparation to receive it is already God’s work. It connects new life with baptism and teaches that God’s initiative calls for a free response and cooperation made possible by His grace. (Catechism §§1996–2002)

The Methodist Articles teach that fallen people cannot turn to God by their own strength: grace comes first, enabling a good will and then working with it. Wesley describes this prior grace as given to everyone, while people may resist its light. This is his explanation of how God enables a response, alongside the Articles’ teaching about grace. (Methodist Articles VIII; Wesley, On Working Out Our Own Salvation, II.1 and III.3–5)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Galatians explains God sending His Son to redeem those under the law so that they receive adoption as His children. The Spirit of the Son then enables them to address God as Father. Romans likewise connects the Spirit, belonging in God’s family and sharing in Christ’s inheritance. These passages show the larger purpose of the new life described here: being brought into the Father’s family through the Son. (Galatians 4:4–7; Romans 8:14–17)

Peter links God’s mercy in giving new birth with a living hope through Jesus’ resurrection and an inheritance kept for His people. Birth from God therefore reaches beyond a change of label; it belongs to the life and future He gives through Christ. (1 Peter 1:3–5)
