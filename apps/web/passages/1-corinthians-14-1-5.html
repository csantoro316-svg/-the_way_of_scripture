Passage

1 Corinthians 14:1-5

Which use of a gift actually helps the church?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Earnestly pursue love and eagerly desire spiritual gifts, especially the gift of prophecy. 2 For he who speaks in a tongue does not speak to men, but to God. Indeed, no one understands him; he utters mysteries in the Spirit. 3 But he who prophesies speaks to men for their edification, encouragement, and comfort. 4 The one who speaks in a tongue edifies himself, but the one who prophesies edifies the church. 5 I wish that all of you could speak in tongues, but I would rather have you prophesy. He who prophesies is greater than one who speaks in tongues, unless he interprets so that the church may be edified.

What is the context?

Paul brings the discussion of love directly into the practice of gifts. Love is to be pursued, and gifts are to be desired, with particular attention to prophecy. His reason is the benefit people receive when they can understand what is said.

The repeated word edification means building people up. Its Greek word, oikodomē (oy-koh-doh-MAY), οἰκοδομή, comes from the language of building and is used for helping people grow in faith and godly life; see Thayer’s entry. Paul is asking what strengthens the community, rather than what produces the most striking display.

An uninterpreted tongue is directed to God and benefits the speaker, while prophecy strengthens, encourages and comforts other people. Paul does not simply condemn tongues: he expresses a desire that the Corinthians speak in them. But he would rather they prophesy because others can be helped.

The exception at the end matters: if the tongue is interpreted so the church is built up, the comparison changes. The deciding concern is not how unusual the speech sounds, but whether other people receive something that helps them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly recognize the priority of building up the congregation, while differing over the kind of speech involved. The Grace Community Church statement on tongues understands the gift as an unlearned human language. The Assemblies of God’s baptism paper also allows speech that may be heavenly rather than an identifiable human language. These are the positions of the named church and denomination, not of all their broader traditions.

There are also differences in explaining prophecy. Wesley’s note on verse 1 emphasizes opening and applying Scripture. The Assemblies of God’s 2025 prophecy paper affirms Spirit-given messages for the church today, subject to testing and without Scripture’s authority. Both accounts direct attention to helping the people who hear, rather than giving a speaker personal importance.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

At Pentecost, people from many nations hear God’s mighty works in their own languages (Acts 2:1–11). The Spirit’s gift serves God’s purpose of making His work known beyond one language group.

That event gives the discussion a wider setting: the Spirit’s work serves God’s people and His witness in the world. Paul’s concern for understandable communication is consistent with that purpose. A message that can be received can strengthen, encourage and comfort the people God is gathering.
