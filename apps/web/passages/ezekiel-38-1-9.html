Passage

Ezekiel 38:1-9

Who is Gog, and why does he come against Israel?

Berean Standard Bible

1 And the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, set your face against Gog of the land of Magog, the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal. Prophesy against him 3 and declare that this is what the Lord GOD says: Behold, I am against you, O Gog, chief prince of Meshech and Tubal. 4 I will turn you around, put hooks in your jaws, and bring you out with all your army—your horses, your horsemen in full armor, and a great company armed with shields and bucklers, all brandishing their swords. 5 Persia, Cush, and Put will accompany them, all with shields and helmets, 6 as well as Gomer with all its troops, and Beth-togarmah from the far north with all its troops—the many nations with you.

7 Get ready; prepare yourself, you and all your company gathered around you; you will be their guard. 8 After a long time you will be summoned. In the latter years you will enter a land that has recovered from war, whose people were gathered from many nations to the mountains of Israel, which had long been desolate. They had been brought out from the nations, and all now dwell securely. 9 You and all your troops, and many peoples with you will go up, advancing like a thunderstorm; you will be like a cloud covering the land.

What is the context?

After the promises of restored life, a reunited people and God’s lasting presence, Ezekiel sees a great threat approaching. Gog leads a force drawn from many peoples against Israel, whose people have been gathered from the nations and are living securely.

The Lord names Gog as His opponent. Although Gog commands a huge army, the Lord describes bringing him out with hooks in his jaws. The invader’s strength will not place him beyond God’s reach.

The BSB calls Gog the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal. The Hebrew word רֹאשׁ, rosh (rohsh), can mean head or chief; see Brown–Driver–Briggs. Some translations instead treat Rosh as another name in the list. This difference matters when identifying the places, but neither translation by itself supplies a map of modern countries.

Gog and Magog are names in the prophecy, not a ready-made key to current events. Brown–Driver–Briggs lists Gog as a personal name with an unknown root; its Magog entry discusses a territory with uncertain identification. The passage’s clear emphasis is the gathering of an overwhelming force against a restored people.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads this as an attack in the latter days that God defeats. The USCCB notes understand Gog as a symbolic final enemy whose defeat strengthens Israel’s hope.

Walvoord distinguishes Ezekiel’s invasion from the attack in Revelation 20, placing Ezekiel’s battle before Christ’s thousand-year reign. Other Christian readings connect the two more closely. In The City of God, book 20, chapter 11, Augustine understands Revelation’s Gog and Magog as the final worldwide assault on the church, rather than two particular nations attacking one geographical city.

These are different readings of the scope and timing. They should be tested against both passages, without treating one teacher’s timetable as the shared Christian view.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 20:7–10 explicitly uses the names Gog and Magog for nations deceived by Satan and gathered against God’s people. Fire consumes the attackers, and the devil who deceived them is finally judged.

The connection is clear even though Christians differ over whether John describes the same battle or uses Ezekiel’s names for a later attack. Revelation carries the hope beyond the defeat of a threatening army to the defeat of the deceiver himself. Opposition to God’s people does not have the last word.
