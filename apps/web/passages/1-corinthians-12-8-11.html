Passage

1 Corinthians 12:8-11

Who decides which gifts each person receives?

Berean Standard Bible

8 To one there is given through the Spirit the message of wisdom, to another the message of knowledge by the same Spirit, 9 to another faith by the same Spirit, to another gifts of healing by that one Spirit, 10 to another the working of miracles, to another prophecy, to another distinguishing between spirits, to another speaking in various tongues, and to still another the interpretation of tongues. 11 All these are the work of one and the same Spirit, who apportions them to each one as He determines.

What is the context?

Paul gives examples of the variety he has just described: messages of wisdom and knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, distinguishing between spirits, tongues and their interpretation. He repeatedly names the same Spirit as their source, then states that the Spirit distributes them as He determines.

The faith listed as one person’s gift cannot simply mean that only some members believe in Christ. It points to a particular exercise of trust within a community of believers. The list likewise distinguishes speaking in tongues from interpreting them. Not everyone is given the same contribution.

Paul does not define every gift in detail here. He will discuss prophecy and tongues more fully in chapter 14. Wisdom and knowledge should not be assigned rigid modern job descriptions from these verses alone, and this list should be read alongside the different examples later in the chapter and in Romans 12:6–8.

The point is the Spirit’s purposeful giving for the common good. These abilities do not become personal possessions to control at will, or grounds for deciding that one recipient matters more than another.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that the gifts Paul describes come from God and serve His people. They differ over which continue in the same form today.

The Assemblies of God affirms prophecy for the church today, with every claim tested and no modern prophecy carrying Scripture’s authority; see its 2025 position paper. The Catholic Catechism 799–801 likewise recognizes extraordinary as well as ordinary gifts, requiring discernment and their use in love under the church’s shepherds.

The Reformed Westminster Confession 1.1 teaches that the former ways of revealing God’s will have ceased now that it has been committed to Scripture. That statement should not be expanded into a claim that God can no longer perform a miracle. Dallas Theological Seminary’s statement, Article XVI, more specifically holds that certain revelatory signs belonged to the apostolic period, while explicitly allowing that God performs miracles in every age. This is that institution’s teaching, not the position of every evangelical Christian.

Even descriptions of individual gifts vary. Wesley’s note on verse 9 allows healing to work alongside natural remedies. It would therefore be inaccurate to describe every Christian reading as requiring the same account of how healing occurs.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Acts 4:29–31, the church asks God for boldness to speak His word and for healing and signs through Jesus’ name. The gifts are placed alongside the mission of bearing witness to Him, rather than attracting attention to the people who receive them.

That setting helps explain their larger purpose. The Spirit equips people so Christ is made known and His people are helped. Whatever conclusions readers reach about gifts today, Paul’s emphasis challenges the use of spiritual ability as a measure of personal importance.
