Passage

Nehemiah 13:1-3

Why does another reading of the Law lead to separation?

Berean Standard Bible

1 At that time the Book of Moses was read aloud in the hearing of the people, and in it they found the passage stating that no Ammonite or Moabite should ever enter the assembly of God, 2 because they had not met the Israelites with food and water, but had hired Balaam to call down a curse against them (although our God had turned the curse into a blessing). 3 As soon as the people heard this law, they excluded from Israel all of foreign descent.

What is the context?

A public reading brings the people to the restriction concerning Ammonites and Moabites in Deuteronomy 23:3–5. The reason recalled is their failure to bring food and water and their hiring of Balaam to curse Israel. God had turned the curse into a blessing.

The people respond by excluding those of foreign descent from Israel. The passage presents this as a response to the Law they have heard, continuing the book’s concern that the community’s life be shaped by God’s instruction.

The wider scriptural setting also includes Ruth, a Moabite woman who commits herself to Israel’s people and God and becomes part of the family line recorded in Matthew 1:5. Her story matters when considering what Scripture says about foreigners and belonging.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Gill understands entering the assembly here in connection with marriage and reads the separation in that setting. Henry emphasizes the distinction between Israel and the surrounding nations and the way hearing Scripture exposes neglected duties. These readings focus on the community’s covenant responsibilities; the passage does not describe every possible case involving a foreigner.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ephesians 2:11–22 explicitly addresses Gentiles once separated from Israel’s commonwealth and the covenants of promise. Through Christ’s blood, those who were far away are brought near.

Paul describes Jews and Gentiles reconciled to God in one body through the cross, with access to the Father by one Spirit. The larger story of belonging therefore reaches its center in Christ, who makes those formerly called foreigners members of God’s household.
