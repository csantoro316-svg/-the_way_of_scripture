Passage

Ezekiel 33:10-20

Is it too late to turn back when our sins are heavy upon us?

Berean Standard Bible

10 Now as for you, son of man, tell the house of Israel that this is what they have said: ‘Our transgressions and our sins are heavy upon us, and we are wasting away because of them! How can we live?’ 11 Say to them: ‘As surely as I live, declares the Lord GOD, I take no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but rather that the wicked should turn from their ways and live. Turn! Turn from your evil ways! For why should you die, O house of Israel?’ 12 Therefore, son of man, say to your people: ‘The righteousness of the righteous man will not deliver him in the day of his transgression; neither will the wickedness of the wicked man cause him to stumble on the day he turns from his wickedness. Nor will the righteous man be able to survive by his righteousness on the day he sins.’ 13 If I tell the righteous man that he will surely live, but he then trusts in his righteousness and commits iniquity, then none of his righteous works will be remembered; he will die because of the iniquity he has committed.

14 But if I tell the wicked man, ‘You will surely die,’ and he turns from his sin and does what is just and right— 15 if he restores a pledge, makes restitution for what he has stolen, and walks in the statutes of life without practicing iniquity—then he will surely live; he will not die. 16 None of the sins he has committed will be held against him. He has done what is just and right; he will surely live.

17 Yet your people say, ‘The way of the Lord is not just.’ But it is their way that is not just. 18 If a righteous man turns from his righteousness and commits iniquity, he will die for it. 19 But if a wicked man turns from his wickedness and does what is just and right, he will live because of this. 20 Yet you say, ‘The way of the Lord is not just.’ But I will judge each of you according to his ways, O house of Israel.”

What is the context?

The people ask how they can live when their sins are weighing them down. The Lord answers with an appeal to turn: He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but desires that they turn from their ways and live.

The warning challenges two kinds of confidence. A righteous past cannot be used as protection for present wrongdoing, and a wicked past does not make turning back useless. The Lord describes repentance through concrete actions, including returning a pledge and making restitution for robbery.

The people call His way unjust. He answers that their own way is unjust and that He will judge each according to his ways. The call reaches both the person who despairs of mercy and the person who treats earlier good deeds as permission to sin.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry distinguishes the city’s determined ruin from the final state of individual people, emphasizing God’s willingness to show mercy. Gill reads the failed righteous person as someone trusting his own righteousness, rather than someone made righteous through Christ. Cambridge emphasizes that a sinful past need not make the future hopeless.

Christians differ in their wider teaching about falling from grace. The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 17, and the 1689 Baptist Confession, chapter 17, teach that those God truly calls and sanctifies will be preserved to the end, while serious sin can bring grave harm and discipline.

Methodist Article XII teaches that people may depart from grace after receiving the Holy Spirit and may rise again through grace and repentance. The Catholic Church teaches that grave sin committed with full knowledge and deliberate consent can result in loss of the state of grace; see the Catechism, paragraphs 1857–1861.

Those documents state the traditions’ wider teachings, rather than serving as official commentaries on this passage. The immediate appeal is clear across these disagreements: turn from wrongdoing, seek the Lord’s mercy, and do not rest in self-confidence.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Luke 19:1–10 shows the call taking visible shape in Zacchaeus. Welcoming Jesus is followed by a commitment to give to the poor and repay those he has cheated. Jesus announces salvation for his house and says that the Son of Man came to seek and save the lost.

Luke does not explicitly quote Ezekiel 33. The connection is the Lord’s welcome to the lost together with a real change in the way others are treated. Restitution appears as part of that turning, not a reason to imagine that a past full of wrong has put someone beyond the reach of mercy.
