Passage

Exodus 13:1-16

Why were the firstborn to be set apart for God?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then the LORD said to Moses, 2 “Consecrate to Me every firstborn male. The firstborn from every womb among the Israelites belongs to Me, both of man and beast.” 3 So Moses told the people, “Remember this day, the day you came out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery; for the LORD brought you out of it by the strength of His hand. And nothing leavened shall be eaten. 4 Today, in the month of Abib, you are leaving. 5 And when the LORD brings you into the land of the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Hivites, and Jebusites—the land He swore to your fathers that He would give you, a land flowing with milk and honey—you shall keep this service in this month. 6 For seven days you are to eat unleavened bread, and on the seventh day there shall be a feast to the LORD. 7 Unleavened bread shall be eaten during those seven days. Nothing leavened may be found among you, nor shall leaven be found anywhere within your borders. 8 And on that day you are to explain to your son, ‘This is because of what the LORD did for me when I came out of Egypt.’ 9 It shall be a sign for you on your hand and a reminder on your forehead that the Law of the LORD is to be on your lips. For with a mighty hand the LORD brought you out of Egypt. 10 Therefore you shall keep this statute at the appointed time year after year.

11 And after the LORD brings you into the land of the Canaanites and gives it to you, as He swore to you and your fathers, 12 you are to present to the LORD the firstborn male of every womb. All the firstborn males of your livestock belong to the LORD. 13 You must redeem every firstborn donkey with a lamb, and if you do not redeem it, you are to break its neck. And every firstborn of your sons you must redeem. 14 In the future, when your son asks you, ‘What does this mean?’ you are to tell him, ‘With a mighty hand the LORD brought us out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. 15 And when Pharaoh stubbornly refused to let us go, the LORD killed every firstborn in the land of Egypt, both of man and beast. This is why I sacrifice to the LORD the firstborn male of every womb, but I redeem all the firstborn of my sons.’ 16 So it shall serve as a sign on your hand and a symbol on your forehead, for with a mighty hand the LORD brought us out of Egypt.”

What is the context?

God commands Israel to consecrate its firstborn males—to set them apart as belonging to Him. The command is joined with another explanation of the rescue from Egypt, the annual observance of unleavened bread and the responsibility to tell children what the LORD has done.

The firstborn are to remind the people of the night when Egypt’s firstborn died and Israel was brought out. The animals offered to God and the sons who are redeemed keep that event before each household. The children’s question is to be answered with the story of deliverance.

The Hebrew word for redeem is פָּדָה, padah (pah-DAH), meaning to ransom; Brown–Driver–Briggs includes these verses under redemption for an assessed price. Here a donkey is to be redeemed with a lamb, and sons must be redeemed. Numbers 18:15–16 later specifies a payment for the redemption of the firstborn son.

The account describes remembrance as a sign on the hand and a reminder on the forehead, with the LORD’s law on their lips. The rescue is to remain part of what they remember, speak about and pass on. These commands are carried into their life in the land God has promised.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The firstborn command is read as an acknowledgment that the people belong to the God who delivered them. Calvin and Wesley both connect the consecration with Israel’s preservation and its responsibility to honor the LORD.

The language about the hand and forehead receives different applications. In the Orthodox Jewish practice described by Chabad, these passages are included in tefillin: small leather boxes containing Scripture, worn on the arm and head. Their explanation joins the physical practice with dedication of thought and action to God. Wesley understands the wording as a proverbial expression for keeping the rescue continually in mind. The Catholic USCCB notes read the feast itself as a visible reminder and also acknowledge the Jewish practice.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Luke 2:22–24 explicitly recalls the command about consecrating the firstborn when Mary and Joseph bring Jesus to Jerusalem to present Him to the Lord. Jesus enters the life of Israel within the law and observances given to the people God redeemed.

Galatians 4:4–7 gives the larger purpose: God sent His Son, born of a woman and under the law, to redeem those under the law so they might receive adoption as His children. The Son comes into Israel’s story of redemption and brings people into the fuller relationship with God that Paul describes.
