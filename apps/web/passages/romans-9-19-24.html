Passage

Romans 9:19-24

What does the picture of the potter and clay explain?

Berean Standard Bible

19 One of you will say to me, “Then why does God still find fault? For who can resist His will?” 20 But who are you, O man, to talk back to God? Shall what is formed say to Him who formed it, “Why did You make me like this?” 21 Does not the potter have the right to make from the same lump of clay one vessel for special occasions and another for common use?

22 What if God, intending to show His wrath and make His power known, bore with great patience the vessels of His wrath, prepared for destruction? 23 What if He did this to make the riches of His glory known to the vessels of His mercy, whom He prepared in advance for glory— 24 including us, whom He has called not only from the Jews, but also from the Gentiles?

What is the context?

Paul voices a further objection: if no one can resist God’s will, why does God still find fault? His response uses the picture of a maker and what he makes. Clay cannot place the potter under its authority; the Creator has rights over His creation.

He then asks readers to consider God bearing patiently with vessels of wrath and making known the riches of His glory to vessels of mercy. The language includes judgment, patience and mercy. Verse 23 explicitly says God prepared the vessels of mercy beforehand for glory.

Verse 24 identifies the people Paul has in view: those God has called from both Jews and Gentiles. That brings the discussion back to the question running through the chapter—how God’s purpose is unfolding among Israel and the nations.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readings differ over what determines the two groups. Calvin’s comments on verses 22–23 connect both their outcomes with God’s prior decision, while distinguishing mercy from deserved judgment. This fits the Reformed emphasis on God’s choice described in the Westminster Confession, chapter 3.

Wesley’s notes on verses 21–24 identify the groups through faith and persistent unbelief. He reads preparation for destruction in terms of continued refusal of mercy, while affirming that God prepares believers for glory by grace. The readings differ over the place of human response.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The potter picture draws on Isaiah 29:15–16 and Isaiah 45:9–13, where people are warned against treating their Maker as though He lacks understanding or authority. Isaiah 45 also places the image beside God’s purpose to restore His exiles.

Paul uses that familiar picture while describing a people called from both Jews and Gentiles. His explanation of mercy has a wider horizon than any one person’s status: God is forming a people who receive and display the riches of His mercy.
