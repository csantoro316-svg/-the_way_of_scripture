Passage

Isaiah 16:1-14

Can proud Moab find refuge under a just ruler?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Send the tribute lambs to the ruler of the land, from Sela in the desert to the mount of Daughter Zion. 2 Like fluttering birds pushed out of the nest, so are the daughters of Moab at the fords of the Arnon: 3 “Give us counsel; render a decision. Shelter us at noonday with shade as dark as night. Hide the refugees; do not betray the one who flees. 4 Let my fugitives stay with you; be a refuge for Moab from the destroyer.” When the oppressor has gone, destruction has ceased, and the oppressors have vanished from the land, 5 in loving devotion a throne will be established in the tent of David. A judge seeking justice and hastening righteousness will sit on it in faithfulness.

6 We have heard of Moab’s pomposity, his exceeding pride and conceit, his overflowing arrogance. But his boasting is empty. 7 Therefore let Moab wail; let them wail together for Moab. Moan for the raisin cakes of Kir-hareseth, you who are utterly stricken. 8 For the fields of Heshbon have withered, along with the grapevines of Sibmah. The rulers of the nations have trampled its choicest vines, which had reached as far as Jazer and spread toward the desert. Their shoots had spread out and passed over the sea.

9 So I weep with Jazer for the vines of Sibmah; I drench Heshbon and Elealeh with my tears. Triumphant shouts have fallen silent over your summer fruit and your harvest. 10 Joy and gladness are removed from the orchard; no one sings or shouts in the vineyards. No one tramples the grapes in the winepresses; I have put an end to the cheering. 11 Therefore my heart laments for Moab like a harp, my inmost being for Kir-heres. 12 When Moab appears on the high place, when he wearies himself and enters his sanctuary to pray, it will do him no good.

13 This is the message that the LORD spoke earlier concerning Moab. 14 And now the LORD says, “In three years, as a hired worker counts the years, Moab’s splendor will become an object of contempt, with all her many people. And those who are left will be few and feeble.”

What is the context?

The message to Moab turns to tribute, counsel and refuge. Lambs are to be sent toward Zion, and the daughters of Moab are pictured as birds pushed from their nest. The request is urgent: hide the refugees and do not betray the person who flees.

In the BSB, the appeal in verse 4 asks for refuge for Moab. It is followed by the prospect of oppression ending and a throne established in loving devotion in David’s house. The ruler is marked by faithfulness, justice and readiness to do what is right.

Then Moab’s excessive pride and empty boasting come back into view. Vineyards are trampled, harvest celebrations fall silent, and the speaker weeps and laments for the devastated country. Turning wearily to its sanctuary does not bring Moab the help it seeks.

The final verses distinguish an earlier message from a fresh announcement: within three years, counted as carefully as a hired worker counts a term of work, Moab’s splendor will be brought low. Those who remain will be few and weak. The judgment has an announced limit in time, even though the passage does not name its starting year.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ over who is asking whom for shelter. The Cambridge commentary reads the speech as Moab appealing to Judah for protection. Wesley and Gill, following a different reading of verse 4, understand it as a call for Moab to shelter Israelite fugitives. The difference affects the direction of the appeal; both explanations center on protection for people driven from home.

The throne in David’s house is also read at more than one level. Henry and Gill connect it with Hezekiah, while seeing a greater fulfillment in Christ’s just rule. The Cambridge commentary recognizes the language of the promised king but reads its place in the speech as an appeal to the Davidic dynasty.

Henry reads the later mourning as real concern over a country’s desolation, alongside the warning that pride refuses needed counsel. The closing three-year limit is understood by Wesley and Gill as a definite period, though they discuss different possible historical settings rather than agreeing on one exact campaign.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise of a faithful ruler in David’s house belongs to the wider hope taken up in Luke 1:30–33, where Gabriel announces that Jesus will receive David’s throne and reign forever. This connects the theme of righteous Davidic rule with Christ without claiming that Luke directly quotes Isaiah 16.

An earlier story also provides a useful parallel to Moab’s need for refuge. In Ruth 2:10–12, a Moabite woman who has joined Israel receives kindness and is described as taking refuge under the Lord’s wings. Ruth lived before Isaiah, so her story is an illustration of refuge, not a fulfillment of this prophecy.

Matthew 1:1–6 names Ruth in the family line leading to David and ultimately to Jesus. Scripture’s larger account therefore includes a woman from Moab receiving refuge and becoming part of the family through which the promised King comes.
