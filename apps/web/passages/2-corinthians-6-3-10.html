Passage

2 Corinthians 6:3-10

What does faithful service look like under pressure?

Berean Standard Bible

3 We put no obstacle in anyone’s way, so that no one can discredit our ministry. 4 Rather, as servants of God we commend ourselves in every way: in great endurance; in troubles, hardships, and calamities; 5 in beatings, imprisonments, and riots; in labor, sleepless nights, and hunger;

6 in purity, knowledge, patience, and kindness; in the Holy Spirit and in sincere love; 7 in truthful speech and in the power of God; with the weapons of righteousness in the right hand and in the left;

8 through glory and dishonor, slander and praise; viewed as imposters, yet genuine; 9 unknown, yet well-known; dying, and yet we live on; punished, yet not killed; 10 sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; poor, yet making many rich; having nothing, and yet possessing everything.

What is the context?

Paul describes how he and his companions carry out the appeal. They seek to put no obstacle in anyone’s way that would discredit the ministry. Their way of serving is meant to support the message rather than undermine it.

The list begins with great endurance. The Greek word is hypomonē (hoo-poh-moh-NAY), ὑπομονή: steady endurance that holds to its purpose through trials; see Thayer’s entry. Paul names troubles, beatings, imprisonment, sleepless nights and hunger. He is describing faithfulness through hardship, not an impressive life free of it.

He also names purity, knowledge, patience, kindness, the Holy Spirit, sincere love and truthful speech. Enduring pain is not his only evidence of service. The character shown within the difficulties matters too.

The closing contrasts distinguish outward impressions from what is true of the workers in God’s service. They can be called imposters while remaining genuine, be sorrowful while rejoicing, and be poor while enriching others. Paul does not admit to fraud or promise that everyone they help will become financially wealthy.

The picture holds costly circumstances and God’s resources together. It gives neither a guarantee that faithful people will avoid suffering nor a rule that suffering by itself proves someone is trustworthy.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand this as a description of sincere ministry sustained by God through hardship, rather than a claim that outward success proves faithfulness. Wesley’s notes on verses 3–10 and the USCCB notes on verses 3–10 both connect the trials, the workers’ character and the contrast between appearances and their true standing. Rejoicing does not erase the sorrow Paul also acknowledges.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Matthew 5:10–16, Jesus speaks of His disciples facing persecution for His sake and of their good deeds bringing glory to their Father. The people who suffer for belonging to Him are also called to be light in the world.

That connection gives the catalog here its larger purpose. God makes His character and message visible through people whose conduct remains marked by truth, kindness and love under pressure. The aim reaches beyond the worker’s reputation to others seeing reason to glorify the Father.
