Passage

Isaiah 1:21-31

How can a corrupt city become faithful again?

Berean Standard Bible

21 See how the faithful city has become a harlot! She once was full of justice; righteousness resided within her, but now only murderers! 22 Your silver has become dross; your fine wine is diluted with water. 23 Your rulers are rebels, friends of thieves. They all love bribes and chasing after rewards. They do not defend the fatherless, and the plea of the widow never comes before them.

24 Therefore the Lord GOD of Hosts, the Mighty One of Israel, declares: “Ah, I will be relieved of My foes and avenge Myself on My enemies. 25 I will turn My hand against you; I will thoroughly purge your dross; I will remove all your impurities. 26 I will restore your judges as at first, and your counselors as at the beginning. After that you will be called the City of Righteousness, the Faithful City.” 27 Zion will be redeemed with justice, her repentant ones with righteousness. 28 But rebels and sinners will together be shattered, and those who forsake the LORD will perish.

29 Surely you will be ashamed of the sacred oaks in which you have delighted; you will be embarrassed by the gardens that you have chosen. 30 For you will become like an oak whose leaves are withered, like a garden without water. 31 The strong man will become tinder and his work will be a spark; both will burn together, with no one to quench the flames.

What is the context?

Jerusalem is pictured as a once-faithful woman who has become unfaithful. Where justice should live, there are murderers; rulers accept bribes, keep company with thieves and ignore those who need protection.

The images of polluted silver and watered-down wine show the loss of what once made the city sound. God announces that He will remove the impurities and restore judges and counselors, so that Jerusalem is again known as faithful and righteous.

The word for being redeemed in verse 27 comes from Hebrew פָּדָה, padah, pronounced pah-DAH. Brown–Driver–Briggs gives the sense of ransom or release and specifically lists this verse. Here Zion’s rescue is joined with justice and righteousness; the verse does not yet describe a payment or name who makes one.

The promise of restoration stands beside judgment on those who continue to forsake God. The sacred trees and gardens that attracted them become images of their own withering. The chapter ends with a fire that consumes the strong person and the work in which that person trusted.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readings differ over the reach of the promised restoration. The NABRE notes emphasize a purifying judgment on the city and its leaders, restoring just government. Henry finds the fullest meaning in God redeeming His people through Christ’s righteousness, death and grace.

Gill also reads the promise through Christ and the community formed by the gospel, connecting the restored judges with the apostles and later faithful teachers. These readings share the conviction that God brings the change; they differ over how the restored city and its leaders are related to the later work of Christ.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise of a purified, faithful city belongs to a larger biblical hope. Revelation 21:1–8, 22–27 describes the new Jerusalem where God dwells with His people, and where nothing unclean enters. Its life and light come from God and the Lamb.

The New Testament also gives a definite account of the cost of redemption: 1 Peter 1:17–23 says believers are redeemed through Christ’s precious blood and calls them to holy living and sincere love. These connections carry the themes of rescue, cleansing and faithfulness beyond the city’s immediate corruption, without turning every detail of Isaiah’s imagery into a separate prediction.
