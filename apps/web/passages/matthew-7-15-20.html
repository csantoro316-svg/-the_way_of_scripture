Passage

Matthew 7:15-20

Recognizing Teachers by Their Fruit

Berean Standard Bible

15 Beware of false prophets. They come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves. 16 By their fruit you will recognize them. Are grapes gathered from thornbushes, or figs from thistles?

17 Likewise, every good tree bears good fruit, but a bad tree bears bad fruit. 18 A good tree cannot bear bad fruit, and a bad tree cannot bear good fruit. 19 Every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire. 20 So then, by their fruit you will recognize them.

What is the context?

After warning about the way that leads to destruction, Jesus warns about people who may lead others there. False prophets appear like members of the flock but are inwardly dangerous. Their outward presentation does not settle whether they can be trusted.

The repeated direction is to recognize them by their fruit. A tree is known by what it produces. Grapes do not come from thornbushes, and figs do not come from thistles. The comparison moves attention from appearance to the actual result of a teacher's life and work.

Jesus also speaks of a fruitless tree being cut down and burned. The warning gives the question weight: the issue is more serious than a teacher's popularity or speaking ability. The following verses show that even striking religious claims cannot take the place of doing the Father's will.

Other New Testament passages also call for testing. 1 John 4:1–3 examines what a message confesses about Jesus, while 1 Thessalonians 5:19–22 calls believers to test what is said and hold to what is good. Such care belongs with the earlier warning against hypocritical judgment.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readers agree that appearances and religious claims are insufficient, while placing different emphasis on what the fruit reveals. Wesley on Matthew 7:15–19 emphasizes the teaching and whether it turns people toward God; he expressly allows that a morally flawed person may sometimes speak true teaching.

The US Catholic bishops' notes on Matthew 7:15–20 emphasize the quality of the prophets' deeds. These explanations place different emphasis on teaching and deeds, while both call for looking beyond an outwardly religious appearance.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God's purpose is to produce a changed life through union with Christ. John 15:1–8 uses another fruit picture: Jesus is the vine, the Father tends it, and the branches bear fruit by remaining in Him. Jesus connects that fruit with His Father's glory. This related image directs attention to the source of a fruitful life.

Galatians 5:22–25 describes the Spirit's fruit in qualities such as love, patience, kindness, and self-control. The larger purpose reaches beyond persuasive speech or a successful-looking ministry: God's Spirit forms people whose lives display the character of Christ.
