Passage

1 Samuel 30:7-20

How does care for an abandoned stranger help lead to rescue?

Berean Standard Bible

7 Then David said to Abiathar the priest, the son of Ahimelech, “Bring me the ephod.” So Abiathar brought it to him, 8 and David inquired of the LORD: “Should I pursue these raiders? Will I overtake them?” “Pursue them,” the LORD replied, “for you will surely overtake them and rescue the captives.” 9 So David and his six hundred men went to the Brook of Besor, where some stayed behind 10 because two hundred men were too exhausted to cross the brook. But David and four hundred men continued in pursuit.

11 Now his men found an Egyptian in the field and brought him to David. They gave the man water to drink and food to eat— 12 a piece of a fig cake and two clusters of raisins. So he ate and was revived, for he had not had any food or water for three days and three nights. 13 Then David asked him, “To whom do you belong, and where are you from?” “I am an Egyptian,” he replied, “the slave of an Amalekite. My master abandoned me three days ago when I fell ill. 14 We raided the Negev of the Cherethites, the territory of Judah, and the Negev of Caleb, and we burned down Ziklag.” 15 “Will you lead me to these raiders?” David asked. And the man replied, “Swear to me by God that you will not kill me or deliver me into the hand of my master, and I will lead you to them.”

16 So he led David down, and there were the Amalekites spread out over all the land, eating, drinking, and celebrating the great amount of plunder they had taken from the land of the Philistines and the land of Judah. 17 And David struck them down from twilight until the evening of the next day. Not a man escaped, except four hundred young men who fled, riding off on camels. 18 So David recovered everything the Amalekites had taken, including his two wives. 19 Nothing was missing, young or old, son or daughter, or any of the plunder the Amalekites had taken. David brought everything back. 20 And he took all the flocks and herds, which his men drove ahead of the other livestock, calling out, “This is David’s plunder!”

What is the context?

David asks Abiathar to bring the ephod and inquires of the LORD about pursuing the raiders. He receives the direction to pursue and the assurance that he will rescue the captives. Six hundred set out, but two hundred are too exhausted to cross the Brook of Besor. Four hundred continue.

They find an Egyptian in a field and give him food and water. After he revives, he explains that he is the slave of an Amalekite who abandoned him when he became ill. He had been with the raiders who burned Ziklag. He agrees to lead David to them if David swears not to kill him or return him to his master.

The raiders are celebrating their plunder when David reaches them. He attacks from twilight until the following evening. Four hundred young men escape on camels, but David recovers everyone and everything taken from his people, including his wives. The outcome matches the promise he received.

The recovered families are accompanied by flocks and herds taken from the raiders. The account now turns from winning the rescue to deciding how its benefits will be shared.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes David’s seeking guidance and his refusal to force exhausted men beyond their strength. Wesley likewise treats the return to inquiry as significant. The rescue begins with dependence rather than a claim that urgency leaves no room to ask God.

Henry and Wesley see God working through the abandoned Egyptian. His master’s cruelty leaves the very person who can guide David to the camp. The food and water are given before his information is recorded, allowing kindness a real place in the account.

Henry also compares David’s care for weaker followers with the care of Christ. The men who cannot continue are still his people. Their exhaustion will become the test of generosity after the battle.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Care for someone connected with the enemy becomes part of the rescue. David’s men do not leave the sick Egyptian where his master abandoned him. His life matters, and his help opens the way to the captives.

Luke 6:27–36 carries the wider theme into Jesus’ teaching: His followers are to do good beyond the circle of those who already love them, reflecting the Father’s mercy. The comparison concerns kindness across hostility, rather than approval of every action taken in ancient warfare.

The episode helps us see God’s purposes moving through care for an easily overlooked person. The stranger is not merely an obstacle on the way to helping David’s own people; tending his need becomes part of that help.
