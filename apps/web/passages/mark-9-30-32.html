Passage

Mark 9:30-32

The Disciples Still Do Not Understand

Berean Standard Bible

30 Going on from there, they passed through Galilee. But Jesus did not want anyone to know, 31 because He was teaching His disciples. He told them, “The Son of Man will be delivered into the hands of men. They will kill Him, and after three days He will rise.” 32 But they did not understand this statement, and they were afraid to ask Him about it.

What is the context?

Jesus and His disciples pass through Galilee, and He seeks privacy because He is teaching them. He repeats what He said after Peter's confession: the Son of Man will be handed over, killed, and raised after three days. The coming death continues to shape the instruction He gives them.

The expression about being delivered uses παραδίδωμι (paradidomi, pah-rah-DEE-doh-mee), meaning to hand over or give into another's power. The word can describe betrayal or delivery into custody; its meaning alone does not identify every person involved or explain all of God's purpose in the event.

The disciples do not understand and are afraid to ask. Mark tells us both things but does not state exactly why they are afraid. This leaves a striking contrast between Jesus' clear announcement and their difficulty receiving it.

The announcement is the second of three concentrated predictions in this part of Mark: 8:31, here, and 10:33–34. The next scene, in which the disciples dispute their greatness, further exposes the distance between His path and their concerns.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly understand this as Jesus preparing the disciples for His death and resurrection while their understanding remains incomplete. Calvin's discussion of this announcement and Wesley on Mark 9:32 connect their difficulty with expectations that do not yet make room for His suffering.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The handing over, death, and resurrection become part of the church's explanation of God's saving love. Romans 8:31–34 says that the Father gives His Son for us, and that the Christ who dies is raised, exalted, and intercedes for His people. That is a later, fuller explanation of the mission being announced here; the Greek verb alone is not proof of all those connections.

Paul then speaks of confidence in Christ's love even amid trouble, persecution, and death (Romans 8:35–39). The announcement that frightens the disciples leads toward hope grounded in what God does through His Son. The suffering is real, but it does not have the last word.
