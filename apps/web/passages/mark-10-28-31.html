Passage

Mark 10:28-31

A New Family, Persecution, and Life to Come

Berean Standard Bible

28 Peter began to say to Him, “Look, we have left everything and followed You.” 29 “Truly I tell you,” Jesus replied, “no one who has left home or brothers or sisters or mother or father or children or fields for My sake and for the gospel 30 will fail to receive a hundredfold in the present age—houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and fields, along with persecutions—and in the age to come, eternal life. 31 But many who are first will be last, and the last will be first.”

What is the context?

Peter responds that the disciples have left everything and followed Jesus. His statement stands beside the wealthy man's unwillingness to let go. Jesus answers with a promise to those who lose home, family ties, or fields for His sake and for the gospel.

The promise has two time settings. In the present age, Jesus describes multiplied homes, family relationships, and provision, together with persecutions. In the age to come, He promises eternal life. Keeping both settings in view prevents the present care and the future hope from being confused.

The family language recalls Mark 3:31–35, where Jesus identifies those who do God's will as His brother, sister, and mother. The promise makes sense within a new community that opens its homes and shares what it has, even while its members may face rejection and suffering.

The final saying about first and last challenges ordinary judgments about importance and advantage. It also keeps the disciples' own sacrifices from becoming a claim to superior standing. Following Jesus changes where they look for security and how they regard one another.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see both the cost of following Jesus and His assurance that those losses are not overlooked. Wesley on Mark 10:30 understands the multiplied return in terms of greater value and happiness, rather than an identical replacement of every possession, and keeps the accompanying persecutions in view. The promise is commonly read as abundant care and future life, rather than a guaranteed financial return for a gift or a risk-free life.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 2:41–47 shows a concrete expression of the new community: people gathered through the gospel devote themselves to teaching, fellowship, meals, and prayer, open their homes, and share possessions with those in need. This is a supported connection with Jesus' family-and-provision promise. God supplies care through people who learn to treat one another as belonging together.

The future promise has the same foundation as the present community. 1 Peter 1:3–9 joins new birth through Jesus' resurrection with an enduring inheritance, while acknowledging painful trials now. The disciples are learning to measure loss and gain within that larger hope: Christ's resurrection opens a future that persecution and the loss of earthly security cannot finally take away.
