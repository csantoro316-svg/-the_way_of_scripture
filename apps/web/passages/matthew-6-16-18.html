Passage

Matthew 6:16-18

Fasting for the Father to See

Berean Standard Bible

16 When you fast, do not be somber like the hypocrites, for they disfigure their faces to show men they are fasting. Truly I tell you, they already have their full reward. 17 But when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, 18 so that your fasting will not be obvious to men, but only to your Father, who is unseen. And your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you.

What is the context?

Fasting is the third example of devotion Jesus addresses after giving and prayer. The same question runs through all three: is the person seeking God or other people's admiration? The fast He describes here has become a public display. A somber, neglected appearance makes sure other people notice the sacrifice.

Jesus says that those who seek this attention already have their reward. He then directs His hearers to anoint their heads and wash their faces, so their fasting will not be obvious. The Father sees what others do not. The value of the devotion is not measured by how much discomfort the person manages to show.

The passage speaks of fasting as a practice His hearers know. Its immediate concern is their purpose and conduct while fasting. It does not give a fasting calendar or specify a number of hours here.

The earlier Scriptures also question a fast that leaves the heart unchanged. In Isaiah 58:3–12, people fast while continuing to oppress and quarrel. God calls for freedom from oppression and practical care for hungry and vulnerable people. Outward self-denial cannot take the place of a life turned toward God and the good of others.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand these verses as calling for fasting directed toward God rather than a display of religious effort. Wesley's notes on Matthew 6:16–17 treat fasting as an established practice and the instructions about appearance as a call to dress normally; the Catholic Catechism, 1430, likewise emphasizes that fasting and other outward acts require a heart turning toward God.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus connects fasting with His own mission in Mark 2:18–20. He compares His presence with His disciples to a bridegroom's presence at a wedding and says they will fast when He is taken from them. Their devotion belongs within their relationship with Him, including the time after His departure.

The early church's fasting also accompanies the work of spreading the gospel. In Acts 13:1–4, worship and fasting surround the Spirit's call to send Barnabas and Saul. In Acts 14:21–23, prayer and fasting accompany the appointment of elders and entrusting the churches to the Lord.

These accounts place fasting within dependence on God as He gathers and strengthens His people. The practice serves that relationship and mission. It is not presented as a performance that purchases God's guidance or forces Him to act.
