Passage

Ezekiel 29:8-16

What do Egypt’s forty years of desolation and its return mean?

Berean Standard Bible

8 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: I will bring a sword against you and cut off from you man and beast. 9 The land of Egypt will become a desolate wasteland. Then they will know that I am the LORD. Because you said, ‘The Nile is mine; I made it,’ 10 therefore I am against you and against your rivers. I will turn the land of Egypt into a ruin, a desolate wasteland from Migdol to Syene, and as far as the border of Cush. 11 No foot of man or beast will pass through, and it will be uninhabited for forty years. 12 I will make the land of Egypt a desolation among desolate lands, and her cities will lie desolate for forty years among the ruined cities. And I will disperse the Egyptians among the nations and scatter them throughout the countries.

13 For this is what the Lord GOD says: At the end of forty years I will gather the Egyptians from the nations to which they were scattered. 14 I will restore Egypt from captivity and bring them back to the land of Pathros, the land of their origin. There they will be a lowly kingdom. 15 Egypt will be the lowliest of kingdoms and will never again exalt itself above the nations. For I will diminish Egypt so that it will never again rule over the nations. 16 Egypt will never again be an object of trust for the house of Israel, but will remind them of their iniquity in turning to the Egyptians. Then they will know that I am the Lord GOD.”

What is the context?

The Lord answers Egypt’s claim to have made the Nile with a threat against its land and rivers. The announced devastation reaches from Migdol to Syene and the border of Cush, with neither people nor animals passing through for forty years.

The people will be scattered, but the announcement does not end there. At the end of the forty years, the Lord will gather the Egyptians and restore them to Pathros, the land of their origin.

The restored kingdom will be lowly rather than dominant. Egypt will no longer be Israel’s object of trust, drawing it away from the Lord. The passage therefore holds together a severe humiliation, a return and the ending of a destructive dependence.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads the forty years as an actual period during the Babylonian era, ending with Cyrus. Jamieson–Fausset–Brown gives more weight to the picture of a wilderness period of political and social humiliation, rather than requiring that no person or animal literally set foot in the land.

A future fulfillment has also been considered. A Lamb & Lion discussion in The Lamplighter raises that only as a slight possibility connected with a future drought, while favoring an ancient judgment. It does not present a settled future timetable.

These readings differ over the period and how literally to take the description. The text clearly announces the fall of Egypt’s pride, a return after judgment and a reduced kingdom. An exact historical date range should not be treated as established simply because a commentator proposes it.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah 19:19–25 adds a wider promise for Egypt: the Lord will make Himself known, hear its cry, and bring people to worship Him. The picture culminates in Egypt and Assyria sharing blessing with Israel.

Ezekiel’s lowly kingdom and Isaiah’s worshiping people should not be flattened into a single detailed calendar. Together they show that the humbling of a proud nation need not be the end of God’s purposes for its people. The larger biblical horizon includes the Lord healing and receiving those who turn to Him.
