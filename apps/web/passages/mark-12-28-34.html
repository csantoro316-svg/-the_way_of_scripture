Passage

Mark 12:28-34

What matters most to God?

Berean Standard Bible

28 Now one of the scribes had come up and heard their debate. Noticing how well Jesus had answered them, he asked Him, “Which commandment is the most important of all?” 29 Jesus replied, “This is the most important: ‘Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One. 30 Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength.’ 31 The second is this: ‘Love your neighbor as yourself.’ No other commandment is greater than these.”

32 “Right, Teacher,” the scribe replied. “You have stated correctly that God is One and there is no other but Him, 33 and to love Him with all your heart and with all your understanding and with all your strength, and to love your neighbor as yourself. This is more important than all burnt offerings and sacrifices.” 34 When Jesus saw that the man had answered wisely, He said, “You are not far from the kingdom of God.” And no one dared to question Him any further.

What is the context?

A scribe hears Jesus answer the Sadducees and recognizes that He has answered well. His question brings the discussion back to the heart of the law: which commandment matters most? Mark presents a thoughtful exchange, ending with Jesus recognizing the man’s understanding. We should let that description stand rather than assume that every questioner has the same motive.

Jesus begins with Deuteronomy 6:4–5 and adds Leviticus 19:18. The first passage calls Israel to love the one God with its whole life; the second requires love for one’s neighbor. The opening Hebrew word in Deuteronomy is שְׁמַע (shema, shuh-MAH), meaning hear or listen. That is where the familiar name Shema comes from. The command calls for attention that reaches beyond words into the way a person lives. (Deuteronomy 6:4–9; Leviticus 19:18, 33–34; Brown–Driver–Briggs, שמע)

The scribe sees that love matters more than sacrifices. This agrees with the earlier warning that religious offerings cannot replace obedience. Jesus tells him he is near God’s kingdom, but Mark does not tell us what he does afterward. Understanding the right answer is important; the passage leaves his next response untold. (1 Samuel 15:22–23)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Across the major Christian traditions, these commands are understood as calling for wholehearted love for God and practical care for other people. Wesley explains them as embracing the person’s whole capacity to love and serve, with love taking priority over even costly sacrifices. (Wesley, notes on Mark 12:28–34)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The contrast with sacrifices points toward a larger question: how will God bring people into a faithful relationship with Him? Hebrews explains that repeated animal sacrifices could not finally remove sin, and that Jesus came to do God’s will through the offering of His own body. His obedience accomplishes what repeated offerings could not. (Hebrews 10:1–10)

The New Testament also shows where love for others begins: God loved us and sent His Son while we were still sinners. Those who receive that love are called to share it. The commands here therefore belong within God’s work of reconciling people to Himself and forming a people who care for one another. (Romans 5:6–11; 1 John 4:7–21)
