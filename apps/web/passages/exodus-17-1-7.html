Passage

Exodus 17:1-7

What did Israel’s demand for water reveal?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then the whole congregation of Israel left the Desert of Sin, moving from place to place as the LORD commanded. They camped at Rephidim, but there was no water for the people to drink. 2 So the people contended with Moses, “Give us water to drink.” “Why do you contend with me?” Moses replied. “Why do you test the LORD?” 3 But the people thirsted for water there, and they grumbled against Moses: “Why have you brought us out of Egypt—to make us and our children and livestock die of thirst?” 4 Then Moses cried out to the LORD, “What should I do with these people? A little more and they will stone me!”

5 And the LORD said to Moses, “Walk on ahead of the people and take some of the elders of Israel with you. Take along in your hand the staff with which you struck the Nile, and go. 6 Behold, I will stand there before you by the rock at Horeb. And when you strike the rock, water will come out of it for the people to drink.” So Moses did this in the sight of the elders of Israel. 7 He named the place Massah and Meribah because the Israelites quarreled, and because they tested the LORD, saying, “Is the LORD among us or not?”

What is the context?

Israel reaches Rephidim by following the LORD’s command, yet there is no water. Being on the commanded route has not removed every need or difficulty. The people turn on Moses, demanding water and accusing him of bringing their families and livestock out to die.

Moses cries to God because their anger threatens his life. God directs him to go ahead with some elders, taking the staff used to strike the Nile. The LORD promises to stand before him by the rock at Horeb. Moses strikes it as commanded, and water comes out before the elders as witnesses.

The names preserve the people’s response as well as the rescue. מַסָּה, Massah (mah-SAH), recalls testing; מְרִיבָה, Meribah (meh-ree-BAH), recalls quarreling. Brown–Driver–Briggs on Massah and Meribah connects the names with this incident. The question behind their complaint is whether the LORD is among them.

The preceding chapter described God testing whether Israel would follow His instructions. Here the people put God to the test, demanding proof of His presence despite the care they have already received. The water meets a real need, while the place names keep the failure of trust in view.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The shared reading sees both Israel’s distrust and God’s patient provision. Wesley and Calvin emphasize that the water comes through God’s power at His command, even while the people quarrel with His servant. The elders witness what He does, and the names preserve a warning for those who come after them.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

1 Corinthians 10:1–12 explicitly connects the wilderness provision with Christ: Paul identifies the spiritual rock as Christ. He also says these events were written as instruction and warning for the church. Receiving God’s gifts is meant to lead people to trust and obey Him.

Jesus’ invitation in John 7:37–39 carries the water picture into His gift to believers. He calls the thirsty to come to Him and drink, and John explains that the living water concerns the Spirit. The provision of water for physical life helps us understand the greater gift of life from God through Christ.

John Gill also draws a parallel between the rock being struck and Christ suffering for those He saves. Isaiah 53:4–6 describes the servant wounded for others, and 1 Peter 2:21–25 applies that suffering and healing to Christ. This is a further comparison alongside Paul’s explicit identification of the rock; Paul does not explain every action in the scene as a separate prediction.
