Passage

Titus 1:10-16

Why must harmful teaching be corrected?

Berean Standard Bible

10 For many are rebellious and full of empty talk and deception, especially those of the circumcision, 11 who must be silenced. For the sake of dishonorable gain, they undermine entire households and teach things they should not.

12 As one of their own prophets has said, “Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons.” 13 This testimony is true. Therefore rebuke them sternly, so that they will be sound in the faith 14 and will pay no attention to Jewish myths or to the commands of men who have rejected the truth.

15 To the pure, all things are pure; but to the defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure. Indeed, both their minds and their consciences are defiled. 16 They profess to know God, but by their actions they deny Him. They are detestable, disobedient, and unfit for any good deed.

What is the context?

The reason for leaders able to correct becomes clear: rebellious teachers are undermining households for dishonest gain. Paul singles out those of the circumcision as a particular concern, rather than saying every person of Jewish background is an opponent.

He quotes a severe saying about Cretans and applies it to the need for firm correction. The stated goal is that people become sound in faith instead of following myths and human commands that turn from the truth.

The closing contrast concerns purity and the life that reveals it. Outward religious claims do not make a corrupt mind and conscience clean. The opponents say they know God, but their actions deny that profession. The correction addresses a real danger to people’s faith and conduct.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Calvin understand the statement that all things are pure in the setting of food and purity restrictions. The point is not that every action becomes morally acceptable to a believer. They connect true purity with faith and the inner life, rather than reliance on religious restrictions alone.

The USCCB note similarly connects the opponents with ritual-purity requirements and harmful speculation. The shared emphasis is that religious claims must agree with truth and conduct. The aim of rebuke is sound faith, rather than humiliation as an end in itself.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Mark 7:14–23, Jesus locates defilement in the evil that comes from the heart rather than in food entering a person. That is a related teaching which helps explain why outward purity rules cannot cure a corrupt inner life.

Acts 10:34–48 gives the wider setting of God receiving people from the nations through Christ. Peter proclaims forgiveness through Jesus, and the Spirit is given to the Gentile hearers. God’s saving work calls people into faith and a new life; human barriers cannot supply or replace that cleansing.
