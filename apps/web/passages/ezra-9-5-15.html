Passage

Ezra 9:5-15

Why does Ezra confess the people’s guilt as our guilt?

Berean Standard Bible

5 At the evening offering, I got up from my humiliation with my tunic and cloak torn, and I fell on my knees, spread out my hands to the LORD my God, 6 and said: “O my God, I am ashamed and embarrassed to lift up my face to You, my God, because our iniquities are higher than our heads, and our guilt has reached the heavens. 7 From the days of our fathers to this day, our guilt has been great. Because of our iniquities, we and our kings and priests have been delivered into the hands of the kings of the earth and subjected to the sword and to captivity, to pillage and humiliation, as we are this day.

8 But now, for a brief moment, grace has come from the LORD our God to preserve for us a remnant and to give us a stake in His holy place. Even in our bondage, our God has given us new life and light to our eyes. 9 Though we are slaves, our God has not forsaken us in our bondage, but He has extended to us grace in the sight of the kings of Persia, giving us new life to rebuild the house of our God and repair its ruins, and giving us a wall of protection in Judah and Jerusalem.

10 And now, our God, what can we say after this? For we have forsaken the commandments 11 that You gave through Your servants the prophets, saying: ‘The land that you are entering to possess is a land polluted by the impurity of its peoples and the abominations with which they have filled it from end to end. 12 Now, therefore, do not give your daughters in marriage to their sons or take their daughters for your sons. Never seek their peace or prosperity, so that you may be strong and may eat the good things of the land, leaving it as an inheritance to your sons forever.’ 13 After all that has come upon us because of our evil deeds and our great guilt (though You, our God, have punished us less than our iniquities deserve and have given us such a remnant as this), 14 shall we again break Your commandments and intermarry with the peoples who commit these abominations? Would You not become so angry with us as to wipe us out, leaving no remnant or survivor? 15 O LORD, God of Israel, You are righteous! For we remain this day as a remnant. Here we are before You in our guilt, though because of it no one can stand before You.”

What is the context?

At the evening offering Ezra kneels and spreads his hands before God. He prays as part of the guilty community, using our and we rather than speaking only about other people’s wrongdoing.

He recalls the long history of sin and its consequences, but also the grace that has preserved a remnant and made a return possible. Even under Persian rule, the people have received help to rebuild God’s house and begin again.

That mercy makes renewed disobedience more troubling. Ezra acknowledges that God has punished them less than they deserved and ends by admitting that the people cannot defend themselves before Him. The prayer offers confession rather than an excuse.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill understand Ezra’s language as identifying himself with the nation and its guilt; it is not a claim that he personally entered one of the marriages being reported. Henry emphasizes shame over sin, recognition of past mercy and reliance upon God rather than a defense of the wrongdoing.

Wesley and Gill also understand the wall of protection in verse 9 as security through God’s care and Persian favor. They do not take it as proof that Jerusalem’s physical walls have already been rebuilt.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ezra’s prayer belongs to the biblical theme of a priest speaking before God on behalf of the people. The New Testament develops priestly intercession in Hebrews 7:23–28, where Jesus lives continually to intercede for those who draw near to God through Him.

The connection includes an important difference: Ezra prays from within a sinful community, while Hebrews describes Jesus as holy and undefiled, offering Himself for sin once for all. The connection is between intercession for guilty people and Christ’s enduring priesthood.
