Passage

Isaiah 4:1-6

How does the promise of shelter follow the city’s disgrace?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In that day seven women will take hold of one man and say, “We will eat our own bread and provide our own clothes. Just let us be called by your name. Take away our disgrace!”

2 On that day the Branch of the LORD will be beautiful and glorious, and the fruit of the land will be the pride and glory of Israel’s survivors. 3 Whoever remains in Zion and whoever is left in Jerusalem will be called holy— all in Jerusalem who are recorded among the living— 4 when the Lord has washed away the filth of the daughters of Zion and cleansed the bloodstains from the heart of Jerusalem by a spirit of judgment and a spirit of fire.

5 Then the LORD will create over all of Mount Zion and over her assemblies a cloud of smoke by day and a glowing flame of fire by night. For over all the glory there will be a canopy, 6 a shelter to give shade from the heat by day, and a refuge and hiding place from the storm and the rain.

What is the context?

The opening verse completes the picture of loss at the end of chapter 3. Seven women seek one man’s name while offering to provide their own food and clothing. Their appeal shows the desperation produced by the city’s disaster.

The tone then changes. The Branch of the Lord and the land’s fruit become the survivors’ glory. Those left in Zion are called holy after the Lord washes away the city’s filth and bloodshed through judgment and fire.

The Hebrew צֶמַח, tsemach, pronounced TSEH-makh, means sprout or growth. Brown–Driver–Briggs lists this verse under growth, while also recording the word’s use for a future ruler in other passages. The word alone does not settle whether the image here describes the land’s renewed growth or points to the coming Messiah.

The promise ends with cloud and fire over Zion’s assemblies and shelter from heat, storm and rain. These are pictures of God’s presence and protection for a people He has cleansed, following the earlier warnings about their exposure and shame.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ over the Branch. The NABRE notes recognize that the word can refer to the future Davidic king, but favor renewed growth here because it stands beside the land’s fruit. Henry and Wesley read the Branch as Christ, connecting the passage with His kingdom and the good He brings to His people.

The royal reading also draws on passages such as Jeremiah 23:5–6 and Zechariah 3:8–10, which use Branch language for God’s promised ruler or servant. These readings differ over the image’s immediate reference while recognizing that the cleansing and shelter come from the Lord.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The cloud and fire recall God guiding His people out of Egypt in Exodus 13:20–22. Isaiah uses that familiar picture of divine presence in a promise concerning Zion’s restored community.

A further parallel appears in Revelation 7:9–17. People from every nation stand before God and the Lamb, their robes washed in the Lamb’s blood. God spreads His tabernacle over them, and the Lamb shepherds them beyond hunger, thirst and scorching heat. The shared themes are cleansing, God dwelling with His people and the shelter He provides.
