Passage

Ezekiel 24:1-14

Why does Jerusalem become a boiling pot?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In the ninth year, on the tenth day of the tenth month, the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, write down today’s date, for on this very day the king of Babylon has laid siege to Jerusalem. 3 Now speak a parable to this rebellious house and tell them that this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Put the pot on the fire; put it on and pour in the water. 4 Put in the pieces of meat, every good piece— thigh and shoulder— fill it with choice bones. 5 Take the choicest of the flock and pile the fuel beneath it. Bring it to a boil and cook the bones in it.’

6 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Woe to the city of bloodshed, to the pot now rusted, whose rust will not come off! Empty it piece by piece; cast no lots for its contents. 7 For the blood she shed is still within her; she poured it out on the bare rock; she did not pour it on the ground to cover it with dust. 8 In order to stir up wrath and take vengeance, I have placed her blood on the bare rock, so that it would not be covered.’

9 Yes, this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Woe to the city of bloodshed! I, too, will pile the kindling high. 10 Pile on the logs and kindle the fire; cook the meat well and mix in the spices; let the bones be burned. 11 Set the empty pot on its coals until it becomes hot and its copper glows. Then its impurity will melt within; its rust will be consumed. 12 It has frustrated every effort; its thick rust has not been removed, even by the fire. 13 Because of the indecency of your uncleanness I tried to cleanse you, but you would not be purified from your filthiness. You will not be pure again until My wrath against you has subsided. 14 I, the LORD, have spoken; the time is coming, and I will act. I will not refrain or show pity, nor will I relent. I will judge you according to your ways and deeds,’ declares the Lord GOD.”

What is the context?

The Lord tells Ezekiel to record the date because Babylon has laid siege to Jerusalem that very day. Second Kings 25:1–12 records the siege beginning on the same day of the same month in Zedekiah’s ninth year, and describes the later famine, breach and burning of the city.

The Lord gives Ezekiel a parable about a cooking pot filled with choice meat and bones. The pot is Jerusalem. Its persistent rust pictures the uncleanness that remains within the city, while the exposed blood recalls violence that has not been put right.

The fire grows until even the emptied pot is heated. The Lord explains the charge: He sought to cleanse the city, but it would not be purified. This is the point at which the long warning becomes an announced action against its ways and deeds.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read the pot as the besieged city, with its people gathered inside and its wrongdoing still present. Both connect the image with the failure to turn from sin despite the opportunities already given.

The BSB describes rust that resists removal. Older commentary often uses the language of scum from its Bible translation. The controlling point is the city’s persistent uncleanness and the judgment now coming, rather than an exact description of a metalworking process.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A later warning over Jerusalem provides a sobering parallel. In Luke 19:41–44, Jesus weeps over the city and announces that enemies will surround and destroy it because it has not recognized the time of God’s visitation.

These are distinct warnings in different periods, not a single siege or a shared timetable. The parallel shows why a city’s sacred history cannot make refusing God harmless. Jesus’ tears also show that announcing judgment does not mean delighting in the loss it brings.
