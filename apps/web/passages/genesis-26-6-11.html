Passage

Genesis 26:6-11

Isaac Deceives Abimelech

Berean Standard Bible

6 So Isaac settled in Gerar. 7 But when the men of that place asked about his wife, he said, “She is my sister.” For he was afraid to say, “She is my wife,” since he thought to himself, “The men of this place will kill me on account of Rebekah, because she is so beautiful.”

8 When Isaac had been there a long time, Abimelech king of the Philistines looked down from the window and was surprised to see Isaac caressing his wife Rebekah. 9 Abimelech sent for Isaac and said, “So she is really your wife! How could you say, ‘She is my sister’?”

Isaac replied, “Because I thought I might die on account of her.”

10 “What is this you have done to us?” asked Abimelech. “One of the people could easily have slept with your wife, and you would have brought guilt upon us.” 11 So Abimelech warned all the people, saying, “Whoever harms this man or his wife will surely be put to death.”

What is the context?

Isaac stays in Gerar as God directed, but his conduct there also reveals fear. When asked about Rebekah, he calls her his sister because he thinks the local men might kill him to obtain her. His decision echoes Abraham's earlier concealment of Sarah's identity, exposing his wife to danger while trying to protect himself.

After a long time, Abimelech sees Isaac caressing Rebekah and recognizes that she is his wife. The Hebrew verb comes from צָחַק, ṣāḥaq (tsah-KHAHK), whose meanings include laughing and playing. Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically identifies marital caresses in this verse. The king sees behavior that contradicts Isaac's account of their relationship.

Abimelech confronts Isaac about the guilt his deception could have brought upon the people. Isaac admits his fear, and the king issues a warning against harming either him or Rebekah. Protection comes despite Isaac's dishonesty. The account shows both his obedience in remaining in the land and his failure to trust God within it.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see Isaac repeating his father's fear-driven deception and putting Rebekah and others at risk. Wesley's note on Genesis 26:7 treats the concealment as a serious failure by a man who otherwise trusts God.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God has just promised to be with Isaac, and the story immediately shows how unevenly Isaac lives in that assurance. The promised family is protected through the exposure of his lie and the king's intervention. God's continuing care is visible alongside a needed rebuke, rather than as approval of the deception.

The wider biblical call is for conduct to agree with professed trust. 1 John 2:3–6 joins knowing God with keeping His word and walking as Jesus walked. Isaac's experience helps make that issue concrete: obeying one direction does not remove the need to trust and act truthfully in the next difficulty.
