Passage

Romans 8:31-39

Can suffering separate us from God’s love?

Berean Standard Bible

31 What then shall we say in response to these things? If God is for us, who can be against us? 32 He who did not spare His own Son but gave Him up for us all, how will He not also, along with Him, freely give us all things? 33 Who will bring any charge against God’s elect? It is God who justifies. 34 Who is there to condemn us? For Christ Jesus, who died, and more than that was raised to life, is at the right hand of God—and He is interceding for us.

35 Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall trouble or distress or persecution or famine or nakedness or danger or sword? 36 As it is written: “For Your sake we face death all day long; we are considered as sheep to be slaughtered.” 37 No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through Him who loved us.

38 For I am convinced that neither death nor life, neither angels nor principalities, neither the present nor the future, nor any powers, 39 neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord.

What is the context?

Paul brings the chapter’s account of life, adoption and hope to a series of questions. If God is for His people, what opponent can overturn His purpose? The gift of His own Son is the reason for confidence that He will provide what is needed to complete His saving work.

The questions then move into a courtroom picture. God is the one who justifies, and Christ has died, risen and taken His place at God’s right hand. He also intercedes for believers. Their assurance rests in what God has done through Christ and what Christ continues to do for them.

Paul names real threats: distress, persecution, hunger, exposure, danger and violent death. He quotes Psalm 44, where faithful people suffer and cry out for help. These hardships can happen to God’s people. The promise is that they cannot sever the love of Christ.

The closing list stretches across death and life, spiritual powers, present and future, height and depth, and the rest of creation. Paul’s confidence reaches beyond any single circumstance: God’s love is found in Christ Jesus our Lord.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read these verses as deep reassurance that suffering and hostile powers cannot defeat God’s love in Christ. Victory through Him does not require escaping every hardship named in the passage.

Traditions differ over whether a believer can finally turn away. Reformed churches following the Westminster Confession, chapter 17, teach that those God truly calls and sanctifies will certainly remain His to the end. They may fall into serious sin, with painful consequences, but will not finally fall from grace.

Wesley’s notes on verses 30 and 38–39 affirm God’s protecting love while retaining the possibility that a believer may turn away. On this reading, no hostile power can tear believers from Christ, but continuing faith still matters.

The Catholic Church also distinguishes the strength of God’s love from a person’s ability to reject grace. It teaches that mortal sin—a grave wrong committed with full knowledge and deliberate consent—can result in the loss of grace and requires repentance and God’s forgiveness (Catechism 1857–1861). This is not the claim that every failure or anxious thought means someone has lost salvation.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Paul explicitly brings Psalm 44:17–26 into his account of life in Christ. The psalm’s speakers say they have not abandoned God’s covenant, yet they face defeat and death. They appeal to His loving devotion for rescue.

That connection places Christian suffering within the longer experience of God’s people, who have cried out while waiting for His help. Paul answers the fear of abandonment by pointing to the Son whom God gave, raised and seated at His right hand. The love revealed in Christ is the ground of hope even when present circumstances remain painful.
