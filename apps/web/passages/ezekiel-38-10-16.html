Passage

Ezekiel 38:10-16

What does Gog want, and what does the Lord intend?

Berean Standard Bible

10 This is what the Lord GOD says: On that day, thoughts will arise in your mind, and you will devise an evil plan. 11 You will say, ‘I will go up against a land of unwalled villages; I will come against a quiet people who dwell securely, all of them living without walls or bars or gates— 12 in order to seize the spoil and carry off the plunder, to turn a hand against the desolate places now inhabited and against a people gathered from the nations, who have acquired livestock and possessions and who live at the center of the land.’ 13 Sheba and Dedan and the merchants of Tarshish with all its villages will ask, ‘Have you come to capture the plunder? Have you assembled your hordes to carry away loot, to make off with silver and gold, to take cattle and goods, to seize great spoil?’

14 Therefore prophesy, son of man, and tell Gog that this is what the Lord GOD says: On that day when My people Israel are dwelling securely, will you not take notice of this? 15 And you will come from your place out of the far north—you and many peoples with you, all riding horses—a mighty horde, a huge army. 16 You will advance against My people Israel like a cloud covering the land. It will happen in the latter days, O Gog, that I will bring you against My land, so that the nations may know Me when I show Myself holy in you before their eyes.

What is the context?

Gog’s intention is laid bare: he sees a people living securely and plans to seize their wealth. Their unwalled villages appear to offer an easy opportunity for plunder. His target is a people gathered from the nations into land that had been desolate.

Sheba, Dedan and the merchants of Tarshish ask about the spoil. Their questions turn attention to the silver, gold, livestock and goods the invaders want. The text does not describe these merchants mounting a rescue.

Alongside Gog’s evil plan, the Lord states His own purpose. He will bring Gog against His land and show Himself holy before the nations. The same event exposes the invader’s greed and becomes the setting in which the Lord makes Himself known. Gog’s purpose and God’s purpose are not the same.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Cambridge sees the merchants’ interest as the prospect of trading for the spoil; Wesley understands their questions as willingness to help for a share of it. Both readings recognize the appeal of gain rather than an effort to defend Israel.

Wesley describes the Lord’s bringing Gog as permitting him to come. Henry emphasizes the failure of attempts to destroy a people under God’s protection. These explanations distinguish the invader’s hostile intentions from the Lord’s faithful purpose.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 2:22–24 gives a further example of evil human action being overtaken by God’s saving purpose. Peter says Jesus was delivered up according to God’s plan, while holding those who killed Him responsible. God then raised Him from the dead.

This is a connection in how God’s purpose prevails, not a claim that Gog’s invasion is a hidden description of the crucifixion. James 1:13–15 also keeps a necessary distinction clear: God does not tempt people to evil; sinful desire draws them toward sin. God’s victory over an evil plan does not turn the evil itself into obedience.
