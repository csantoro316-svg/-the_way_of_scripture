Passage

John 10:11-21

What makes Jesus the good shepherd?

Berean Standard Bible

11 I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep. 12 The hired hand is not the shepherd, and the sheep are not his own. When he sees the wolf coming, he abandons the sheep and runs away. Then the wolf pounces on them and scatters the flock. 13 The man runs away because he is a hired servant and is unconcerned for the sheep.

14 I am the good shepherd. I know My sheep and My sheep know Me, 15 just as the Father knows Me and I know the Father. And I lay down My life for the sheep. 16 I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them in as well, and they will listen to My voice. Then there will be one flock and one shepherd. 17 The reason the Father loves Me is that I lay down My life in order to take it up again. 18 No one takes it from Me, but I lay it down of My own accord. I have authority to lay it down and authority to take it up again. This charge I have received from My Father.”

19 Again there was division among the Jews because of Jesus’ message. 20 Many of them said, “He is demon-possessed and insane. Why would you listen to Him?” 21 But others replied, “These are not the words of a man possessed by a demon. Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?”

What is the context?

Jesus explains His care through a contrast with a hired hand who abandons the sheep when danger comes. The difference appears in what each does for the flock: Jesus lays down His life. The picture is about love and costly faithfulness, not proof that every worker who receives wages is untrustworthy.

He knows His sheep, and they know Him. He also speaks of other sheep whom He must bring, so that there will be one flock under one shepherd. The Greek word ποίμνη, poimnē (POYM-nay), means a flock; Thayer’s lexicon directly connects its use here with people following Christ. The emphasis is on a people gathered around Him, rather than a requirement that everyone occupy one physical enclosure.

His coming death is voluntary and belongs with His resurrection. He has authority to lay down His life and take it up again, in obedience to the Father’s command. This does not remove the part played by those who crucify Him. Acts 2:23–24 holds human responsibility together with God’s purpose and His raising Jesus from death.

The teaching again divides the listeners. Some repeat the accusation that He has a demon; others return to the evidence of the opened eyes. The healing in John 9 remains part of the question before them: does Jesus’ work reveal the Father who sent Him?

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that the shepherd’s laying down His life points to Jesus’ saving death, joined here to His resurrection. Calvin on verses 16–18 and Wesley on those verses also identify the other sheep with Gentiles being gathered into one people with Jewish believers.

Traditions differ over how His death for the sheep relates to His saving purpose for humanity. The Reformed Westminster Confession, 8.5 and 8.8, teaches that Christ purchases redemption for those given to Him by the Father and certainly brings that redemption to them. This reading emphasizes a saving work that achieves its purpose for His people.

The Catholic Church teaches that Christ died for every human being without exception (Catechism 605). The Methodist Article XX likewise describes His one offering as the complete sacrifice for the sins of the whole world, and Wesley’s note on verse 18 speaks of His ransom for the whole world. These readings do not take the mention of the sheep to restrict everyone for whom He died. They also do not mean that everyone is automatically saved.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ezekiel 34:23–24 promises one shepherd from David’s line to care for God’s people. Jesus’ self-giving care fits that hope, and Hebrews 13:20–21 explicitly calls the risen Jesus the great Shepherd of the sheep. His death and resurrection are central to how He brings His people into life with God.

The gathering reaches beyond one people. Ephesians 2:11–22 describes Jews and Gentiles reconciled to God in one body through the cross, with shared access to the Father by one Spirit. That gives the promise of one flock a wider setting: Christ brings people who were far apart into one household under His care.
