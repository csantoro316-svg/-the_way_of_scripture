Passage

Luke 20:27-40

Can God’s promise to His people end at death?

Berean Standard Bible

27 Then some of the Sadducees, who say there is no resurrection, came to question Him. 28 “Teacher,” they said, “Moses wrote for us that if a man’s brother dies and leaves a wife but no children, the man is to marry his brother’s widow and raise up offspring for him. 29 Now there were seven brothers. The first one married a wife but died childless. 30 Then the second 31 and the third married the widow, and in the same way all seven died, leaving no children. 32 And last of all, the woman died. 33 So then, in the resurrection, whose wife will she be? For all seven were married to her.”

34 Jesus answered, “The sons of this age marry and are given in marriage. 35 But those who are considered worthy to share in the age to come and in the resurrection from the dead will neither marry nor be given in marriage. 36 In fact, they can no longer die, because they are like the angels. And since they are sons of the resurrection, they are sons of God.

37 Even Moses demonstrates that the dead are raised, in the passage about the burning bush. For he calls the Lord ‘the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.’ 38 He is not the God of the dead, but of the living, for to Him all are alive.” 39 Some of the scribes answered, “Teacher, You have spoken well!” 40 And they did not dare to question Him any further.

What is the context?

The Sadducees, whom Luke identifies as denying resurrection, propose a case involving seven brothers and one woman. Each brother marries her after the previous one dies without children. They ask whose wife she will be in the resurrection.

The question draws on the instruction that a surviving brother should preserve his deceased brother’s line through marriage to the widow. Deuteronomy explains that family obligation; the Sadducees use an extreme case to challenge resurrection. (Deuteronomy 25:5–10)

Jesus distinguishes this age from the resurrection. Those who share in that age neither marry nor are given in marriage, and they can no longer die. His comparison with angels belongs within that explanation of life beyond death.

He then points to Moses’ account of the burning bush, where God identifies Himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Jesus makes that relationship with the living the basis of His answer about resurrection. Some scribes praise His response, and the questioning stops. (Exodus 3:2–6, 15–17)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly understand this as affirming resurrection and a life that is changed from the conditions of the present age. The Catholic bishops’ notes emphasize Jesus’ correction of the Sadducees’ assumptions and His appeal to Moses; Wesley connects God’s enduring relationship with His people to their continued life and resurrection. (USCCB notes on Luke 20:27–40; Wesley on Luke 20:34–38)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Exodus, the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob also promises to bring their descendants out of oppression. His identity is tied to faithful care and promises that stretch across generations. Hebrews describes the patriarchs dying in faith while still awaiting what was promised, and looking toward the city God prepared. Death does not make His faithfulness or their hope meaningless. (Exodus 3:15–17; Hebrews 11:13–16, 39–40)

Paul explicitly places the resurrection of those who belong to Christ within the victory begun by Christ’s own resurrection. His reign leads to the destruction of death itself. That supplies the wider gospel setting for Jesus’ answer here. (1 Corinthians 15:20–26)

Paul also describes the body being raised from weakness and perishability into power and lasting life, using the seed and the body God gives it as his illustration. The resurrection hope is larger than an endless repetition of present conditions. (1 Corinthians 15:35–49)

What is our position?

Who is The Angel of the Lord?, in its section on Exodus 3:2–6, identifies the One who appears and speaks from the burning bush as the Son before His human birth. The study explicitly connects that passage with Jesus’ answer to the Sadducees in Matthew’s parallel account. This is the study’s conclusion about the speaker in Exodus; Jesus’ immediate point here concerns resurrection and God’s relationship with the living.
