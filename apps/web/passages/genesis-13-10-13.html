Passage

Genesis 13:10-13

Lot Proceeds toward Sodom

Berean Standard Bible

10 And Lot looked out and saw that the whole plain of the Jordan, all the way to Zoar, was well watered like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt. (This was before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah.) 11 So Lot chose the whole plain of the Jordan for himself and set out toward the east. And Abram and Lot parted company.

12 Abram lived in the land of Canaan, but Lot settled in the cities of the plain and pitched his tent toward Sodom. 13 But the men of Sodom were wicked, sinning greatly against the LORD.

What is the context?

Lot looks across the Jordan plain and chooses its well-watered land. The comparison with the garden of the LORD and Egypt emphasizes its visible abundance. For a man with large herds, it appears to offer the resources that were lacking when the two households lived together.

The narrator gives us information that makes the choice troubling. This is the region of Sodom and Gomorrah before their destruction, and the people of Sodom are described as greatly wicked before the LORD. The land's appeal and the city's moral condition stand side by side. Good pasture does not tell the whole story of what it will mean to live there.

Lot settles among the cities of the plain and pitches his tent toward Sodom, while Abram remains in Canaan. The account is beginning to separate not only their grazing grounds but also the settings in which their next decisions will unfold. We will need the later chapters to see the cost of Lot's proximity to Sodom.

How is this passage commonly understood?

A common Christian reading sees a warning about judging a place by its advantages while overlooking the character of life there. Wesley's notes on Genesis 13:10, 13 recognize the plain's garden-like appeal and the open wickedness of its inhabitants.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament returns to the outcome of this choice. 2 Peter 2:6–9 describes Sodom's judgment and calls Lot a righteous man distressed by the lawless conduct around him. That later description matters: Genesis' warning about his choice should not be turned into a declaration that Lot never belonged to God.

Peter's larger point is that God knows how to rescue the godly and judge wrongdoing. Lot's story will show both the misery of living amid evil and God's power to deliver. The attractive landscape is only the beginning of the account; the ending reveals how much more is at stake than the prosperity Lot first sees.
