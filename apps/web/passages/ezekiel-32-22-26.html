Passage

Ezekiel 32:22-26

What do Assyria, Elam, Meshech and Tubal have in common?

Berean Standard Bible

22 Assyria is there with her whole company; her graves are all around her. All of them are slain, fallen by the sword. 23 Her graves are set in the depths of the Pit, and her company is all around her grave. All of them are slain, fallen by the sword— those who once spread terror in the land of the living.

24 Elam is there with all her multitudes around her grave. All of them are slain, fallen by the sword— those who went down uncircumcised to the earth below, who once spread their terror in the land of the living. They bear their disgrace with those who descend to the Pit. 25 Among the slain they prepare a resting place for Elam with all her hordes, with her graves all around her. All of them are uncircumcised, slain by the sword, although their terror was once spread in the land of the living. They bear their disgrace with those who descend to the Pit. They are placed among the slain.

26 Meshech and Tubal are there with all their multitudes, with their graves all around them. All of them are uncircumcised, slain by the sword, because they spread their terror in the land of the living.

What is the context?

The lament names Assyria with its company, Elam with its multitudes, and Meshech and Tubal with their people. Graves surround them, and each group lies among those slain by the sword.

The repeated accusation is that they spread terror in the land of the living. Their power was experienced by others as fear and violence.

Now they bear disgrace with those who descend to the Pit. The repetition makes Egypt’s warning unmistakable: the ability to terrify others does not give a nation immunity from judgment or a lasting place above those it threatened.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the scene as the disgrace of once-powerful rulers, subjects and allies. Cambridge emphasizes the consequence of lives marked by violence: their dishonor follows them into the scene of death.

The names serve the lament’s repeated warning. The passage does not require turning them into a complete sequence of later world empires in order to understand why Egypt is being shown their graves.
