Passage

Ezekiel 28:6-10

Will the ruler still claim to be a god when death comes?

Berean Standard Bible

6 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: Because you regard your heart as the heart of a god, 7 behold, I will bring foreigners against you, the most ruthless of nations. They will draw their swords against the beauty of your wisdom and will defile your splendor. 8 They will bring you down to the Pit, and you will die a violent death in the heart of the seas. 9 Will you still say, ‘I am a god,’ in the presence of those who slay you? You will be only a man, not a god, in the hands of those who wound you. 10 You will die the death of the uncircumcised at the hands of foreigners. For I have spoken, declares the Lord GOD.”

What is the context?

The Lord’s answer to the ruler’s self-exaltation is the arrival of ruthless foreigners. The beauty associated with his wisdom will be defiled, and he will be brought down to the Pit.

The question becomes painfully direct: will he still say that he is a god in the hands of those who kill him? The approaching death exposes the claim as something he cannot make true.

The death of the uncircumcised completes the picture of humiliation. The ruler’s treasured standing does not give him control over his end. The Lord’s word, rather than the ruler’s estimate of himself, determines the outcome.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The USCCB notes understand the death as a humiliating exposure of the ruler’s claim to divinity. Wesley emphasizes that the ruler remains subject to the troubles and limits of human life.

The point is the defeat of his imagined divine security. The judgment confronts his pride and conduct, rather than making outward circumcision a guarantee that another person will escape death.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A useful contrast appears in John 10:14–18. Tyre’s ruler claims a status that cannot protect him when death comes. Jesus says that He lays down His life for the sheep of His own accord and has authority to take it up again.

This is a contrast between the passages, not an identification of Tyre’s ruler as a prediction of Jesus. The Gospel shows why Jesus’ death must not be understood as the collapse of an empty claim: He gives His life in obedience to the Father as the good shepherd.
