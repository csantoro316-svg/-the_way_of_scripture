Passage

1 Samuel 28:1-6

What happens when both David and Saul face the coming battle?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now in those days the Philistines gathered their forces for warfare against Israel. So Achish said to David, “You must understand that you and your men are to go out to battle with me.” 2 David replied, “Then you will come to know what your servant can do.” “Very well,” said Achish. “I will make you my bodyguard for life.”

3 Now by this time Samuel had died, and all Israel had mourned for him and buried him in Ramah, his own city. And Saul had removed the mediums and spiritists from the land. 4 The Philistines gathered together and camped at Shunem, while Saul gathered all Israel and camped at Gilboa. 5 When Saul saw the Philistine army, he was afraid and trembled violently. 6 He inquired of the LORD, but the LORD did not answer him by dreams or Urim or prophets.

What is the context?

The Philistines gather to fight Israel, and Achish tells David that he and his men must march with him. David gives an answer Achish takes as willingness to serve, and the king promises to make him his lifelong bodyguard. The arrangement that offered shelter now requires loyalty in a war against David’s own people.

The scene then turns to Saul. Samuel is dead, and Saul has removed mediums and spiritists from the land. The Philistines camp at Shunem while Saul gathers Israel at Gilboa. Seeing their army, he is terrified.

Saul asks the LORD for guidance, but receives no answer through dreams, Urim or prophets. The chapter places two crises beside each other: David is caught in the obligations of his refuge, and Saul cannot obtain the direction he seeks.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry describes David’s position as a dilemma involving loyalty to Achish and loyalty to Israel. Wesley reads his answer as deliberately ambiguous. Neither the king’s confidence nor David’s wording tells the reader exactly what David intends to do in battle.

Henry connects Saul’s distress with his earlier refusal to heed God’s word. The silence is part of the relationship the narrative has traced, rather than evidence that Saul has never asked God a question before.

The account names the means by which no answer comes. It does not explain the workings of the Urim here or identify a new priest or replacement set of objects serving Saul.
