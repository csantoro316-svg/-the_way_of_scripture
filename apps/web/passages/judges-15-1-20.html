Passage

Judges 15:1-20

What happens when each side insists on paying the other back?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Later on, at the time of the wheat harvest, Samson took a young goat and went to visit his wife. “I want to go to my wife in her room,” he said. But her father would not let him enter. 2 “I was sure that you thoroughly hated her,” said her father, “so I gave her to one of the men who accompanied you. Is not her younger sister more beautiful than she? Please take her instead.” 3 Samson said to them, “This time I will be blameless in doing harm to the Philistines.”

4 Then Samson went out and caught three hundred foxes. And he took torches, turned the foxes tail-to-tail, and fastened a torch between each pair of tails. 5 Then he lit the torches and released the foxes into the standing grain of the Philistines, burning up the piles of grain and the standing grain, as well as the vineyards and olive groves. 6 “Who did this?” the Philistines demanded. “It was Samson, the son-in-law of the Timnite,” they were told. “For his wife was given to his companion.” So the Philistines went up and burned her and her father to death. 7 And Samson told them, “Because you have done this, I will not rest until I have taken vengeance upon you.” 8 And he struck them ruthlessly with a great slaughter, and then went down and stayed in the cave at the rock of Etam.

9 Then the Philistines went up, camped in Judah, and deployed themselves near the town of Lehi. 10 “Why have you attacked us?” said the men of Judah. The Philistines replied, “We have come to arrest Samson and pay him back for what he has done to us.” 11 In response, three thousand men of Judah went to the cave at the rock of Etam, and they asked Samson, “Do you not realize that the Philistines rule over us? What have you done to us?” “I have done to them what they did to me,” he replied. 12 But they said to him, “We have come down to arrest you and hand you over to the Philistines.” Samson replied, “Swear to me that you will not kill me yourselves.” 13 “No,” they answered, “we will not kill you, but we will tie you up securely and hand you over to them.” So they bound him with two new ropes and led him up from the rock.

14 When Samson arrived in Lehi, the Philistines came out shouting against him. And the Spirit of the LORD came mightily upon him. The ropes on his arms became like burnt flax, and the bonds broke loose from his hands. 15 He found the fresh jawbone of a donkey, reached out his hand and took it, and struck down a thousand men. 16 Then Samson said: “With the jawbone of a donkey I have piled them into heaps. With the jawbone of a donkey I have slain a thousand men.” 17 And when Samson had finished speaking, he cast the jawbone from his hand; and he named that place Ramath-lehi.

18 And being very thirsty, Samson cried out to the LORD, “You have accomplished this great deliverance through Your servant. Must I now die of thirst and fall into the hands of the uncircumcised?” 19 So God opened up the hollow place in Lehi, and water came out of it. When Samson drank, his strength returned, and he was revived. That is why he named it En-hakkore, and it remains in Lehi to this day. 20 And Samson judged Israel for twenty years in the days of the Philistines.

What is the context?

Samson returns during the wheat harvest with a young goat, intending to visit his wife. Her father has given her to Samson’s companion because he thought Samson hated her. He offers a younger daughter instead. Samson responds by announcing that this time he will be blameless when he harms the Philistines. That is Samson’s explanation of his actions.

He catches three hundred foxes, ties torches between their tails in pairs, and releases them into the crops. Grain, vineyards, and olive groves burn. The Hebrew word שׁוּעָל, shual (shoo-AHL), can mean fox and may also refer to a jackal. Brown–Driver–Briggs allows that possibility here; the BSB renders it foxes. The account does not explain how long the catching took or whether anyone helped.

The Philistines burn Samson’s wife and her father to death. Samson retaliates with a great slaughter and withdraws to the rock of Etam. Then the Philistines move into Judah to arrest him. Each response spreads the harm beyond the original dispute. The woman who was threatened with burning in chapter 14 now suffers that very fate.

Three thousand men of Judah confront Samson because they fear their Philistine rulers. He agrees to be bound and handed over, provided his own people do not kill him. When the Philistines approach, the Spirit of the LORD comes mightily upon him. He breaks free and strikes down a thousand men with a donkey’s jawbone.

Victory leaves him desperately thirsty. He calls the deliverance God’s work and asks for help; God provides water, and he revives. The chapter closes by saying he judged Israel for twenty years during Philistine rule. His victories begin Israel’s rescue, as the birth announcement promised, but do not yet end that rule.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands Samson’s attacks as the work of a judge avenging injuries against Israel, even though the actions can look like private revenge. Henry likewise sees God using these unlikely means to humble the Philistines. Both place the conflict within Samson’s work against Israel’s oppressors; that is how they explain its purpose.

The distinction between God’s purpose and the people’s stated motives remains important when reading the account. Samson and the Philistines repeatedly explain themselves in terms of paying back what others have done. The narrator explicitly identifies the Spirit’s empowering in verse 14 and God’s provision in verse 19. Those statements should be read alongside the characters’ own explanations.

Wesley and Henry also read Samson’s willingness to be handed over as a picture that points toward Christ. Their comparison draws attention to a rescuer rejected by his own people and willing to be bound. Samson’s later thirst leads them to emphasize that even someone greatly used by God remains dependent on Him. Wesley offers pride as a possible reason for the distress, rather than something the narrator expressly states.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The comparison with Jesus becomes clearer when we read Matthew 26:47–56. Jesus could call on His Father for angelic help, yet accepts arrest so that Scripture will be fulfilled. Samson submits to being bound and then fights his way free. Jesus willingly goes further, through suffering and death, to accomplish His saving work. This is a comparison between the accounts; Matthew does not identify Samson’s arrest as a prediction of Jesus’ arrest.

The unlikely weapon and the thirsty victor also bring out a wider biblical theme: the rescue depends on God. First Corinthians 1:26–31 explains that God works through what the world regards as weak so that people boast in Him, and locates our righteousness and redemption in Christ. Second Corinthians 4:7–12 shows the same truth in fragile servants carrying the message of Jesus. God’s power does not make His servants self-sufficient.

For those following Jesus, the response to personal injury is stated directly in Romans 12:17–21: seek peace, leave vengeance to God, and overcome evil with good. Reading the larger story keeps Samson’s particular work as Israel’s judge from becoming a general instruction to repay harm with harm.
