Passage

John 5:1-9

How does Jesus help the man who cannot reach the water?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Some time later there was a feast of the Jews, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem. 2 Now there is in Jerusalem near the Sheep Gate a pool with five covered colonnades, which in Hebrew is called Bethesda. 3 On these walkways lay a great number of the sick, the blind, the lame, and the paralyzed.

5 One man there had been an invalid for thirty-eight years. 6 When Jesus saw him lying there and realized that he had spent a long time in this condition, He asked him, “Do you want to get well?” 7 “Sir,” the invalid replied, “I have no one to help me into the pool when the water is stirred. While I am on my way, someone else goes in before me.” 8 Then Jesus told him, “Get up, pick up your mat, and walk.” 9 Immediately the man was made well, and he picked up his mat and began to walk. Now this happened on the Sabbath day,

What is the context?

Jesus goes to Jerusalem for a Jewish feast, which this account does not name. Near the Sheep Gate is the pool called Bethesda, with five covered colonnades where many sick people lie. One man has been ill for thirty-eight years. That describes the length of his illness; it does not say he has spent all those years beside the pool.

Excavations near St. Anne’s Church uncovered a double-pool complex. Archaeologist E. Jerry Vardaman describes how a covered walkway between the pools, together with those around the outside, explains the five-colonnade arrangement. This gives useful physical background to the scene. (Vardaman, The Pool of Bethesda, pages 27–28)

When Jesus asks whether he wants to get well, the man explains that no one helps him into the water before someone else gets there. Jesus tells him to get up, carry his mat and walk. He is made well immediately, and the Gospel notes that this happens on the Sabbath.

If you notice the verse numbers moving from 3 to 5, this follows the BSB’s text. Its footnote records the additional words found in some manuscripts about an angel stirring the water. The main account still records the man’s expectation about the water in verse 7. (BSB footnote on John 5:3–4)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readers commonly see Jesus giving the man help that he cannot obtain by reaching the pool. His recovery comes through Jesus’ command, and the Sabbath setting prepares for the dispute that follows. (John 5:6–16)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah pictures God coming to save, with weak hands strengthened, blind eyes opened and the lame leaping for joy. The man walking at Jesus’ word is a supported parallel with that promised restoration. It shows His life-giving work within the wider hope of God coming to rescue and restore His people. (Isaiah 35:3–10)

The chapter goes on to connect this act with the Son’s power to give life, including the promise that the dead will hear His voice. The healing introduces that larger claim; it is more than an isolated display of power. (John 5:19–29)
