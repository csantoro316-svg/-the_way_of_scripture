Passage

Mark 10:17-22

The Man Who Could Not Let Go

Berean Standard Bible

17 As Jesus started on His way, a man ran up and knelt before Him. “Good Teacher,” he asked, “what must I do to inherit eternal life?” 18 “Why do you call Me good?” Jesus replied. “No one is good except God alone. 19 You know the commandments: ‘Do not murder, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not bear false witness, do not cheat others, honor your father and mother.’” 20 “Teacher,” he replied, “all these I have kept from my youth.”

21 Jesus looked at him, loved him, and said to him, “There is one thing you lack: Go, sell everything you own and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. Then come, follow Me.” 22 But the man was saddened by these words and went away in sorrow, because he had great wealth.

What is the context?

A man runs to Jesus, kneels, and asks what he must do to inherit eternal life. The encounter follows Jesus' teaching about receiving the kingdom like a child. Here someone comes with an account of commandments he has kept, yet still has a question about life with God.

Jesus asks why he calls Him good and directs attention to God as the measure of goodness. He then names commands concerning other people, including the prohibition of cheating them. The man says he has kept these from his youth. Mark reports that claim without saying that it proves a complete, sinless life.

Jesus looks at him and loves him. The costly instruction that follows belongs within that love: sell what he owns, give to the poor, receive treasure in heaven, and follow Him. The invitation reaches beyond adding another achievement to the man's record. It asks him to release what he holds and entrust his future to Jesus.

He goes away sorrowful because he has great wealth. Mark tells us his response at this encounter, not the story of everything that happens to him afterward. The next paragraph will develop the danger wealth presents and the need for God's saving power.

The familiar description of him as a rich young ruler brings together details from the related accounts. Matthew 19:16–22 calls him young; Luke 18:18–23 calls him a ruler. Neither description by itself identifies him as a member of the Sanhedrin.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see the encounter as exposing an attachment that stands in the way of following Jesus. Wesley on Mark 10:21 connects the command with the man's love of riches and his need to love God. Jesus' love for him and the seriousness of His call belong together.

Traditions differ over how the command to sell everything relates to Christian life more generally. The Catholic Catechism 2053 connects this scene with following Christ and His call to poverty. Its account of commands and counsels, 1973–1974, distinguishes what love requires of everyone from particular ways of giving oneself more fully according to one's calling. It does not require every Christian to follow every counsel in the same form.

Reformed churches following Westminster 16.1–5 reject the idea that anyone can do more than God requires and place good works within obedience that depends on Christ's Spirit. This reading treats the man's surrender as a response to God's claim on him, rather than an extra level of obedience through which eternal life could be earned. These are differences between the cited teachings, not descriptions of every individual Catholic or Protestant.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The invitation to follow Jesus leads toward the saving work He is going to accomplish in Jerusalem. 2 Corinthians 5:14–21 explains that Christ dies and rises so that people may live for Him, and that God reconciles people to Himself through Him. Becoming right with God rests on what God does through Christ, and brings a changed direction for life.

Ephesians 2:4–10 holds the same two things together: salvation is God's gift, received through faith rather than earned by works, and those made alive in Christ are created for good works. The man's question and Jesus' costly call belong within that wider account of grace creating a new life. Generosity and following Jesus are not reduced to the price of admission.
