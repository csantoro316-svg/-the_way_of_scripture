Passage

Ezekiel 21:18-24

Why does Babylon’s choice of road lead to Jerusalem?

Berean Standard Bible

18 Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 19 “Now you, son of man, mark out two roads for the sword of the king of Babylon to take, both starting from the same land. And make a signpost where the road branches off to each city. 20 Mark out one road for the sword to come against Rabbah of the Ammonites, and another against Judah into fortified Jerusalem. 21 For the king of Babylon stands at the fork in the road, at the junction of the two roads, to seek an omen: He shakes the arrows, he consults the idols, he examines the liver. 22 In his right hand appears the portent for Jerusalem, where he is to set up battering rams, to call for the slaughter, to lift a battle cry, to direct the battering rams against the gates, to build a ramp, and to erect a siege wall.

23 It will seem like a false omen to the eyes of those who have sworn allegiance to him, but it will draw attention to their guilt and take them captive. 24 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Because you have drawn attention to your guilt, exposing your transgressions, so that your sins are revealed in all your deeds—because you have come to remembrance—you shall be taken in hand.

What is the context?

Ezekiel is to mark two roads for Babylon’s king: one toward Rabbah of the Ammonites and one toward Jerusalem. At the fork, the king seeks an omen through arrows, idols and examination of a liver.

The omen points toward Jerusalem. The scene then shows the practical consequences: battering rams, battle cries, a ramp and a siege wall directed against the city.

Records of liver omens help explain this unfamiliar detail. The Metropolitan Museum’s account of a Mesopotamian liver-omen tablet describes predictions about an army’s success drawn from features of a sacrificial sheep’s liver. That later surviving tablet illustrates the kind of practice described; it is not an artifact from this particular campaign.

Those who have sworn allegiance dismiss the omen as false, but the Lord brings attention to their exposed guilt. Their confidence will not keep them from capture.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry understands Babylon’s decision through divination as still subject to God’s direction of the coming judgment. Wesley connects the sworn allegiance with Zedekiah and his officials’ broken pledge to Babylon.

The scene does not commend the king’s methods as a way for God’s people to seek guidance. Its point is that an apparently favorable reason to dismiss the danger cannot overturn the Lord’s warning or erase the conduct for which Jerusalem is being judged.
