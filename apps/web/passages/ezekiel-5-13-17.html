Passage

Ezekiel 5:13-17

What will the ruined city make known to the nations?

Berean Standard Bible

13 And when My anger is spent and I have vented My wrath against them, I will be appeased. And when I have spent My wrath on them, they will know that I, the LORD, in My zeal have spoken. 14 I will make you a ruin and a disgrace among the nations around you, in the sight of all who pass by. 15 So you will be a reproach and a taunt, a warning and a horror to the nations around you, when I execute judgments against you in anger, wrath, and raging fury. I, the LORD, have spoken.

16 When I shower you with the deadly arrows of famine and destruction that I will send to destroy you, I will intensify the famine against you and cut off your supply of food. 17 I will send famine and wild beasts against you, and they will leave you childless. Plague and bloodshed will sweep through you, and I will bring a sword against you. I, the LORD, have spoken.”

What is the context?

The Lord declares that His anger and the fulfillment of His word will be recognized through the judgment. The city that stood among the nations will become a warning and an object of horror to them.

The repeated declaration that the Lord has spoken gives the passage its solemn force. The destruction is presented as the outcome of His announced judgment, rather than evidence that His word or rule has failed.

The closing images include the arrows of famine, a cut-off food supply, wild beasts, plague, bloodshed and the sword. They describe a community losing the things that ordinarily sustain and protect life.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes that God’s word will prove true, even when people presume they will escape the announced consequences. Wesley connects the city’s condition with a warning visible to other nations.

Their explanations treat the recognition of the Lord as central to the outcome. The calamities expose the seriousness of His rejected word and the failure of the confidence that had ignored it.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The same set of judgments appears in Revelation 6:1–8, where the Lamb opens the seals and the fourth rider is given authority involving sword, famine, plague and wild beasts. This is a recurring biblical pattern of judgment, also found elsewhere in Ezekiel, rather than a statement that Revelation quotes only this passage.

The connection places these warnings within the New Testament’s presentation of Christ’s authority as the Lamb. God’s saving purpose includes a real reckoning with evil; the language of rescue does not make His warnings empty.
