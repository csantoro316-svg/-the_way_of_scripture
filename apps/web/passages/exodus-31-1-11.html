Passage

Exodus 31:1-11

Who will do the work God has commanded?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then the LORD said to Moses, 2 “See, I have called by name Bezalel son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah. 3 And I have filled him with the Spirit of God, with skill, ability, and knowledge in all kinds of craftsmanship, 4 to design artistic works in gold, silver, and bronze, 5 to cut gemstones for settings, and to carve wood, so that he may be a master of every craft.

6 Moreover, I have selected Oholiab son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan, as his assistant. I have also given skill to all the craftsmen, that they may fashion all that I have commanded you: 7 the Tent of Meeting, the ark of the Testimony and the mercy seat upon it, and all the other furnishings of the tent— 8 the table with its utensils, the pure gold lampstand with all its utensils, the altar of incense, 9 the altar of burnt offering with all its utensils, and the basin with its stand— 10 as well as the woven garments, both the holy garments for Aaron the priest and the garments for his sons to serve as priests, 11 in addition to the anointing oil and fragrant incense for the Holy Place. They are to make them according to all that I have commanded you.”

What is the context?

God has described the tent, its furnishings, the priests’ clothing, the oil, and the incense. Now He names the people who will make them. Bezalel comes from Judah; Oholiab, his assistant, comes from Dan. The work also involves a wider group of skilled craftsmen. Moses receives the instructions, but he will not carry them out alone.

Being filled with God’s Spirit here includes the ability to design, work metal, cut stones, and carve wood. The passage gives careful craftsmanship a place in serving God. It does not tell us how much training these workers had before this assignment. What it does say is that God supplies the skill needed to carry out His instructions.

The list in verses 7–11 brings the earlier instructions together into one shared task. Their freedom to design and make beautiful things serves a clear purpose: they are to produce what God has commanded. The next passage sets a limit even on this important work—the Sabbath must still be kept.

How is this passage commonly understood?

John Wesley and John Calvin both read these verses as a reminder that practical skill comes from God and should lead to gratitude. Calvin includes the growth of abilities a person already has; neither prayer nor dependence on God makes the actual work unnecessary.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The Bible’s opening picture of creation includes the Spirit of God at work over the waters (Genesis 1:1–3). Here the same God equips people to prepare a place for His presence among Israel. This is a useful parallel: God’s Spirit brings the ability to carry out His purpose, including work that others can see and use.

In the New Testament, Paul describes the Spirit giving different gifts for the good of the whole body of Christ. No member can dismiss another as unnecessary (1 Corinthians 12:4–13, 18–27). The craftsmen are not a detailed code for those gifts, but their shared work helps us see the larger pattern: God equips different people to serve His people together. Peter puts the responsibility plainly—use the gift you have received to serve others, with the strength God provides (1 Peter 4:10–11).
