Passage

Hebrews 12:25-29

How do we respond to a kingdom that cannot be shaken?

Berean Standard Bible

25 See to it that you do not refuse Him who speaks. For if the people did not escape when they refused Him who warned them on earth, how much less will we escape if we reject Him who warns us from heaven? 26 At that time His voice shook the earth, but now He has promised, “Once more I will shake not only the earth, but heaven as well.” 27 The words “Once more” signify the removal of what can be shaken—that is, created things—so that the unshakable may remain.

28 Therefore, since we are receiving an unshakable kingdom, let us be filled with gratitude, and so worship God acceptably with reverence and awe. 29 “For our God is a consuming fire.”

What is the context?

The invitation to the heavenly gathering is followed by a warning: do not refuse the One who speaks. Greater access through Christ gives greater reason to listen, rather than making God’s voice unimportant.

The writer recalls Sinai’s shaking, then quotes Haggai 2:6 about a further shaking of heaven and earth. He draws the distinction between created things that can be removed and the unshakable kingdom believers receive.

The response is gratitude expressed in acceptable worship, with reverence and awe. The final description of God as a consuming fire comes from Deuteronomy 4:23–24, where Israel is warned against forgetting the covenant and turning to idols.

The paragraph holds assurance and warning together. God’s kingdom is dependable, and His holiness calls for a serious response. The writer moves from that response into practical instructions about love, hospitality and faithful living in chapter 13.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin and Wesley both connect the shaking with Christ’s kingdom, though their explanations emphasize different stages. Calvin emphasizes the change brought through Christ’s coming and the gospel’s reach, while recognizing the need for created things to be brought into a better state. Wesley sees the shaking as beginning with Christ’s first coming and reaching its completion at His return, with the new heaven and earth.

They also handle verse 28 differently. Calvin favors the sense that receiving the kingdom gives believers grace for worship. Wesley urges holding fast the grace through which they serve. The BSB expresses the response as being filled with gratitude. These readings connect God’s gift with reverent service, rather than making the kingdom an achievement of human effort.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

2 Peter 3:10–13 likewise joins the passing of the present order with holy living and the expectation of a new heaven and earth where righteousness dwells. Future hope shapes present conduct.

1 Corinthians 15:24–28 describes Christ’s victory over every enemy and the kingdom brought to its appointed completion. The lasting kingdom of Hebrews belongs within that purpose, rather than the security of something people can build for themselves.
