Passage

Acts 9:10-19

Why does Jesus send Ananias to Saul?

Berean Standard Bible

10 In Damascus there was a disciple named Ananias. The Lord spoke to him in a vision, “Ananias!” “Here I am, Lord,” he answered. 11 “Get up!” the Lord told him. “Go to the house of Judas on Straight Street and ask for a man from Tarsus named Saul, for he is praying. 12 In a vision he has seen a man named Ananias come and place his hands on him to restore his sight.” 13 But Ananias answered, “Lord, many people have told me about this man and all the harm he has done to Your saints in Jerusalem. 14 And now he is here with authority from the chief priests to arrest all who call on Your name.” 15 “Go!” said the Lord. “This man is My chosen instrument to carry My name before the Gentiles and their kings, and before the people of Israel. 16 I will show him how much he must suffer for My name.”

17 So Ananias went to the house, and when he arrived, he placed his hands on Saul. “Brother Saul,” he said, “the Lord Jesus, who appeared to you on the road as you were coming here, has sent me so that you may see again and be filled with the Holy Spirit.” 18 At that instant, something like scales fell from Saul’s eyes, and his sight was restored. He got up and was baptized, 19 and after taking some food, he regained his strength. And he spent several days with the disciples in Damascus.

What is the context?

The Lord speaks to Ananias, a disciple in Damascus, and gives him an address and a person to find. Saul is praying and has seen a vision of Ananias laying hands on him so that he may see again. God is preparing both men for a meeting neither has arranged.

Ananias knows about Saul’s violence and the authority behind his visit. His concern is specific and understandable. Jesus repeats the command to go and identifies Saul as His chosen instrument to carry His name before Gentiles, kings and Israel. The calling includes suffering; it is not a promise of an easy life.

Ananias goes, lays hands on Saul and addresses him as a brother. He says Jesus has sent him for the restoration of Saul’s sight and his filling with the Holy Spirit. Something like scales falls from Saul’s eyes, he sees again and is baptized. Food restores his strength, and he spends several days with the Damascus disciples. The community he came to imprison now receives him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see Ananias as an obedient disciple through whom Jesus restores and welcomes Saul. They differ over how baptism relates to the forgiveness and new life involved in his response.

The Catholic Church’s Catechism, paragraph 1213, teaches that through baptism people are freed from sin, born into God’s family and joined to Christ and the church. The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 28, paragraphs 5–6, teaches that God truly gives the grace baptism promises, while denying that salvation is inseparably tied to receiving it or that its effect must occur at the moment it is performed.

The later retelling in Acts 22:12–16 includes Ananias’s call to baptism, washing away sins and calling on Jesus’ name. The two accounts belong together. Here Luke gives the sequence of restoration, baptism and food without stopping to define the exact instant at which every part of Saul’s response takes place.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus names a purpose that reaches beyond Saul’s own recovery: witness before the nations, rulers and Israel. Galatians 1:15–16 gives Paul’s own account of being called by grace so that he might proclaim God’s Son among the Gentiles. This is an explicit explanation of his calling, rather than a hidden meaning assigned to his blindness.

Jesus also brings Saul into contact with a disciple who has reason to fear him. The commission comes from the Lord, yet its outworking includes prayer, an obedient visit and the help of the people Saul has harmed. God’s larger purpose does not make those personal acts of care unimportant.

What is our position?

Baptism addresses this same encounter through Ananias’s words in Acts 22:16. The study connects the washing away of sins with calling on Jesus in genuine faith and rejects the idea that the water acts independently of faith. Saul’s baptism belongs to his response to Jesus; the study does not treat the physical water as having saving power of its own.
