Passage

John 8:21-30

Why does Jesus point His listeners toward the cross?

Berean Standard Bible

21 Again He said to them, “I am going away, and you will look for Me, but you will die in your sin. Where I am going, you cannot come.” 22 So the Jews began to ask, “Will He kill Himself, since He says, ‘Where I am going, you cannot come’ ?” 23 Then He told them, “You are from below; I am from above. You are of this world; I am not of this world. 24 That is why I told you that you would die in your sins. For unless you believe that I am He, you will die in your sins.”

25 “Who are You?” they asked. “Just what I have been telling you from the beginning,” Jesus replied. 26 “I have much to say about you and much to judge. But the One who sent Me is truthful, and what I have heard from Him, I tell the world.” 27 They did not understand that He was telling them about the Father.

28 So Jesus said, “When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am He, and that I do nothing on My own, but speak exactly what the Father has taught Me. 29 He who sent Me is with Me. He has not left Me alone, because I always do what pleases Him.” 30 As Jesus spoke these things, many believed in Him.

What is the context?

Jesus continues speaking about where He comes from and where He is going. His listeners wonder whether His departure means suicide. He redirects them to the real danger: dying in their sins through unbelief. His contrast between above and below is about His origin with the Father and their outlook in a world estranged from God, not a claim that some people are worth less than others.

When they ask who He is, Jesus refers them back to what He has been telling them. The BSB renders His reply as a reminder of His earlier teaching. Verse 27 then says they did not understand that He was speaking about the Father. We should not fill in a complete picture of their understanding or motives when John himself describes their misunderstanding.

The turning point is His coming death. He says they will know who He is when they lift up the Son of Man. Later, John 12:32–33 explicitly explains lifting up as a reference to the kind of death Jesus would die. The cross is therefore central to this exchange, even though His listeners do not yet grasp it. His obedience to the Father continues through what lies ahead; He does not speak or act as an independent rival to the Father.

John ends this part by saying that many believed in Him. The next paragraph turns to continuing in His word. Keeping those statements together helps us hear both the response to His teaching and the call to a life shaped by it, without assuming that we know the final spiritual condition of every person present.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings share the central warning: sin is not overcome by rejecting Jesus, and faith in Him matters for life with God. They also connect His being lifted up with the cross. The details of His self-identification and the recognition He promises are explained in more than one way.

The words translated “I am He” receive different emphasis. John Wesley’s notes on verses 24 and 28 see a claim to the divine name. The Catholic bishops’ Bible note on these verses connects them with God’s self-identification in Isaiah 43:10. John Calvin’s commentary on verses 24 and 28 instead stresses recognizing Jesus as the promised Messiah and the One carrying out the Father’s saving work. This describes their readings of these particular verses; it does not mean Calvin denies Christ’s deity.

They also differ over the knowledge promised in verse 28. Wesley includes people coming to recognize and believe in Jesus through His crucifixion. Calvin emphasizes opponents being forced to recognize the authority they rejected, which does not necessarily mean repentance. Neither reading makes the cross a defeat of the Father’s purpose.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus places the cross within His obedience to the Father. What His opponents will do to Him does not turn His mission into a failed attempt at rescue. In John 12:27–33, He describes His coming hour as the purpose for which He came, asks that the Father be glorified, and connects His death with drawing people to Himself.

This gives the warning about sin a wider setting: the One warning His listeners is also going to the cross. God’s purpose in Christ includes redemption and forgiveness, as Ephesians 1:7–10 explains. The passage holds together the seriousness of refusing Him and the saving work He has come to complete.
