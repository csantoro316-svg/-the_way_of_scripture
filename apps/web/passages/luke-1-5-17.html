Passage

Luke 1:5-17

Why is John’s birth announced before Jesus’?

Berean Standard Bible

5 In the time of Herod king of Judea there was a priest named Zechariah, who belonged to the priestly division of Abijah, and whose wife Elizabeth was a descendant of Aaron. 6 Both of them were righteous in the sight of God, walking blamelessly in all the commandments and decrees of the Lord. 7 But they had no children, because Elizabeth was barren, and they were both well along in years.

8 One day while Zechariah’s division was on duty and he was serving as priest before God, 9 he was chosen by lot, according to the custom of the priesthood, to enter the temple of the Lord and burn incense. 10 And at the hour of the incense offering, the whole congregation was praying outside. 11 Just then an angel of the Lord appeared to Zechariah, standing at the right side of the altar of incense. 12 When Zechariah saw him, he was startled and gripped with fear.

13 But the angel said to him, “Do not be afraid, Zechariah, because your prayer has been heard. Your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you are to give him the name John. 14 He will be a joy and delight to you, and many will rejoice at his birth, 15 for he will be great in the sight of the Lord. He shall never take wine or strong drink, and he will be filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother’s womb. 16 Many of the sons of Israel he will turn back to the Lord their God. 17 And he will go on before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to their children and the disobedient to the wisdom of the righteous—to make ready a people prepared for the Lord.”

What is the context?

Luke moves from his introduction into the time of Herod, king of Judea. Zechariah serves as a priest in the division of Abijah, and Elizabeth also comes from Aaron’s family. The divisions belong to the priestly order described in 1 Chronicles, where Abijah is named among the twenty-four groups. (1 Chronicles 24:1–19)

The couple are described as righteous and faithful to God’s commands, yet they have no child and are well along in years. Luke puts their faithfulness and their sorrow together. Their childlessness is not presented as proof that they have failed to please God.

While the congregation prays outside, Zechariah is chosen by lot to burn incense inside the temple. An angel appears and tells him that his prayer has been heard: Elizabeth will bear a son named John. The promised joy will reach beyond their household because John has a work to do among the people of Israel.

John will be set apart for God’s service, filled with the Holy Spirit from his mother’s womb, and sent to turn people back to the Lord. His work is described in terms of Elijah’s spirit and power, including reconciliation within families and a turning from disobedience. The announcement reaches back to Malachi’s promise of a messenger preparing the Lord’s way. (Malachi 3:1; Malachi 4)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian traditions commonly read John as the promised forerunner who prepares people for Jesus, serving with the kind of Spirit-given power associated with Elijah. Calvin, Wesley, and the Catholic bishops’ notes connect his work with Malachi’s promise and the call to return to God. (Calvin on Luke 1:16–17; Wesley on Luke 1:16–17; USCCB notes on Luke 1:17)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus later identifies John as the messenger who prepares His way and explains his connection with Elijah. John himself denies being Elijah in person and directs attention to the One coming after him. Luke’s description of spirit and power helps us understand the connection as a God-given role and work, rather than the same person living another life. (Matthew 11:7–15; Matthew 17:9–13; John 1:19–34)

Later in the chapter, Zechariah connects these events with God remembering His covenant with Abraham. He describes John’s task as giving people knowledge of salvation through forgiveness of sins. The family’s joy is part of that wider mercy: God is preparing people to receive the Savior He promised. Paul’s account of Israel’s story in Acts likewise places John’s call to repentance immediately before the arrival of Jesus. (Luke 1:67–79; Acts 13:16–26)
