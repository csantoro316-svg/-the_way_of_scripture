Passage

Isaiah 63:7-14

What does remembering the exodus reveal about God’s care?

Berean Standard Bible

7 I will make known the LORD’s loving devotion and His praiseworthy acts, because of all that the LORD has done for us— the many good things for the house of Israel according to His great compassion and loving devotion. 8 For He said, “They are surely My people, sons who will not be disloyal.” So He became their Savior. 9 In all their distress, He too was afflicted, and the Angel of His Presence saved them. In His love and compassion He redeemed them; He lifted them up and carried them all the days of old. 10 But they rebelled and grieved His Holy Spirit. So He turned and became their enemy, and He Himself fought against them.

11 Then His people remembered the days of old, the days of Moses. Where is He who brought them through the sea with the shepherds of His flock? Where is the One who set His Holy Spirit among them, 12 who sent His glorious arm to lead them by the right hand of Moses, who divided the waters before them to gain for Himself everlasting renown, 13 who led them through the depths like a horse in the wilderness, so that they did not stumble? 14 Like cattle going down to the valley, the Spirit of the LORD gave them rest. You led Your people this way to make for Yourself a glorious name.

What is the context?

The voice changes to praise and remembrance. The speaker recounts the Lord’s compassion and loving devotion toward the house of Israel. He became their Savior, shared their distress, and redeemed and carried them in love.

The Angel of His Presence is named in that rescue. Yet the people rebelled and grieved His Holy Spirit, and the relationship turned into conflict: the One who had saved them fought against them.

The memory then returns to Moses and the journey through the sea. Repeated questions ask where the God who did these things is now. His Spirit had been among them, His glorious arm had led them, and He had made a path through the waters.

The horse moving without stumbling and cattle descending to rest picture the care of His leading. The remembrance acknowledges both His goodness and the people’s rebellion, while recalling that His guidance made His name glorious.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The USCCB notes understand this as the beginning of a community lament that remembers the exodus before asking for help again. Henry and Wesley likewise connect the remembered rescue with the appeal that follows.

Henry and Wesley identify the Angel of His Presence with the Son of God, reading the rescue through His care for the people. They also emphasize the seriousness of rebellion against the Spirit who had guided them.

The remembrance is therefore both praise and an honest account of failure. It recalls the Lord’s faithfulness without making His past kindness an excuse to ignore the people’s response.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ephesians 4:25–32 takes up the warning about grieving God’s Holy Spirit. Paul addresses people sealed for the day of redemption and calls them to leave falsehood, stealing, destructive speech and malice behind.

The call ends with kindness, compassion and forgiveness patterned on God’s forgiveness in Christ. Isaiah’s memory of rescue and rebellion thus has a direct connection with the life of redeemed people: receiving God’s care calls for conduct that honors His Spirit and serves others.
