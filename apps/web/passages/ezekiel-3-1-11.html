Passage

Ezekiel 3:1-11

How can Ezekiel carry a word his people do not want to hear?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Son of man,” He said to me, “eat what you find here. Eat this scroll, then go and speak to the house of Israel.” 2 So I opened my mouth, and He fed me the scroll. 3 “Son of man,” He said to me, “eat and fill your stomach with this scroll I am giving you.” So I ate, and it was as sweet as honey in my mouth.

4 Then He said to me, “Son of man, go now to the house of Israel and speak My words to them. 5 For you are not being sent to a people of unfamiliar speech or difficult language, but to the house of Israel— 6 not to the many peoples of unfamiliar speech and difficult language whose words you cannot understand. Surely if I had sent you to them, they would have listened to you. 7 But the house of Israel will be unwilling to listen to you, since they are unwilling to listen to Me. For the whole house of Israel is hard-headed and hard-hearted.

8 Behold, I will make your face as hard as their faces, and your forehead as hard as their foreheads. 9 I will make your forehead like a diamond, harder than flint. Do not be afraid of them or dismayed at their presence, even though they are a rebellious house.” 10 “Son of man,” He added, “listen carefully to all the words I speak to you, and take them to heart. 11 Go to your people, the exiles; speak to them and tell them, ‘This is what the Lord GOD says,’ whether they listen or refuse to listen.”

What is the context?

Ezekiel obeys the command to eat, and the Lord feeds him the scroll. It fills him and tastes as sweet as honey. The scroll’s words of mourning are now the message he must receive and carry.

He is sent to the house of Israel, whose language he understands. The barrier is their unwillingness to hear the Lord, rather than a need for translation. The Lord adds that unfamiliar peoples would have listened if Ezekiel had been sent to them.

The language of hardness now has a second use. Against the people’s hard faces and hearts, the Lord gives Ezekiel a face and forehead strong enough to persist. His resolve is compared with diamond harder than flint.

The closing instructions join inward reception with outward speech. He must listen carefully, take all the Lord’s words to heart, then speak to the exiles whether they listen or refuse.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley understand eating the scroll as receiving and taking in God’s word before delivering it. They connect its sweetness with the goodness of receiving revelation from Him, even when the message announces painful things.

Both emphasize that Ezekiel must speak the words he is given and continue despite resistance. The strengthened forehead is the resolve needed for that work.

Wesley also distinguishes the widespread refusal described in the passage from a claim that every individual Israelite will reject the message. The accusation addresses the resistant community without requiring the same response from every person.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 10:8–11 takes up the action of receiving and eating a scroll that tastes sweet as honey. John is then told that he must prophesy again about peoples, nations, languages and kings.

There is a difference as well: John’s stomach becomes bitter, a detail not stated about Ezekiel’s stomach here. The common action connects receiving revelation with the responsibility to make it known.

The wider horizon in Revelation shows the message reaching beyond one community. God entrusts His word to servants for proclamation, including words that are difficult for both messenger and hearer to face.
