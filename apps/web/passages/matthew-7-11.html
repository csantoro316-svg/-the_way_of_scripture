Passage

Matthew 7:11

The Father's Good Gifts

Berean Standard Bible

11 So if you who are evil know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!

What is the context?

This verse completes the asking, seeking, and knocking teaching in Matthew 7:7–11. Jesus has used bread, fish, a stone, and a snake to compare a parent's response to a child with the Father's response to His people. The comparison directs attention to the giver's goodness.

Jesus describes His hearers as evil while acknowledging that they know how to give good gifts. His reasoning moves from imperfect human parents to the greater goodness of the Father. His character is the reason for confidence in approaching Him.

Matthew describes the Father's gifts as good things. The related saying in Luke 11:9–13 specifically names the Holy Spirit. Both accounts direct the hearer's confidence toward the generous Father.

The Father is also the One whose will Jesus has taught His followers to seek in Matthew 6:9–13. Confidence in His good gifts belongs with that relationship of trust and submission.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read these verses as encouragement to keep praying with confidence in the Father's goodness. Wesley on Matthew 7:7–11, the Reformed Westminster Larger Catechism, questions 184–185, and the Catholic Compendium, question 575, all direct asking toward God's will and the good He gives, rather than treating Him as a means of obtaining whatever the person wants.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise of the Spirit in Luke 11:13 points toward the gift that brings God's children into a lived relationship with Him. Romans 8:14–17 describes the Spirit leading them, enabling their cry to the Father, and bearing witness that they belong to His family.

Ephesians 1:13–14 also connects the Spirit with the future inheritance God promises. The Father's good gift reaches beyond a single circumstance: He gives His people His presence and a hope that extends to their final redemption.

What is our position?

Our study Prayer and Faith understands the Father's good things in the light of Luke's explicit promise of the Holy Spirit. It reads the passage as directing faith toward God's presence, power, and purposes, rather than guaranteeing a preferred possession or circumstance. Our companion study What Does Prayer Look Like? develops the same focus while distinguishing surrender from a rule that asking is sinful.
