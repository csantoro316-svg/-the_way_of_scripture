Passage

Ruth 1:15-18

What is Ruth choosing when she chooses to stay?

Berean Standard Bible

15 “Look,” said Naomi, “your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and her gods; follow her back home.” 16 But Ruth replied: “Do not urge me to leave you or to turn from following you. For wherever you go, I will go, and wherever you live, I will live; your people will be my people, and your God will be my God. 17 Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. May the LORD punish me, and ever so severely, if anything but death separates you and me.” 18 When Naomi saw that Ruth was determined to go with her, she stopped trying to persuade her.

What is the context?

After Orpah leaves, Naomi urges Ruth to follow her back to her people and gods. Ruth refuses. She commits herself to Naomi’s journey, home, people, God, death, and burial. Her answer makes clear that staying is more than continuing a trip together.

The previous verse says Ruth clings to Naomi. The Hebrew verb דָּבַק, dabaq (dah-BAHK), means to cling or remain close. Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically places Ruth’s attachment here among uses expressing loyalty and affection. Her following speech explains the depth of that attachment in her own words.

Ruth invokes the LORD in a solemn pledge that only death will separate them. She accepts a future among Naomi’s people and with Naomi’s God despite the uncertainty Naomi has described. When Naomi sees that she is determined, she stops trying to turn her back.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Henry read Ruth’s words as a deliberate commitment to the God of Israel as well as affection for Naomi. Henry emphasizes God’s grace in her choice; Wesley draws attention to the lasting character of her promise, even concerning burial.

The USCCB Bible study notes likewise explain the speech as a profound oath of loyalty, including belonging with Naomi’s family in death. These readers recognize both parts of the commitment: Ruth attaches herself to a person she loves and to that person’s people and God.

Wesley and Henry also draw a Christian illustration from the contrast with Orpah, comparing attachment that ends when the cost rises with commitment that continues. That illustration is their application. The chapter records Ruth’s confession clearly without telling the later course of Orpah’s life.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ruth’s decision offers a comparison with the costly allegiance Jesus describes in Luke 14:25–33. He calls people to weigh what following Him involves, including claims of family, possessions, and personal safety. Ruth’s pledge is made within her relationship with Naomi; Jesus’ call centers on allegiance to Himself.

This call follows His story of a banquet whose invitation reaches people left outside the expected guest list in Luke 14:15–24. Together, the passages show both welcome and a serious response to it. The wider hope is life in God’s kingdom, with loyalty that reaches beyond what is immediately comfortable or secure.
