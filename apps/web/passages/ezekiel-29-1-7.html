Passage

Ezekiel 29:1-7

Why is Pharaoh pictured as a monster caught in his own river?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In the tenth year, on the twelfth day of the tenth month, the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, set your face against Pharaoh king of Egypt and prophesy against him and against all Egypt. 3 Speak to him and tell him that this is what the Lord GOD says: Behold, I am against you, O Pharaoh king of Egypt, O great monster who lies among his rivers, who says, ‘The Nile is mine; I made it myself.’

4 But I will put hooks in your jaws and cause the fish of your streams to cling to your scales. I will haul you up out of your rivers, and all the fish of your streams will cling to your scales. 5 I will leave you in the desert, you and all the fish of your streams. You will fall on the open field and will not be taken away or gathered for burial. I have given you as food to the beasts of the earth and the birds of the air. 6 Then all the people of Egypt will know that I am the LORD. For you were only a staff of reeds to the house of Israel. 7 When Israel took hold of you with their hands, you splintered, tearing all their shoulders; when they leaned on you, you broke, and their backs were wrenched.

What is the context?

The Lord directs Ezekiel against Pharaoh and Egypt. The ruler boasts that the Nile belongs to him and that he made it. The image places him at ease in the very waters he claims as his own creation.

The Hebrew word תַּנִּין, tannin (tah-NEEN), is used for a serpent, dragon or sea creature in Brown–Driver–Briggs. Here the river monster pictures Pharaoh. The Lord will hook its jaws, haul it out with the fish clinging to it, and leave it exposed as food for animals and birds.

The accusation then changes pictures: Egypt has been a staff of reeds for Israel. Instead of supporting those who lean on it, it breaks and injures them. Jeremiah 37:4–11 describes Egypt’s attempted help during Jerusalem’s siege and the Lord’s warning that it would not deliver the city.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads the monster as a crocodile and the fish as the people attached to their king. The USCCB notes also explain the Nile crocodile picture while noting the word’s broader associations with a threatening sea monster.

Henry emphasizes pride in possessions and the danger of a false source of security. The two pictures belong together: Egypt claims a power it does not possess and fails the people who depend on it.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A supported parallel appears in Revelation 19:11–21, where the rider called the Word of God defeats opposing rulers and armies, and birds feed on the slain. Both passages expose power that cannot protect itself against the Lord.

This shared judgment picture does not establish that every detail in Ezekiel describes the same event or date. Revelation carries the larger theme toward Christ’s victory over the powers gathered against Him. Their rank and strength do not decide the outcome.
