Passage

Isaiah 54:1-17

Can the forsaken city become a home overflowing with children?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Shout for joy, O barren woman, who bears no children; break forth in song and cry aloud, you who have never travailed; because more are the children of the desolate woman than of her who has a husband,” says the LORD. 2 “Enlarge the site of your tent, stretch out the curtains of your dwellings, do not hold back. Lengthen your ropes and drive your stakes in deep. 3 For you will spread out to the right and left; your descendants will dispossess the nations and inhabit the desolate cities.

4 Do not be afraid, for you will not be put to shame; do not be intimidated, for you will not be humiliated. For you will forget the shame of your youth and will remember no more the reproach of your widowhood. 5 For your husband is your Maker— the LORD of Hosts is His name— the Holy One of Israel is your Redeemer; He is called the God of all the earth. 6 For the LORD has called you back, like a wife deserted and wounded in spirit, like the rejected wife of one’s youth,” says your God. 7 “For a brief moment I forsook you, but with great compassion I will bring you back. 8 In a surge of anger I hid My face from you for a moment, but with everlasting kindness I will have compassion on you,” says the LORD your Redeemer.

9 “For to Me this is like the days of Noah, when I swore that the waters of Noah would never again cover the earth. So I have sworn that I will not be angry with you or rebuke you. 10 Though the mountains may be removed and the hills may be shaken, My loving devotion will not depart from you, and My covenant of peace will not be broken,” says the LORD, who has compassion on you.

11 “O afflicted city, lashed by storms, without solace, surely I will set your stones in antimony and lay your foundations with sapphires. 12 I will make your pinnacles of rubies, your gates of sparkling jewels, and all your walls of precious stones. 13 Then all your sons will be taught by the LORD, and great will be their prosperity. 14 In righteousness you will be established, far from oppression, for you will have no fear. Terror will be far removed, for it will not come near you.

15 If anyone attacks you, it is not from Me; whoever assails you will fall before you. 16 Behold, I have created the craftsman who fans the coals into flame and forges a weapon fit for its task; and I have created the destroyer to wreak havoc. 17 No weapon formed against you shall prosper, and you will refute every tongue that accuses you. This is the heritage of the servants of the LORD, and their vindication is from Me,” declares the LORD.

What is the context?

After the Servant’s suffering and promised success, Jerusalem is addressed as a woman who has borne no children. She is told to sing and make her tent larger, because a growing family will fill it. Desolate cities will be inhabited again.

The next picture is of a deserted wife called back by her husband. Her Maker is her husband, the Holy One of Israel her Redeemer, and His rule reaches the whole earth. The Lord contrasts a brief time of anger with the lasting kindness of His promised compassion.

The comparison with Noah emphasizes the reliability of His promise. Even if mountains move and hills shake, His loving devotion and covenant of peace will endure.

The afflicted city is then pictured with precious stones, beautiful gates and secure walls. Her children will be taught by the Lord, and righteousness will replace oppression and terror.

The closing assurance concerns attacks and accusations. The Lord made both those who forge weapons and those who use them; they cannot establish the final outcome against His purpose. He declares that the servants’ vindication comes from Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The USCCB notes explain the opening picture through Jerusalem receiving returning exiles as children, and recognize Paul’s later use of it in a new setting. The marriage picture emphasizes the tenderness with which the Lord restores His people.

Henry and Wesley give prominent attention to the church and the gathering of Gentiles through the gospel. They connect the enlarged tent with a growing family of believers, and the lasting kindness with God’s faithfulness to those He brings to Himself.

These explanations differ in the setting they emphasize, while treating restoration and God’s dependable mercy as central. The promise to the afflicted city should be read as part of that whole account, rather than as a detached assurance that a person will never experience injury or accusation.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Galatians 4:21–31 directly quotes the opening call to the barren woman. Paul uses it in his explanation of the free Jerusalem above and calls believers children of promise, like Isaac. The promised family therefore has an explicit New Testament connection to people brought into freedom through God’s promise.

Jesus also quotes the promise of being taught by God in John 6:41–51. He connects hearing and learning from the Father with coming to Him, believing and receiving eternal life.

The jeweled city provides a further parallel with Revelation 21:18–27, where precious foundations and gates accompany the presence of God and the Lamb. Revelation does not quote this chapter there, but the shared picture carries restoration beyond repaired buildings to a people living in God’s light.
