Passage

Mark 10:35-45

The King Who Gives His Life to Serve

Berean Standard Bible

35 Then James and John, the sons of Zebedee, came to Jesus and declared, “Teacher, we want You to do for us whatever we ask.” 36 “What do you want Me to do for you?” He inquired. 37 They answered, “Grant that one of us may sit at Your right hand and the other at Your left in Your glory.” 38 “You do not know what you are asking,” Jesus replied. “Can you drink the cup I will drink, or be baptized with the baptism I will undergo?” 39 “We can,” the brothers answered. “You will drink the cup that I drink,” Jesus said, “and you will be baptized with the baptism that I undergo. 40 But to sit at My right or left is not Mine to grant. These seats belong to those for whom they have been prepared.”

41 When the ten heard about this, they became indignant with James and John. 42 So Jesus called them together and said, “You know that those regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their superiors exercise authority over them. 43 But it shall not be this way among you. Instead, whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, 44 and whoever wants to be first must be the slave of all. 45 For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many.”

What is the context?

James and John ask for the places at Jesus' right and left in His glory, immediately after His announcement of suffering. He answers by asking about the cup He will drink and the baptism He will undergo. They say they can share them; He tells them they will, while refusing to promise the seats they request.

The cup and baptism describe suffering that lies ahead. Jesus will use the cup image again when He prays in Mark 14:32–36. Here the brothers seek the honor without understanding its cost. The prediction of their sharing suffering does not mean that their deaths will do the same saving work as His.

The other ten become indignant. Jesus gathers them all and contrasts the domination associated with rulers with the service He expects among His followers. Anyone seeking greatness must serve, and anyone wanting to be first must become the slave of all. The correction reaches the whole group, rather than leaving the ten as judges of the two.

He grounds this calling in His own mission: the Son of Man gives His life as a ransom for many. The Greek noun λύτρον (lytron, LOO-tron) means a price of release. Here His life is given to free others from the misery and penalty of sin. His death is both saving service and the pattern that confronts their ambition.

Matthew 20:20–28 includes the brothers' mother in the request and explicitly names the Father as the one who prepares the places. Mark gives the brothers' request directly. These added details need not be turned into a claim that a known family relationship with Jesus explains their motives.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the cup and baptism as suffering, and the instruction about greatness as a call to humble service. The USCCB notes on Mark 10:38–45 connect that service with Jesus' death for others; Wesley on verses 38–45 likewise connects suffering, glory, and the giving of His life.

Traditions differ over the scope of the ransom. The Catholic Church teaches that Christ died for everyone without exception, taking many as a contrast between the one Redeemer and humanity as a whole (Catechism 605). The Methodist Articles XX also describe His sacrifice as being for the sins of the whole world.

Reformed churches following Westminster 8.5 and 8.8, and Baptists following the 1689 Confession 8.5 and 8.8, teach that His death secures redemption for those the Father has given Him, and that He brings them to faith and gives them the inheritance He has won. These are the cited confessions' teachings, rather than every Baptist's or Protestant's position. The shared conviction is that His self-giving death rescues people from sin.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The language strongly parallels Isaiah 53:10–12: the Servant gives His life, bears the sins of many, and brings others into a right relationship with God. Jesus' promised death belongs within that picture of one serving by giving Himself for others. The cost He asks the disciples to face grows out of the rescue He accomplishes, not a competition to outrank one another.

Philippians 2:3–13 brings the same pattern into the church's life. Believers are to put selfish ambition aside and attend to others, taking the attitude of Christ in His humility, obedience to the cross, and exaltation. God is at work in them to shape both their willing and doing.

Sharing Christ's sufferings also becomes part of the church's experience. Acts 12:1–2 records James' death, and 1 Peter 4:12–19 encourages those suffering as Christians to entrust themselves to their faithful Creator and continue doing good. Their calling is faithful service in the face of cost, while Christ remains the One whose life brings the ransom.
