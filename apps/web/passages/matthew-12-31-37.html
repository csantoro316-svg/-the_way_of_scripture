Passage

Matthew 12:31-37

The Spirit’s Work and the Words of the Heart

Berean Standard Bible

31 Therefore I tell you, every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven men, but the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven. 32 Whoever speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven, but whoever speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the one to come.

33 Make a tree good and its fruit will be good, or make a tree bad and its fruit will be bad, for a tree is known by its fruit. 34 You brood of vipers, how can you who are evil say anything good? For out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks. 35 The good man brings good things out of his good store of treasure, and the evil man brings evil things out of his evil store of treasure.

36 But I tell you that men will give an account on the day of judgment for every careless word they have spoken. 37 For by your words you will be acquitted, and by your words you will be condemned.”

What is the context?

The warning follows the accusation that Jesus drives out demons by evil power. He has just identified His work with God's Spirit. His words about blasphemy must be heard within that confrontation: what has brought freedom is being called the work of Satan.

Jesus speaks both of the breadth of forgiveness and of a grave exception concerning speech against the Holy Spirit. He distinguishes it from a word spoken against the Son of Man, and says that it will not be forgiven in this age or the one to come. Mark 3:28–30 directly connects the warning with the accusation that He has an unclean spirit.

The tree and fruit then turn attention to what words reveal. Jesus challenges His hearers about recognizing good and evil, and describes speech as coming from what fills the heart. The accusation is not simply a harmless sound; it gives expression to what is being welcomed or resisted within.

The warning broadens to the day of judgment. The Greek word translated careless is ἀργός, argos (ar-GOSS), idle or unproductive. Thayer's entry applies it here to harmful, unprofitable speech. Jesus treats words as matters for which people must answer. His closing statement belongs with the preceding explanation that speech reveals the heart.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The warning is commonly read in light of the accusation just made against Jesus. Christian teachers differ over how it applies beyond that moment. Wesley on Matthew 12:31–37 identifies the blasphemy with calling the miracles Jesus did by the Holy Spirit the devil's work. He treats words at judgment as evidence of the person's faith and life.

The Reformed teacher John Calvin, on Matthew 12:31–32, understands it as deliberately calling God's revealed work evil, even while knowing its source. He distinguishes this from opposition that comes from ignorance. In his reading, people hardened in this way do not turn back in repentance. The Reformed Westminster Confession, 15.3–4, affirms that forgiveness is God's grace and no sin condemns those who truly repent.

The Catholic Church's Catechism, 1864, explains the warning through deliberately refusing the mercy and forgiveness offered by the Spirit, a hardening that can end in dying without repentance. This differs from Calvin's more specific account of consciously slandering God's revealed work. The readings share the seriousness of rejecting God's grace, while describing the offense in different ways.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The heart-and-words connection belongs to a larger biblical need for inward renewal. In Ezekiel 36:25–27, God promises cleansing, a new heart, and His Spirit enabling His people to walk in His ways. The answer to a corrupted heart reaches deeper than improving outward appearances.

Titus 3:3–7 describes God's mercy bringing new birth and renewal through the Holy Spirit, poured out through Jesus Christ. That wider connection shows why the Spirit's work matters so deeply: He is at work bringing the new life people need. The warning here and the promise of renewal belong within the Bible's serious account of sin and God's saving mercy.
