Passage

Galatians 5:1-6

Why must they stand firm in the freedom Christ gives?

Berean Standard Bible

1 It is for freedom that Christ has set us free. Stand firm, then, and do not be encumbered once more by a yoke of slavery. 2 Take notice: I, Paul, tell you that if you let yourselves be circumcised, Christ will be of no value to you at all. 3 Again I testify to every man who gets himself circumcised that he is obligated to obey the whole law. 4 You who are trying to be justified by the law have been severed from Christ; you have fallen away from grace.

5 But by faith we eagerly await through the Spirit the hope of righteousness. 6 For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision has any value. What matters is faith expressing itself through love.

What is the context?

Paul draws the conclusion of his comparison of the free woman and the slave woman: Christ has set His people free, and they must stand firm in that freedom. Taking on the yoke again would reverse the direction of the gospel he has been explaining.

His warning about circumcision belongs in that setting. Verse 4 identifies the issue as trying to be justified by the law. Accepting circumcision on those terms places a person under an obligation to keep the whole law, while treating Christ as unable to supply what is needed.

Paul uses severe language: those seeking justification that way have been severed from Christ and have fallen away from grace. The warning calls for a decisive return to dependence on Christ.

He then describes the hope of those who wait through the Spirit, by faith. Their future rests with God. In Christ, neither being circumcised nor being uncircumcised supplies the standing they need. What matters is faith expressing itself through love.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand this as a warning against seeking justification through the law and circumcision. Wesley’s notes on verses 1–6 and the USCCB notes on verses 1–6 both connect freedom in Christ with faith that acts through love.

Christians differ over how the warning about falling from grace relates to a believer’s final salvation. The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 17, teaches that those truly accepted in Christ will persevere to the end, while acknowledging that they can fall into serious sin and suffer its consequences. Within that framework, the warning cannot mean that God finally abandons someone He has truly saved.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, article XII, teach that people who have received the Holy Spirit may depart from grace and fall into sin, and that God’s grace makes repentance and restoration possible. The Catholic Catechism, paragraph 162, likewise teaches that faith is God’s free gift and can be lost. These teachings allow the warning to address a real turning away from grace already received. They also leave room for return and repentance.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Romans 8:23–25 connects the Spirit’s presence with waiting for the redemption of our bodies. Hope concerns what God has promised and what His people do not yet see.

That larger horizon helps explain the waiting in verse 5. Life through the Spirit includes confidence in God’s coming completion of His work. The freedom Christ gives leads His people toward that hope, with faith taking visible shape in love now.
