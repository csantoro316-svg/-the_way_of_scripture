Passage

Ezekiel 26:15-18

Why do the rulers of the sea tremble at Tyre’s fall?

Berean Standard Bible

15 This is what the Lord GOD says to Tyre: ‘Will not the coastlands quake at the sound of your downfall, when the wounded groan at the slaughter in your midst? 16 All the princes of the sea will descend from their thrones, remove their robes, and strip off their embroidered garments. Clothed with terror, they will sit on the ground, trembling every moment, appalled over you. 17 Then they will lament for you, saying, “How you have perished, O city of renown inhabited by seafaring men— she who was powerful on the sea, along with her people, who imposed terror on all peoples! 18 Now the coastlands tremble on the day of your downfall; the islands in the sea are dismayed by your demise.”’

What is the context?

The announcement turns from the city’s destruction to the reaction around it. The princes of the sea step down from their thrones, remove their fine clothing and sit trembling.

Their lament remembers Tyre’s reputation and power on the sea. Its fall makes the coastlands and islands afraid: a place that had imposed terror on others has itself become the cause of dismay.

The passage is also a reversal of Tyre’s own response to Jerusalem. Tyre welcomed its neighbor’s ruin; its own fall is met with a funeral song and fear.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads the princes as rulers of the islands and understands their trembling as fear for their own concerns. The USCCB notes connect these rulers with the coastal communities commercially linked to Tyre.

Henry draws attention to the collapse of apparently established prosperity. The lament exposes how deeply other rulers had tied their sense of security to this city.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A closely supported parallel appears in Revelation 18:7–10. Babylon claims that it will never know grief, but when judgment comes, the kings associated with its luxury stand at a distance in fear and lament its fall.

The parallel carries the exposure of worldly security into Revelation’s account of God’s judgment. It does not require Tyre and Babylon to be the same city. In both scenes, the rulers discover that impressive power and shared prosperity cannot protect the city on which they relied.
