Passage

Revelation 4:1-6

What does John see when heaven is opened to him?

Berean Standard Bible

1 After this I looked and saw a door standing open in heaven. And the voice I had previously heard speak to me like a trumpet was saying, “Come up here, and I will show you what must happen after these things.” 2 At once I was in the Spirit, and I saw a throne standing in heaven, with someone seated on it. 3 The One seated there looked like jasper and carnelian, and a rainbow that gleamed like an emerald encircled the throne.

4 Surrounding the throne were twenty-four other thrones, and on these thrones sat twenty-four elders dressed in white, with golden crowns on their heads. 5 From the throne came flashes of lightning, rumblings, and peals of thunder. Before the throne burned seven torches of fire. These are the seven spirits of God. 6 And before the throne was something like a sea of glass, as clear as crystal. In the center, around the throne, were four living creatures, covered with eyes in front and back.

What is the context?

After the letters to the seven churches, John sees an open door in heaven. The voice he heard before, compared with a trumpet, calls him to come and see what must happen next. He immediately describes himself as being in the Spirit. The vision moves from the churches’ circumstances to the throne at the center of heaven’s court.

John describes the One seated there through the brilliance of precious stones and an emerald-like rainbow. The comparisons convey overwhelming glory. He then describes twenty-four elders on surrounding thrones, dressed in white and wearing gold crowns.

Lightning, rumblings and thunder come from the throne. Seven burning torches stand before it, identified as the seven spirits of God. Something like a clear sea of glass lies before the throne, and four living creatures appear around it. Their description and worship continue in the following verses.

The scene recalls earlier visions of God’s glory. Ezekiel 1:22–28 includes a crystal-like expanse, a throne, radiant light and a rainbow. The thunder and lightning also recall God’s appearance at Sinai in Exodus 19:16–20. These connections help us recognize the majesty of the One John sees before trying to assign a separate meaning to every detail.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings broadly recognize a vision of God’s authority and glory. They differ over how its details relate to the church and the events that follow. Wesley understands John’s ascent as an experience in the Spirit. John Walvoord places the scene after Christ gathers the church to heaven, but explicitly says the invitation to John is not itself a direct description of that event. The words address John; their connection with a timetable for the whole church is an interpretation.

The elders receive several explanations. Wesley sees representatives of the saints and also considers an association with the Jewish people of God. Gill favors the whole gospel church, linking twenty-four with the priestly divisions. The USCCB notes understand the number as the twelve tribes together with the twelve apostles. These readings agree on the importance of God’s people while differing over precisely whom the elders represent.

The seven spirits are also understood differently. Wesley and Gill read them as the Holy Spirit in the fullness of His work. The USCCB notes identify seven angels attending God. The torches are expressly identified with the seven spirits in the passage, but the text does not go on to explain that phrase in the same detail.

The sea of glass has several proposed meanings. Wesley considers the life-giving work of the Spirit; Gill favors the gospel’s clarity and peace. The USCCB notes connect the image with the crystal-like expanse in Ezekiel’s vision. The comparison with crystal is explicit; a complete explanation of the image is not supplied here.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The heavenly throne has already appeared in Daniel 7:9–14, where earthly violence is brought before God’s court and the Son of Man receives an everlasting kingdom. That provides a scriptural parallel for the perspective Revelation now gives: events on earth unfold before the God whose rule outlasts every earthly power.

The rainbow can also recall Genesis 9:12–17, where God gives it as the sign of His covenant with Noah and living creatures. Reading that alongside the throne’s thunder brings His faithfulness and authority into the same picture. This is a connection between scriptural images, not a claim that John explains every part of the rainbow’s meaning. The vision prepares readers to hear the difficult events ahead in the presence of the One who remains sovereign and faithful.
