Passage

Matthew 15:21-28

A Canaanite Woman's Great Faith

Berean Standard Bible

21 Leaving that place, Jesus withdrew to the district of Tyre and Sidon. 22 And a Canaanite woman from that region came to Him, crying out, “Lord, Son of David, have mercy on me! My daughter is miserably possessed by a demon.” 23 But Jesus did not answer a word. So His disciples came and urged Him, “Send her away, for she keeps crying out after us.” 24 He answered, “I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.”

25 The woman came and knelt before Him. “Lord, help me!” she said. 26 But Jesus replied, “It is not right to take the children’s bread and toss it to the dogs.” 27 “Yes, Lord,” she said, “even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their master’s table.” 28 “O woman,” Jesus answered, “your faith is great! Let it be done for you as you desire.” And her daughter was healed from that very hour.

What is the context?

Jesus withdraws to the district of Tyre and Sidon. A Canaanite woman calls Him Lord and Son of David and asks for mercy for her demon-tormented daughter. Mark 7:24–30 identifies her as a Greek woman of Syrophoenician origin and describes the encounter at a house.

Jesus initially gives no answer, and the disciples ask Him to send her away. He then speaks of His mission to Israel's lost sheep. The woman comes and kneels before Him, continuing to ask for help. The conversation places her need alongside the priority of His mission to Israel.

Jesus uses the picture of children's bread and dogs. The Greek word is κυνάριον, kynarion (koo-NAH-ree-on), a little dog, according to Thayer's entry. The household meal picture gives her reply its force: even the dogs receive crumbs from their master's table. The word's form does not remove the difficult distinction in the exchange.

Her answer asks for mercy within that picture. Jesus praises her great faith and grants her request; her daughter is healed from that hour. The account ends with Jesus praising a Gentile woman's faith and extending His help to her family.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see persistent trust in Jesus' power and mercy, together with the reach of His help beyond Israel. Wesley on Matthew 15:23–28 reads the delay as a trial of faith and the final praise as recognition of her reliance on God's goodness. The US Catholic bishops' note on 15:28 likewise emphasizes Jesus granting the request in connection with her great faith. The mother's persistence and Jesus' final welcome are central to that reading.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The priority of Israel and mercy for the Gentiles belong together in Romans 15:7–13. Paul says Christ confirms God's promises to the patriarchs so that the Gentiles also glorify God for His mercy. He supports that hope from Israel's Scriptures. Mercy reaching this woman fits that larger purpose without requiring Israel's place to be erased.

The mission will become openly worldwide. The risen Jesus sends His disciples to make disciples of all nations in Matthew 28:18–20. Her encounter gives an early glimpse of faith and mercy crossing the boundary that the later commission will tell the disciples to cross.
