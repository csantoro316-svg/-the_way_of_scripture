Passage

Exodus 2:11-22

Why did Moses flee to Midian?

Berean Standard Bible

11 One day, after Moses had grown up, he went out to his own people and observed their hard labor. He saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his own people. 12 After looking this way and that and seeing no one, he struck down the Egyptian and hid his body in the sand. 13 The next day Moses went out and saw two Hebrews fighting. He asked the one in the wrong, “Why are you attacking your companion?” 14 But the man replied, “Who made you ruler and judge over us? Are you planning to kill me as you killed the Egyptian?” Then Moses was afraid and thought, “This thing I have done has surely become known.” 15 When Pharaoh heard about this matter, he sought to kill Moses. But Moses fled from Pharaoh and settled in the land of Midian, where he sat down beside a well.

16 Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters, and they came to draw water and fill the troughs to water their father’s flock. 17 And when some shepherds came along and drove them away, Moses rose up to help them and watered their flock. 18 When the daughters returned to their father Reuel, he asked them, “Why have you returned so early today?” 19 “An Egyptian rescued us from the shepherds,” they replied. “He even drew water for us and watered the flock.” 20 “So where is he?” their father asked. “Why did you leave the man behind? Invite him to have something to eat.” 21 Moses agreed to stay with the man, and he gave his daughter Zipporah to Moses in marriage. 22 And she gave birth to a son, and Moses named him Gershom, saying, “I have become a foreigner in a foreign land.”

What is the context?

As an adult, Moses goes out to his own people and sees their hard labor. When he sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, he looks around, kills the Egyptian and hides the body. The next day he tries to stop two Hebrews fighting, but the man in the wrong challenges his authority and mentions the killing.

Moses realizes that the matter is known. Pharaoh seeks his life, and he flees to Midian. His concern for his people has brought him into danger, and they have not recognized him as their ruler or judge.

At a well he again encounters people being mistreated. Shepherds drive away the daughters of the priest of Midian, and Moses helps the women and waters their flock. They describe him to their father Reuel as an Egyptian who rescued them.

Moses is welcomed into the household, marries Zipporah and has a son. He names the child Gershom and explains that he has become a foreigner in a foreign land. The man raised in Egypt’s royal household now lives away from both the court and his own people.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The killing receives different moral explanations. Calvin and Wesley understand Moses as acting under a special divine calling to deliver his people. They distinguish that reading from a general permission for private people to use violence. David Guzik instead understands Moses’ secrecy as a troubled conscience and his action as an attempt to deliver Israel through his own strength. These are the teachers’ readings of his motives and calling.

The household names also receive different explanations. The USCCB notes identify Reuel with Jethro, the name used for Moses’ father-in-law in the next chapter. Wesley suggests that Reuel is the grandfather and Jethro is the immediate father. The narrative here names Reuel and centers on Moses finding welcome and a family in Midian.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 11:24–26 explains Moses’ choice to identify with God’s people through faith. It connects his willingness to suffer with disgrace for Christ and his hope of reward. That is the New Testament’s own connection between this part of his life and the larger purpose of God.

Acts 7:23–37 explains that Moses expected his people to recognize God’s deliverance through him. Stephen later returns to the challenge about who made him ruler and judge: the rejected Moses is the very one God sends to lead them out. Within Stephen’s address, that pattern leads toward his warning about resistance to God’s messengers and the betrayal of the Righteous One in verses 51–53. The rejection does not cancel God’s purpose for the deliverer.
