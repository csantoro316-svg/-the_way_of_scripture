Passage

Exodus 7:14-25

What did the judgment on the Nile reveal?

Berean Standard Bible

14 Then the LORD said to Moses, “Pharaoh’s heart is unyielding; he refuses to let the people go. 15 Go to Pharaoh in the morning as you see him walking out to the water. Wait on the bank of the Nile to meet him, and take in your hand the staff that was changed into a snake. 16 Then say to him, ‘The LORD, the God of the Hebrews, has sent me to tell you: Let My people go, so that they may worship Me in the wilderness. But until now you have not listened. 17 This is what the LORD says: By this you will know that I am the LORD. Behold, with the staff in my hand I will strike the water of the Nile, and it will turn to blood. 18 The fish in the Nile will die, the river will stink, and the Egyptians will be unable to drink its water.’”

19 And the LORD said to Moses, “Tell Aaron, ‘Take your staff and stretch out your hand over the waters of Egypt—over their rivers and canals and ponds and all the reservoirs—that they may become blood.’ There will be blood throughout the land of Egypt, even in the vessels of wood and stone.” 20 Moses and Aaron did just as the LORD had commanded; in the presence of Pharaoh and his officials, Aaron raised the staff and struck the water of the Nile, and all the water was turned to blood. 21 The fish in the Nile died, and the river smelled so bad that the Egyptians could not drink its water. And there was blood throughout the land of Egypt.

22 But the magicians of Egypt did the same things by their magic arts. So Pharaoh’s heart was hardened, and he would not listen to Moses and Aaron, just as the LORD had said. 23 Instead, Pharaoh turned around, went into his palace, and did not take any of this to heart. 24 So all the Egyptians dug around the Nile for water to drink, because they could not drink the water from the river. 25 And seven full days passed after the LORD had struck the Nile.

What is the context?

After Pharaoh refuses to listen, God sends Moses to meet him beside the Nile in the morning. The demand remains the same: release God’s people to worship Him. This time the warning names a judgment that will make His identity known—water becoming blood.

Aaron strikes the Nile before Pharaoh and his officials. The fish die, the river stinks, and its water becomes undrinkable. The judgment reaches Egypt’s rivers, canals, ponds and reservoirs, even vessels of wood and stone. The Egyptians dig around the river seeking drinking water.

The river also had religious meaning. The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes Hapy as the Egyptian god who personified its annual flooding and was associated with fertility. That background helps explain the force of the judgment: a source associated with life and abundance has become a source of death and distress.

The magicians imitate the sign, and Pharaoh turns back to his palace without taking it to heart. The account closes with seven full days passing after the LORD struck the Nile. The imitation has given him a reason to dismiss the warning, while his people continue facing its consequences.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The shared reading sees the judgment as a display of the LORD’s authority over the water on which Egypt depends. Wesley emphasizes the reversal in a river the Egyptians honored and the failure of the magicians to restore it.

There are different explanations of how the magicians could repeat the sign after the waters had become blood. Gill proposes a small quantity of water, perhaps newly dug or not yet affected, used to produce an appearance of blood. The Catholic USCCB notes instead treat the comparison as an exaggerated description echoing the earlier contest with the staffs. These are different explanations of the scope and wording of their imitation; the narrative reports it without giving its source of water.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Scripture later gives the plagues an explicitly religious purpose. Exodus 12:12 announces judgment against Egypt’s gods, and Numbers 33:3–4 recalls that judgment as Israel leaves. The attack on Egypt’s security belongs within God making Himself known as the One who can deliver His people.

A later parallel appears in Revelation 16:4–7, where waters turn to blood and the judgment answers the shedding of the blood of God’s servants. The repeated image connects the Exodus with a wider theme of God judging those who oppress and kill His people.
