Passage

Matthew 15:1-9

When Tradition Displaces God's Command

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then some Pharisees and scribes came to Jesus from Jerusalem and asked, 2 “Why do Your disciples break the tradition of the elders? They do not wash their hands before they eat.” 3 Jesus replied, “And why do you break the command of God for the sake of your tradition?

4 For God said, ‘Honor your father and mother’ and ‘Anyone who curses his father or mother must be put to death.’ 5 But you say that if anyone says to his father or mother, ‘Whatever you would have received from me is a gift devoted to God,’ 6 he need not honor his father or mother with it. Thus you nullify the word of God for the sake of your tradition.

7 You hypocrites! Isaiah prophesied correctly about you: 8 ‘These people honor Me with their lips, but their hearts are far from Me. 9 They worship Me in vain; they teach as doctrine the precepts of men.’”

What is the context?

Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem question Jesus about His disciples eating without washing their hands. Their complaint concerns the tradition of the elders. Mark 7:1–5 explains that the washing belongs to their practices of ceremonial cleanliness. The dispute is about religious obligation, rather than a lesson about hygiene.

Jesus answers with a question about their own teaching. God commands people to honor their parents, yet they allow a declaration that resources are devoted to God to keep those resources from helping a father or mother. A religious rule is being used to set aside a duty God has given.

Mark calls the declaration Corban. The Greek spelling is κορβᾶν, korban (kor-BAHN), from the Hebrew word for an offering. Thayer's entry explains it as a gift offered, or intended to be offered, to God. In Mark 7:9–13, the problem is that the declaration prevents the help the parents should receive.

Jesus then applies Isaiah's warning about honoring God with words while the heart is far away. The claimed devotion does not excuse the neglected command. His reply asks whether religious teaching leads people to obey God or gives them a way around what He says.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as a warning against human teaching that defeats God's commands. Traditions differ over how to distinguish a human custom from teaching received with divine authority.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 1.6 and 1.10, makes Scripture the final test and rejects additions to God's revealed teaching from human traditions. The 1689 Baptist Confession, 1.6 and 1.10, teaches the same principle. The Methodist Articles of Religion, V, likewise say that a belief cannot be required as necessary for salvation unless it is taught in Scripture or can be proved from it.

The Catholic Church's Catechism, 80–83, distinguishes apostolic Tradition, received from Christ and the apostles, from later customs that can change. It receives Scripture and apostolic Tradition together. Paragraph 86 says the Church's teaching office serves God's word. Catholic teaching therefore does not understand Jesus' rebuke as a rejection of apostolic Tradition. The difference concerns where authoritative teaching is received and how it is recognized.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus explicitly connects this dispute with Isaiah 29:13–14. The warning about lips and hearts shows that the problem reaches deeper than one disputed washing practice. God's people are called to honor Him in truth, including how they treat people entrusted to their care.

That connection continues in James 1:22–27. Hearing God's word must become doing it; an unrestrained tongue can make a person's religious claims empty, while care for vulnerable people belongs to faithful devotion. God's purpose for His word includes a changed life, not simply a religious explanation for avoiding responsibility.
