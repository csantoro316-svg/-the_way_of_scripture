Passage

Isaiah 29:17-24

Can people who refused to listen learn to hear again?

Berean Standard Bible

17 In a very short time, will not Lebanon become an orchard, and the orchard seem like a forest? 18 On that day the deaf will hear the words of the scroll, and out of the deep darkness the eyes of the blind will see. 19 The humble will increase their joy in the LORD, and the poor among men will rejoice in the Holy One of Israel.

20 For the ruthless will vanish, the mockers will disappear, and all who look for evil will be cut down— 21 those who indict a man with a word, who ensnare the mediator at the gate, and who with false charges deprive the innocent of justice.

22 Therefore the LORD who redeemed Abraham says of the house of Jacob: “No longer will Jacob be ashamed and no more will his face grow pale. 23 For when he sees his children around him, the work of My hands, they will honor My name, they will sanctify the Holy One of Jacob, and they will stand in awe of the God of Israel. 24 Then the wayward in spirit will come to understanding, and those who grumble will accept instruction.”

What is the context?

The promise answers the blindness, deafness and confusion of the preceding passage. Lebanon and fruitful land exchange their familiar appearance; the deaf hear the scroll, and the blind see out of darkness. The humble and poor find increasing joy in the Lord.

This restoration reaches public life as well as personal understanding. Ruthless people and mockers disappear. Those who twist words into charges, trap someone seeking justice and deprive the innocent of a fair hearing are cut down.

The Lord speaks to the house of Jacob as the One who redeemed Abraham. Jacob’s shame gives way to seeing children who are the work of God’s hands, honoring His name and standing in awe of Him. The final promise is especially hopeful: the wayward gain understanding, and those who grumbled become willing to learn.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The USCCB notes connect the changed landscape, opened senses and removal of injustice with a reversal of the people’s condition. The promise concerns a community made able to receive God’s instruction and live without its former oppression.

Henry, Wesley and Gill also read the passage through the spread of the gospel. Wesley and Gill connect the fruitful landscape with Gentiles receiving God’s word; Gill also connects the final promise with a future turning of Jewish people to Christ. These are readings of how the promised restoration unfolds, rather than different views of whether God can give understanding.

Across these explanations, the changed hearing and sight belong with changed conduct. Learning God’s word is pictured alongside humility, joy, honest judgment and reverence.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

When John the Baptist’s disciples ask whether Jesus is the One to come, Jesus points to the blind receiving sight, the deaf hearing and the poor receiving good news in Matthew 11:2–6. These works provide a strong parallel with Isaiah’s promised reversal. Matthew does not identify this answer as a quotation of Isaiah 29 alone.

The connection helps explain what restoration looks like in the ministry of Jesus: people receive sight and hearing, and the poor are welcomed with good news. Isaiah’s wider picture also keeps justice and willingness to learn in view. God’s purpose reaches people’s understanding, their treatment of one another and their joy in Him.
