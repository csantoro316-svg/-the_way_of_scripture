Passage

Ezekiel 30:1-9

Why does Egypt’s day of judgment shake its allies too?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Again the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, prophesy and declare that this is what the Lord GOD says: Wail, ‘Alas for that day!’ 3 For the day is near, the Day of the LORD is near. It will be a day of clouds, a time of doom for the nations. 4 A sword will come against Egypt, and there will be anguish in Cush when the slain fall in Egypt, its wealth is taken away, and its foundations are torn down. 5 Cush, Put, and Lud, and all the various peoples, as well as Libya and the men of the covenant land, will fall with Egypt by the sword.

6 For this is what the LORD says: The allies of Egypt will fall, and her proud strength will collapse. From Migdol to Syene they will fall by the sword within her, declares the Lord GOD. 7 They will be desolate among desolate lands, and their cities will lie among ruined cities. 8 Then they will know that I am the LORD when I set fire to Egypt and all her helpers are shattered. 9 On that day messengers will go out from Me in ships to frighten Cush out of complacency. Anguish will come upon them on the day of Egypt’s doom. For it is indeed coming.

What is the context?

The next message opens with a call to wail because the Day of the Lord is near. It is a day of clouds and doom for the nations, beginning with a sword against Egypt and anguish in Cush.

The threat reaches the peoples and allies bound up with Egypt. When its proud strength collapses, the support built around it also fails. Its ruined cities become part of the wider desolation.

Messengers go out in ships to frighten Cush out of complacency. The Lord’s action in one place carries a warning beyond its borders. The repeated outcome is that the people will know Him when Egypt and its helpers are shattered.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the announced destruction through Babylon and its army, while distinguishing such judgments from the greater wrath from which Jesus delivers His people. Wesley emphasizes Egypt’s allies sharing in the calamity.

Cambridge gives the Day of the Lord a wider reach as the Lord’s decisive judgment and vindication of His cause. Gaebelein, in his discussion of these chapters, also sees earlier national judgments as anticipating the final judgment. These readings connect the immediate setting and the larger horizon differently; the phrase alone does not supply a detailed seven-year schedule.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

First Thessalonians 5:1–11 carries the Day of the Lord theme into the church’s expectation. Paul contrasts claims of peace and security with sudden destruction, then calls believers to stay awake, sober, faithful and loving.

His purpose is not to give believers a way to date Egypt’s overthrow. He directs them toward the salvation obtained through Jesus Christ, who died so that they might live with Him, and tells them to encourage one another. The larger judgment theme produces watchfulness and hope grounded in Christ.
