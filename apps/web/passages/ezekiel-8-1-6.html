Passage

Ezekiel 8:1-6

Why is an idol standing at the Lord’s temple?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In the sixth year, on the fifth day of the sixth month, I was sitting in my house, and the elders of Judah were sitting before me; and there the hand of the Lord GOD fell upon me. 2 Then I looked and saw a figure like that of a man. From His waist down His appearance was like fire, and from His waist up He was as bright as the gleam of amber. 3 He stretched out what looked like a hand and took me by the hair of my head. Then the Spirit lifted me up between earth and heaven and carried me in visions of God to Jerusalem, to the entrance of the north gate of the inner court, where the idol that provokes jealousy was seated. 4 And there I saw the glory of the God of Israel, like the vision I had seen in the plain.

5 “Son of man,” He said to me, “now lift up your eyes to the north.” So I lifted up my eyes to the north, and in the entrance north of the Altar Gate I saw this idol of jealousy. 6 “Son of man,” He said to me, “do you see what they are doing—the great abominations that the house of Israel is committing—to drive Me far from My sanctuary? Yet you will see even greater abominations.”

What is the context?

Ezekiel is sitting in his house with the elders of Judah when the Lord’s hand comes upon him. The date places this after his first vision. He remains among the exiles, but the Spirit takes him to Jerusalem in a vision.

The fiery brightness recalls the glory he has already seen. At the entrance to the inner court, however, he sees an idol that provokes the Lord’s jealousy. God’s glory and the worship of a rival are being brought together in the place devoted to Him.

The Lord asks Ezekiel to look closely. The question is whether the people’s own conduct is driving Him far from His sanctuary. The temple’s presence cannot make their worship acceptable while they give its space to other gods.

The vision has only begun. Ezekiel is told that still greater offenses will be shown to him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The central reading is that worship of other gods has invaded the Lord’s own sanctuary. Henry and Wesley understand Ezekiel’s journey as a vision, and both connect the temple’s corruption with God’s coming judgment.

Wesley identifies the idol as Baal, but the passage itself gives no name. Its importance here lies in what it does: it places a rival object of worship at the entrance to the Lord’s house. The Lord’s jealousy concerns the people’s broken faithfulness to Him.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Second Corinthians 6:14–18 asks what agreement God’s temple can have with idols. Paul applies the temple picture to the people who belong to the living God, drawing on God’s promises to dwell among His people and receive them as His children.

This develops the same larger concern seen in Ezekiel: God intends to dwell with His people, and their worship belongs to Him. Paul’s application goes beyond the fate of Jerusalem’s building to the life of the believing community. He is not explicitly quoting Ezekiel 8.
