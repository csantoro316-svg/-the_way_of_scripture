Passage

Acts 17:23

Does Paul approve of worship directed toward an unknown god?

Berean Standard Bible

23 For as I walked around and examined your objects of worship, I even found an altar with this inscription: TO AN UNKNOWN GOD. Therefore what you worship as something unknown, I now proclaim to you.

What is the context?

Paul has been invited to explain the message he was preaching in Athens. He points to an altar whose inscription acknowledges an unknown god. That gives him an opening to tell his hearers about the Creator; it does not mean he accepts the surrounding idol worship as an adequate knowledge of Him.

The rest of the speech explains who this God is: Creator and giver of life, unlike an image made by human skill. Paul concludes by calling for repentance in view of the coming judgment and the resurrection. The opening should be read with that conclusion, not detached as approval of every form of devotion.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the altar as Paul’s starting point for explaining the God his hearers do not know. The invitation to listen leads toward a change of belief and life, rather than an assurance that religious sincerity by itself is enough.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The speech joins creation, the nations and the risen Jesus in one account of God’s purposes. Genesis 1:1, 26–27 presents God as Creator and humanity as His creation. Paul takes his hearers back to that shared dependence before speaking about what God now commands them to do. The many nations are not outside the Creator’s concern.

The appointed Judge is the same risen Jesus whom Paul has been proclaiming. Acts 10:42–43 explicitly connects Jesus’ appointment as Judge with forgiveness through faith in Him. Athens therefore belongs to the wider witness to all peoples: the One on whom everyone depends is calling people everywhere to respond, and the resurrection makes that call urgent.

What is our position?

Worship as the Early Church Knew It reads Acts 17:23 as sincere devotion needing to be redirected toward the truth. Paul recognizes the Athenians’ religious activity while explaining the God they do not know. Reverence matters, but it must be directed toward the true God.
