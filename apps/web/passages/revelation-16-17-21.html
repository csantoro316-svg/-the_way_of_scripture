Passage

Revelation 16:17-21

What happens when the seventh bowl is poured into the air?

Berean Standard Bible

17 Then the seventh angel poured out his bowl into the air, and a loud voice came from the throne in the temple, saying, “It is done!” 18 And there were flashes of lightning, rumblings, peals of thunder, and a great earthquake the likes of which had not occurred since men were upon the earth—so mighty was the great quake. 19 The great city was split into three parts, and the cities of the nations collapsed. And God remembered Babylon the great and gave her the cup of the wine of the fury of His wrath.

20 Then every island fled, and no mountain could be found. 21 And great hailstones weighing almost a hundred pounds each rained down on them from above. And men cursed God for the plague of hail, because it was so horrendous.

What is the context?

The seventh angel pours the bowl into the air. A voice from the throne in the temple announces completion. Lightning, rumblings, thunder and an earthquake follow. John describes the earthquake as greater than any since human beings were on the earth.

The great city splits into three parts, and the cities of the nations collapse. Babylon is remembered before God and receives the cup of His fierce wrath. Remembered here leads directly to action: the judgment already announced against Babylon is being carried forward.

Islands flee and mountains cannot be found. Enormous hailstones fall, described in the BSB as weighing almost a hundred pounds each. The scene depicts upheaval reaching beyond a single city. Yet the human response at the end of the chapter is still to curse God.

The bowls have reached their climax, but the story has more to disclose. The next chapters explain Babylon’s character and downfall in greater detail. The chapter ends with judgment underway and resistance still exposed.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The great city is a point of disagreement. Wesley identifies the divided city as Jerusalem, distinguished from Babylon. The USCCB notes understand it as Rome and its empire. Gill understands the wider domain of Roman religious power rather than the city alone. The phrase should therefore not be treated as an agreed code for one location in every passage.

Walvoord discusses both Jerusalem and Babylon in explaining the divided city, while expecting a future physical judgment. He does not claim that the wording removes every uncertainty about the geography. All these readings recognize Babylon as a particular object of the judgment.

The earthquake, disappearing mountains and great hail are read with different degrees of physical and symbolic detail. The central point is the overthrow of powers and places that seemed secure. The passage does not supply the geological mechanism or a complete map of the changed world.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The hail recalls Exodus 9:22–26, another judgment confronting a ruler who resists God. The repeated language of thunder, lightning and earthquake also connects this bowl with the earlier heavenly scenes in Revelation 8:5 and 11:19. The final bowl belongs to the same unfolding account of divine authority.

Hebrews 12:26–29 gives a related biblical theme: what can be shaken is removed, while God’s kingdom cannot be shaken. That is a parallel, not an exact identification of the earthquake’s physical effects. It directs attention toward what endures when earthly power and security fail, and toward worship marked by reverence for God.
