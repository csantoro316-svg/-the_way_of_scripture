Passage

Genesis 15:8-21

God Confirms His Promise

Berean Standard Bible

8 But Abram replied, “Lord GOD, how can I know that I will possess it?”

9 And the LORD said to him, “Bring Me a heifer, a goat, and a ram, each three years old, along with a turtledove and a young pigeon.”

10 So Abram brought all these to Him, split each of them down the middle, and laid the halves opposite each other. The birds, however, he did not cut in half. 11 And the birds of prey descended on the carcasses, but Abram drove them away. 12 As the sun was setting, Abram fell into a deep sleep, and suddenly great terror and darkness overwhelmed him.

13 Then the LORD said to Abram, “Know for certain that your descendants will be strangers in a land that is not their own, and they will be enslaved and mistreated four hundred years. 14 But I will judge the nation they serve as slaves, and afterward they will depart with many possessions. 15 You, however, will go to your fathers in peace and be buried at a ripe old age. 16 In the fourth generation your descendants will return here, for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete.”

17 When the sun had set and darkness had fallen, behold, a smoking firepot and a flaming torch appeared and passed between the halves of the carcasses. 18 On that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying, “To your descendants I have given this land—from the river of Egypt to the great River Euphrates— 19 the land of the Kenites, Kenizzites, Kadmonites, 20 Hittites, Perizzites, Rephaites, 21 Amorites, Canaanites, Girgashites, and Jebusites.”

What is the context?

Abram asks how he can know that he will possess the land. God responds by directing him to bring animals. Abram divides the larger animals and lays the halves opposite each other, leaving the birds whole. As he waits, he drives away birds of prey; at sunset he falls into a deep sleep with terror and darkness.

God tells Abram that the fulfillment will include a long and painful wait. His descendants will live as strangers, suffer enslavement and mistreatment, and later leave with possessions after God judges the nation they serve. Abram himself will die in peace. Their return is also connected with the Amorites' wrongdoing, which has not yet reached its full measure. The promise concerns God's dealings with other peoples as well as this family.

A smoking firepot and a flaming torch pass between the pieces, and the LORD makes the covenant, naming the land and its peoples. Abram is not described as walking between them. Jeremiah 34:18–20 describes another covenant in which people pass between a divided calf and face judgment for breaking their commitment. That later passage helps explain the seriousness of the scene here: God is giving Abram a solemn assurance, not a casual prediction.

How is this passage commonly understood?

A common Christian reading sees God confirming the land promise through a solemn covenant and preparing Abram for the delay before his descendants return. Wesley's notes on Genesis 15:8–18 connect the divided animals with Jeremiah's covenant scene and read the promise of departure with possessions in relation to Israel's later deliverance from Egypt.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 7:6–7 explicitly recalls this announcement of foreign residence, oppression, and God's judgment on the enslaving nation. The promised future passes through suffering and deliverance, not an uninterrupted rise in comfort. Exodus 12:29–36 later records judgment on Egypt and the people's departure with possessions, bringing the earlier assurance into the story of Israel's rescue.

The larger theme is God's trustworthy promise through a long wait. Hebrews 6:13–20 appeals to God's later oath to Abraham and describes hope grounded in His unchanging purpose as an anchor for believers. The covenant scene here belongs to that unfolding assurance: God has not forgotten His people when the road to fulfillment includes years they would never have chosen.
