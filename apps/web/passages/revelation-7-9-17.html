Passage

Revelation 7:9-17

What future does the Lamb give people who have suffered?

Berean Standard Bible

9 After this I looked and saw a multitude too large to count, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue, standing before the throne and before the Lamb. They were wearing white robes and holding palm branches in their hands. 10 And they cried out in a loud voice: “Salvation to our God, who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb!” 11 And all the angels stood around the throne and around the elders and the four living creatures. And they fell facedown before the throne and worshiped God, 12 saying, “Amen! Blessing and glory and wisdom and thanks and honor and power and strength be to our God forever and ever! Amen.”

13 Then one of the elders addressed me: “These in white robes,” he asked, “who are they, and where have they come from?” 14 “Sir,” I answered, “you know.” So he replied, “These are the ones who have come out of the great tribulation; they have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.

15 For this reason, they are before the throne of God and serve Him day and night in His temple; and the One seated on the throne will spread His tabernacle over them. 16 ‘Never again will they hunger, and never will they thirst; nor will the sun beat down upon them, nor any scorching heat.’ 17 For the Lamb in the center of the throne will be their shepherd. ‘He will lead them to springs of living water,’ and ‘God will wipe away every tear from their eyes.’”

What is the context?

John now sees a multitude too large to count, drawn from every nation, tribe, people and language. They stand before God’s throne and the Lamb, wearing white robes and holding palm branches. They praise God and the Lamb for salvation, and the surrounding angels join in worship.

One of the elders asks John who these people are and where they came from. John leaves the answer to him. The elder identifies them as those who have come out of the great tribulation and washed their robes in the Lamb’s blood. Their cleansing and their place before God are bound to Christ’s death.

The final description moves from their suffering to God’s care. They serve in His presence, sheltered by Him. Hunger, thirst and scorching heat no longer afflict them. The Lamb becomes their shepherd, guiding them to living water, and God wipes away their tears.

This scene should be heard beside the question at the end of chapter 6 about who can stand. Here a multitude is standing before the throne. The vision of judgment is accompanied by a vision of salvation and lasting care for people from across the nations.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The scope of the great tribulation is read differently. Walvoord identifies the multitude as people martyred during a future tribulation. Wesley explicitly includes the righteous across history who have passed through many kinds of affliction, rather than limiting the group to martyrs. The USCCB notes connect the distress with Roman persecution. These readings locate the suffering in different settings.

Gill also includes God’s people across ages, but places the scene especially in Christ’s future reign with His people on earth. Wesley emphasizes the righteous in God’s heavenly presence. Their difference concerns the setting of the promise, alongside their shared confidence that the suffering will end.

Wesley and Gill both connect the white robes with cleansing through the Lamb’s blood. The praise attributes salvation to God and the Lamb. Both direct attention to Christ’s death as the basis for their place with God and to the care He gives His people.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise of no hunger, thirst or scorching heat takes up Isaiah 49:8–13, where God has compassion on His afflicted people and guides them beside springs. The removal of tears recalls Isaiah 25:6–9, where God swallows up death and comforts His people. Revelation places those promises within the Lamb’s care for this multitude.

The Lamb who gave His life is also the shepherd who leads them. That connects with Jesus’ own description of the good shepherd laying down His life for His sheep in John 10:11–18. Revelation 21:1–4 will return to God dwelling with His people and wiping away their tears. The larger hope is more than surviving a crisis: it is life with Him, where the suffering that made those tears necessary has ended.
