Passage

Genesis 3:1-7

The Serpent’s Deception

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now the serpent was more crafty than any beast of the field that the LORD God had made. And he said to the woman, “Did God really say, ‘You must not eat from any tree in the garden?’”

2 The woman answered the serpent, “We may eat the fruit of the trees of the garden, 3 but about the fruit of the tree in the middle of the garden, God has said, ‘You must not eat of it or touch it, or you will die.’”

4 “You will not surely die,” the serpent told the woman. 5 “For God knows that in the day you eat of it, your eyes will be opened and you will be like God, knowing good and evil.”

6 When the woman saw that the tree was good for food and pleasing to the eyes, and that it was desirable for obtaining wisdom, she took the fruit and ate it. She also gave some to her husband who was with her, and he ate it.

7 And the eyes of both of them were opened, and they knew that they were naked; so they sewed together fig leaves and made coverings for themselves.

What is the context?

The last scene showed the man and woman naked without shame. Now a conversation changes how they hear God's command. The serpent presents God's generous provision as a restriction, then directly denies the warning about death. The temptation is to distrust God's goodness and expect a better life by disobeying Him.

The woman repeats the restriction but adds touching the fruit, which is absent from the command recorded in Genesis 2:16–17. Genesis does not explain where that addition originated. The serpent's reply goes further: what God said would happen, he says will not happen. The woman eats, gives the fruit to her husband, and he eats. The immediate result is shame and an attempt to cover themselves, not the freedom the serpent offered.

The serpent is described as עָרוּם, ʿārûm (ah-ROOM), meaning crafty or shrewd. Brown–Driver–Briggs shows that the word can describe sensible caution elsewhere, but here that cleverness serves deception. Intelligent-sounding words can still lead away from what God has said. Verse 7 must also be read with what follows: covering themselves does not stop them from hiding when God approaches.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians read this as humanity's fall into sin, not simply the discovery of a new kind of knowledge. Romans 5:12–19 connects Adam's disobedience with the sin and death that affect humanity. Christians differ over how to explain our connection with that first sin.

Where do traditions differ? The Westminster Confession, 6.2–4, and 1689 Baptist Confession, 6.2–4, teach that Adam's descendants inherit both the guilt of his sin and a corrupted nature. In these readings, the fall leaves people unable to turn to God without His grace.

The Catholic Catechism, 404–405, describes an inherited loss of humanity's original holiness, while distinguishing that condition from a personal sinful act by each descendant. The Methodist Articles, VII–VIII, emphasize an inherited corruption and our need for God's grace before we can turn toward Him.

The Orthodox Church in America's explanation of original sin emphasizes inherited consequences, especially death, while reserving guilt for Adam and Eve's own act to them.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Paul does more than look back at the damage. In Romans 5:15–19, he sets Adam's disobedience beside Christ's obedience. One man's act brings condemnation; Christ's obedience brings righteousness and life. The answer to the fall is not a return to the tree to make a better choice, but what God gives through Jesus Christ.

2 Corinthians 11:2–4 also turns Eve's deception into a warning for the church. People who belong to Christ can be led away from devotion to Him by a different message about Jesus. That makes this passage part of both the story of our need for rescue and the continuing call to hold to the truth.

What is our position?

Returning to the Word and Is the Bible the Inerrant Word of God? use this passage to explain our concern about changing God's words. We should notice both the added restriction in the woman's reply and the serpent's direct contradiction of God's warning. An addition and a denial are different actions, but each changes what was recorded in God's command.
