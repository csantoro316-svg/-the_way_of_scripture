Passage

Ephesians 5:3-7

Why does Paul warn against treating sin lightly?

Berean Standard Bible

3 But among you, as is proper among the saints, there must not be even a hint of sexual immorality, or of any kind of impurity, or of greed. 4 Nor should there be obscenity, foolish talk, or crude joking, which are out of character, but rather thanksgiving.

5 For of this you can be sure: No immoral, impure, or greedy person (that is, an idolater) has any inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God. 6 Let no one deceive you with empty words, for because of such things the wrath of God is coming on the sons of disobedience. 7 Therefore do not be partakers with them.

What is the context?

Paul has just called believers to walk in the love shown by Christ. He now names conduct that conflicts with that calling: sexual immorality, impurity, greed and degrading speech. Thanksgiving is the positive alternative he names for their speech.

The warning about inheritance gives these instructions weight. Paul connects greed with idolatry and says that the immoral, impure or greedy person has no inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and God. He tells the readers not to be deceived by empty words or become partners in these things.

The contrast continues in verse 8: they were once darkness, but now are light in the Lord. Paul is calling people who belong to Him to take that change seriously. The text gives no permission to excuse harmful conduct as unimportant to God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand these verses as a serious warning that sexual sin, greed and degrading speech conflict with life in God’s kingdom. They differ in how they relate the warning to perseverance and the possibility of falling away.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 17, teaches that those truly called and sanctified will finally be saved, while recognizing that they can fall into serious sins that grieve the Spirit and hurt others. The warning is therefore taken seriously within an account of God preserving His people.

The Wesleyan Methodist Articles of Religion, article XII, teach that those who have received the Holy Spirit can depart from grace and fall into sin, and can be restored through God’s grace. The Catholic Catechism, paragraphs 1272–1274, teaches that baptism’s spiritual mark endures while sin can prevent it from bearing saving fruit. These documents give differing accounts of security and faithfulness; none treats this conduct as harmless.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Calling greed idolatry connects this warning with the Bible’s larger concern about what people worship. Romans 1:21–25 describes the disorder that follows when people exchange the truth about God for devotion to created things.

Colossians 3:5 also explicitly identifies greed as idolatry. This parallel helps explain why the warning reaches beyond outward respectability: the issue includes what claims our trust and devotion in place of God.
