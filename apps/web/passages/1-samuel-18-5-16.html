Passage

1 Samuel 18:5-16

Why does David’s success make Saul afraid?

Berean Standard Bible

5 So David marched out and prospered in everything Saul sent him to do, and Saul set him over the men of war. And this was pleasing in the sight of all the people, and of Saul’s officers as well. 6 As the troops were returning home after David had killed the Philistine, the women came out of all the cities of Israel to meet King Saul with singing and dancing, with joyful songs, and with tambourines and other instruments. 7 And as the women danced, they sang out: “Saul has slain his thousands, and David his tens of thousands.” 8 And Saul was furious and resented this song. “They have ascribed tens of thousands to David,” he said, “but only thousands to me. What more can he have but the kingdom?” 9 And from that day forward Saul kept a jealous eye on David.

10 The next day a spirit of distress sent from God came upon Saul, and he prophesied inside the house while David played the harp as usual. Now Saul was holding a spear, 11 and he hurled it, thinking, “I will pin David to the wall.” But David eluded him twice. 12 So Saul was afraid of David, because the LORD was with David but had departed from Saul. 13 Therefore Saul sent David away and gave him command of a thousand men. David led the troops out to battle and back, 14 and he continued to prosper in all his ways, because the LORD was with him. 15 When Saul saw that David was very successful, he was afraid of him. 16 But all Israel and Judah loved David, because he was leading them out to battle and back.

What is the context?

David succeeds in the work Saul gives him, and his appointment pleases both the people and Saul’s officers. Then the women celebrating the victory praise David more highly than Saul. Saul hears a threat to his kingdom in their song and begins watching David with jealousy.

The next scene brings that hostility into the house. While David plays the harp, Saul throws his spear at him. David escapes twice. Saul then sends him out to command a thousand men, but distance does not remove Saul’s fear. David continues to succeed, and the people love him as their military leader.

The Hebrew word behind David’s success, sakal (sah-KAHL), can describe acting wisely as well as having success. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes both senses and lists these verses. The picture is of capable, wise service that goes well, with the account repeatedly pointing to the LORD’s presence.

Saul’s response moves in the opposite direction. Instead of welcoming help against Israel’s enemies, he becomes afraid of the man God is helping. David’s growing place among the people and Saul’s loss of favor become increasingly visible.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes how quickly celebration becomes danger for David: the praise of others stirs Saul’s envy. Wesley likewise reads Saul’s fear in light of God’s presence with David. The passage presents a king who increasingly treats a faithful servant as a rival.

The BSB says Saul prophesied while the spirit of distress was upon him. Gill discusses several readings, including genuine prophetic speech, imitation of prophetic behavior and disturbed speech or actions; he favors the last. Wesley also reads it as disordered behavior. These are explanations of an unusual scene, rather than a reason to replace the BSB wording or assign Saul a modern medical diagnosis.

Henry contrasts David’s service with Saul’s violence and credits God’s care for David’s safety. David’s restraint matters, and so does his escape. The story does not make remaining within reach of a violent person a condition of faithfulness.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Henry connects David’s endurance under hostility with the call to consider Christ in Hebrews 12:1–3. David serves while being treated as a threat; Jesus endures the hostility of sinners and the shame of the cross, then sits at God’s right hand.

The parallel points beyond a young commander surviving jealousy. God’s saving purpose reaches its fullness through Jesus, who endures suffering and gives His people reason to keep going. Hebrews calls readers to fix their eyes on Him when opposition makes them weary.
