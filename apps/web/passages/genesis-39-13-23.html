Passage

Genesis 39:13-23

Joseph Falsely Imprisoned

Berean Standard Bible

13 When she saw that he had left his cloak in her hand and had run out of the house, 14 she called her household servants. “Look,” she said, “this Hebrew has been brought to us to make sport of us. He came to me so he could sleep with me, but I screamed as loud as I could. 15 When he heard me scream for help, he left his cloak beside me and ran out of the house.”

16 So Potiphar’s wife kept Joseph’s cloak beside her until his master came home. 17 Then she told him the same story: “The Hebrew slave you brought us came to me to make sport of me, 18 but when I screamed for help, he left his cloak beside me and ran out of the house.”

19 When his master heard the story his wife told him, saying, “This is what your slave did to me,” he burned with anger. 20 So Joseph’s master took him and had him thrown into the prison where the king’s prisoners were confined. While Joseph was there in the prison, 21 the LORD was with him and extended kindness to him, granting him favor in the eyes of the prison warden. 22 And the warden put all the prisoners under Joseph’s care, so that he was responsible for all that was done in the prison. 23 The warden did not concern himself with anything under Joseph’s care, because the LORD was with Joseph and gave him success in whatever he did.

What is the context?

Potiphar's wife uses the cloak Joseph left behind to support a false account of what happened. She tells the servants, then her husband, that Joseph approached her sexually and fled when she screamed. The reader has already seen the reverse: she pursued him, and he fled her grasp.

Her accusation repeatedly emphasizes Joseph's identity as a Hebrew and a slave, and she blames her husband for bringing him into the household. Potiphar becomes angry and sends Joseph to the place where the king's prisoners are confined. The account does not tell us all his private conclusions about either Joseph or his wife.

As in the earlier deception of Jacob, a garment becomes supposed evidence for a false story. Joseph loses his place in the household after doing what is right. Yet the account immediately repeats that the LORD is with him, showing kindness and giving him favor with the prison warden.

The Hebrew word for favor is חֵן, ḥēn (khayn). Brown–Driver–Briggs gives the sense of favor or acceptance and specifically cites Genesis 39:21. Here God's care is expressed through the warden's trust: he puts the prisoners and their affairs under Joseph's supervision. God's presence continues inside the prison, before release or vindication comes.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see unjust suffering alongside God's continuing care. Joseph's imprisonment does not show that God has abandoned him or that his refusal was a mistake. Wesley's notes on Genesis 39:20–21 emphasize that confinement cannot shut out God's presence and connect Joseph's experience with the suffering of Christ.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 7:9–10 directly gathers Joseph's troubles into the larger account of God's presence, eventual rescue, and gift of authority in Egypt. Psalm 105:17–22 likewise places his confinement before his release and appointment to serve. Prison is a real injustice, but it does not put him beyond God's reach or end his role in preserving life.

His suffering after refusing wrong also offers a parallel to the New Testament's account of Christ's faithful endurance. 1 Peter 2:19–24 speaks of suffering for doing good and points to Jesus, who entrusted Himself to the One who judges justly. Christ's bearing of sins is His own saving work; the shared lesson is that God's care and a person's faithful obedience can be present even when the immediate outcome is unjust suffering.
