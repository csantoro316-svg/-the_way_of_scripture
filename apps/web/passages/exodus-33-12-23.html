Passage

Exodus 33:12-23

How does God answer Moses’ plea to know Him?

Berean Standard Bible

12 Then Moses said to the LORD, “Look, You have been telling me, ‘Lead this people up,’ but You have not let me know whom You will send with me. Yet You have said, ‘I know you by name, and you have found favor in My sight.’ 13 Now if indeed I have found favor in Your sight, please let me know Your ways, that I may know You and find favor in Your sight. Remember that this nation is Your people.” 14 And the LORD answered, “My Presence will go with you, and I will give you rest.” 15 “If Your Presence does not go with us,” Moses replied, “do not lead us up from here. 16 For how then can it be known that Your people and I have found favor in Your sight, unless You go with us? How else will we be distinguished from all the other people on the face of the earth?” 17 So the LORD said to Moses, “I will do this very thing you have asked, for you have found favor in My sight, and I know you by name.”

18 Then Moses said, “Please show me Your glory.” 19 “I will cause all My goodness to pass before you,” the LORD replied, “and I will proclaim My name—the LORD—in your presence. I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.” 20 But He added, “You cannot see My face, for no one can see Me and live.”

21 The LORD continued, “There is a place near Me where you are to stand upon a rock, 22 and when My glory passes by, I will put you in a cleft of the rock and cover you with My hand until I have passed by. 23 Then I will take My hand away, and you will see My back; but My face must not be seen.”

What is the context?

Moses does not settle for reaching the land without God. He asks to know God’s ways and insists that the people need His presence with them. What distinguishes Israel is not merely where they are going, but the God who goes with them. God agrees to Moses’ request and promises His presence and rest.

The word translated favor is חֵן, chen (KHAYN), meaning favor or grace. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes these verses among examples of finding favor with God. Moses appeals to the relationship God has given him; he does not claim that Israel’s conduct has earned this assurance.

Then Moses asks to see God’s glory. God answers by promising to make His goodness pass before him and to proclaim His name. He also declares His freedom to show mercy and compassion. Knowing God includes learning what He is like, not simply seeing something impressive.

The answer comes with protection and a limit. God places Moses in a cleft of the rock and shields him as His glory passes. Moses may see what God permits, described as His back, but not His face. The passage presents a real encounter while making clear that Moses cannot bear an unlimited sight of God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Calvin both understand the limited sight as an act of kindness toward a human being unable to bear God’s full glory. They also read the promise of mercy as freely given, not something creatures can demand as a debt. Their explanations keep God’s goodness and His freedom together.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Paul directly quotes God’s declaration to Moses in Romans 9:14–18, explaining that mercy rests on God rather than human desire or effort. The words spoken after Israel’s sin become part of his explanation of God’s mercy in the larger story of salvation.

Moses’ desire to know God also leads us toward what the New Testament says about Jesus. John 1:14–18 describes the Word dwelling among us, revealing glory, grace, and truth, and making the unseen God known. Paul says that God gives the knowledge of His glory in the face of Jesus Christ (2 Corinthians 4:3–6). God’s self-revelation reaches beyond this protected glimpse on the mountain.

There is a further parallel in the prayers. Moses pleads for his people to have God’s presence; Jesus prays for His people to be with Him and see His glory (John 17:18–24). The prayers are not identical, but both concern a people brought near to God through the one who pleads for them. Revelation’s closing hope is that God’s servants will see His face (Revelation 22:1–5).
