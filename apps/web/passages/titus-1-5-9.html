Passage

Titus 1:5-9

What kind of people should care for the church?

Berean Standard Bible

5 The reason I left you in Crete was that you would set in order what was unfinished and appoint elders in every town, as I directed you. 6 An elder must be blameless, the husband of but one wife, having children who are believers and who are not open to accusation of indiscretion or insubordination.

7 As God’s steward, an overseer must be above reproach—not self-willed, not quick-tempered, not given to drunkenness, not violent, not greedy for money. 8 Instead, he must be hospitable, a lover of good, self-controlled, upright, holy, and disciplined. 9 He must hold firmly to the faithful word as it was taught, so that he can encourage others by sound teaching and refute those who contradict it.

What is the context?

Paul explains why he left Titus in Crete: to complete what remained unfinished and appoint elders in every town. The instructions move from their household life to their conduct and their grasp of the teaching.

The elder in verse 5 and overseer in verse 7 are described within the same set of qualifications. The person is God’s steward, responsible for something belonging to God. Hospitality, self-control and a love of good stand against arrogance, anger, violence and greed.

The final requirement serves the people who hear: holding firmly to the faithful word enables a leader both to encourage sound belief and to correct teaching that contradicts it. Character and the ability to teach belong together.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The USCCB note recognizes that elder and overseer refer to the same people here. Traditions differ over how those roles developed and should be organized. The Catholic Church teaches three degrees of ordained ministry—bishop, priest and deacon (Catechism 1554). The Baptist 1689 Confession 26.8–9 instead describes elders or bishops and deacons within a local church.

Household qualifications also receive different readings. Calvin understands the children as believers. A Bible.org question-and-answer article reads the requirement as reliable, well-disciplined children, connecting it with 1 Timothy 3:4–5. The Greek pistos (pis-TOS) can mean faithful or believing; Thayer lists this verse under believing. The word’s range alone does not remove the contextual question. The BSB reads children who are believers.

Calvin also reads the one-wife wording as marital faithfulness that does not exclude remarriage after a spouse’s death. Catholic practice generally requires celibacy of Latin-rite priests while allowing married priests in Eastern Catholic traditions (Catechism 1579–1580). These are distinct applications, rather than one uniform Christian rule.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 14:21–23 likewise joins strengthening disciples, appointing elders and entrusting them to the Lord. The aim is the continued care of people who have received the gospel.

In 1 Peter 5:1–4, elders shepherd God’s flock without greed or domination, as examples under the Chief Shepherd. That gives the larger setting for these qualifications: the people belong to Christ, and their leaders serve under His care and authority.

What is our position?

In Who Shepherds the Church?, we understand elder and overseer here as descriptions of the same work of caring for the church. Our study calls for plural leadership among the people, marked by character, maturity and the ability to teach and correct. Rejecting elevated ranks does not remove the need for qualified teachers who keep the gathering anchored to Scripture.
