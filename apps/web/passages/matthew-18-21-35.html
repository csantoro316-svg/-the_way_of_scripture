Passage

Matthew 18:21-35

Forgive from the Heart

Berean Standard Bible

21 Then Peter came to Jesus and asked, “Lord, how many times shall I forgive my brother who sins against me? Up to seven times?” 22 Jesus answered, “I tell you, not just seven times, but seventy-seven times!

23 Because of this, the kingdom of heaven is like a king who wanted to settle accounts with his servants. 24 As he began the settlements, a debtor owing ten thousand talents was brought to him. 25 Since the man was unable to pay, the master ordered that he be sold to pay his debt, along with his wife and children and everything he owned. 26 Then the servant fell on his knees before him. ‘Have patience with me,’ he begged, ‘and I will pay back everything.’ 27 His master had compassion on him, forgave his debt, and released him.

28 But when that servant went out, he found one of his fellow servants who owed him a hundred denarii. He grabbed him and began to choke him, saying, ‘Pay back what you owe me!’ 29 So his fellow servant fell down and begged him, ‘Have patience with me, and I will pay you back.’ 30 But he refused. Instead, he went and had the man thrown into prison until he could pay his debt. 31 When his fellow servants saw what had happened, they were greatly distressed, and they went and recounted all of this to their master.

32 Then the master summoned him and said, ‘You wicked servant! I forgave all your debt because you begged me. 33 Shouldn’t you have had mercy on your fellow servant, just as I had on you?’ 34 In anger his master turned him over to the jailers to be tortured, until he should repay all that he owed. 35 That is how My heavenly Father will treat each of you unless you forgive your brother from your heart.”

What is the context?

After the instructions about restoring a brother, Peter asks how often he must forgive one who sins against him. He suggests seven times. Jesus answers with seventy-seven in the BSB; its footnote gives seventy times seven as another translation. The story that follows moves the question beyond keeping a count.

A servant owes his king ten thousand talents and cannot pay. The king first orders the sale of the servant, his family, and his possessions, but then responds to his plea with compassion and cancels the debt. The BSB footnotes describe one talent as about twenty years' wages for a laborer and a denarius as a usual day's wage. The difference between this debt and the hundred denarii owed by a fellow servant is enormous.

The forgiven servant seizes and chokes his fellow servant, refuses his plea for patience, and has him imprisoned. When the king hears about it, he calls the servant back. He had received mercy and was expected to show mercy. Instead, the king turns him over to punishment until the debt is paid. Jesus closes with a warning about forgiving a brother from the heart.

Forgive is ἀφίημι, aphiēmi (ah-FEE-ay-mee). Thayer's entry includes releasing a debt and forgiving wrongdoing, using examples from this parable. The canceled debt gives a concrete picture of the mercy the servant received and then refused to extend.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see the enormous forgiven debt as a picture of God's mercy and the servant's conduct as a warning against withholding mercy from others. The call is to wholehearted forgiveness rather than stopping after a fixed number. Traditions differ over how the closing judgment relates to salvation.

The US Catholic bishops' notes on 18:34–35 understand it as a warning that forgiveness can be withdrawn at final judgment. Catholic teaching also describes grave sin after baptism as a loss of grace that calls for reconciliation (Catechism, 1446). Wesley on 18:34–35 reads the servant's pardon as genuinely withdrawn. The Methodist Articles, XII, likewise allow for departing from grace and affirm restoration through repentance.

Reformed churches following the Westminster Confession, 17.1–3, and Baptists following the 1689 Confession, 17.1–3, understand those truly saved as continuing in faith to the end. They also teach that serious sin brings God's displeasure and judgment in this life. Within that understanding, the warning calls professing believers to repentance and mercy; it does not mean that God finally abandons someone He has truly saved.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God's forgiveness comes through the work of Christ. Colossians 2:13–14 describes God making people alive with Christ, forgiving their sins, and canceling the debt that stood against them through the cross. That gives the parable's picture of canceled debt a clear connection with the gospel.

The mercy received is meant to shape the family God is forming. Ephesians 4:31–32 joins the removal of bitterness and malice with forgiving as God forgave in Christ. Colossians 3:12–15 connects the same forgiveness with compassion, patience, love, and peace among members of one body. God's saving mercy bears fruit in how His people live together.
