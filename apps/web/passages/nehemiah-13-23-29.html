Passage

Nehemiah 13:23-29

Why does Nehemiah point to Solomon when confronting these marriages?

Berean Standard Bible

23 In those days I also saw Jews who had married women from Ashdod, Ammon, and Moab. 24 Half of their children spoke the language of Ashdod or of the other peoples, but could not speak the language of Judah. 25 I rebuked them and called down curses on them. I beat some of these men and pulled out their hair. Then I made them take an oath before God and said, “You must not give your daughters in marriage to their sons or take their daughters as wives for your sons or for yourselves! 26 Did not King Solomon of Israel sin in matters like this? There was not a king like him among many nations, and he was loved by his God, who made him king over all Israel—yet foreign women drew him into sin. 27 Must we now hear that you too are doing all this terrible evil and acting unfaithfully against our God by marrying foreign women?”

28 Even one of the sons of Jehoiada son of Eliashib the high priest had become a son-in-law to Sanballat the Horonite. Therefore I drove him away from me. 29 Remember them, O my God, because they have defiled the priesthood and the covenant of the priesthood and of the Levites.

What is the context?

Nehemiah sees Jews married to women from Ashdod, Ammon and Moab. Half of the children speak the languages of those peoples and cannot speak the language of Judah. He confronts the men, calls down curses, beats some and pulls out their hair, then requires an oath against arranging such marriages.

He appeals to Solomon’s example. 1 Kings 11:1–13 recounts how Solomon’s wives turned his heart toward other gods despite the favor God had shown him. Nehemiah’s warning concerns unfaithfulness to God, not an assumption that Solomon lacked intelligence or standing.

The problem reaches the priestly family: a son of Jehoiada, grandson of Eliashib the high priest, is Sanballat’s son-in-law. Nehemiah drives him away and appeals to God concerning the defilement of the priesthood.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Gill understand the marriages as breaches of the community’s obligations and read the beating as punishment administered under Nehemiah’s authority as governor. The narrative records severe measures alongside his warning from Solomon’s failure.

The New Testament gives instructions for Christian marriages in their own setting. 1 Corinthians 7:10–16 tells a believer not to divorce an unbelieving spouse who is willing to remain. That instruction must be heard when considering the relationship between this ancient community’s reforms and Christian family life.
