Passage

Exodus 23:10-13

Who was meant to benefit from Israel’s rest?

Berean Standard Bible

10 For six years you are to sow your land and gather its produce, 11 but in the seventh year you must let it rest and lie fallow, so that the poor among your people may eat from the field and the wild animals may consume what they leave. Do the same with your vineyard and olive grove.

12 For six days you are to do your work, but on the seventh day you must cease, so that your ox and your donkey may rest and the son of your maidservant may be refreshed, as well as the foreign resident. 13 Pay close attention to everything I have said to you. You must not invoke the names of other gods; they must not be heard on your lips.

What is the context?

The seventh year is a rest for the land, but its purpose is also about people and animals. The poor may eat what grows, and the wild animals may eat what remains. The vineyard and olive grove are included. Israel’s use of the land must leave room for needs beyond the landholder’s own harvest.

The weekly rest likewise reaches those who could otherwise be kept working: the ox, donkey, servant’s child and foreign resident. The command gives them refreshment. Rest is not reserved for the person with enough power to decide the household’s schedule.

Leviticus 25:18–22 later addresses the question of food during the unsown year. God promises a sixth-year crop sufficient for three years, carrying the people through the return to sowing and the next harvest. Here the rest instructions end with a call to heed everything God has said and refuse the invocation of other gods. Work, provision and loyalty to Him belong together.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers generally see care for the poor and rest for dependents alongside trust in God’s provision. Wesley emphasizes that trust: the people must allow the year’s rest without treating constant production as their only security.

Christian traditions differ over the weekly Sabbath’s present observance. The Westminster Confession, 21.7–8, teaches a continuing weekly obligation observed on Sunday. The Seventh-day Adventist statement of belief retains the seventh day. The Catholic Catechism, 2174–2176, explains Sunday worship in relation to Christ’s resurrection and fulfillment of the Sabbath. These differences about Christian practice should be distinguished from the rest this passage explicitly requires for Israel’s land, workers and animals.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The later history shows that the land’s rest was a serious part of Israel’s covenant responsibility. 2 Chronicles 36:15–23 describes the people’s refusal to hear God’s messengers, the exile and the land enjoying its Sabbath rest during the desolation. The account ends with God opening the way for a return. His dealings include judgment and renewed opportunity, rather than treating the land as something His people possess without responsibility.

The theme also reaches beyond agricultural or weekly rest. Hebrews 4:1–11 joins God’s creation rest, the wilderness warning and the continuing promise of entering His rest. It calls for a response of faith. Hebrews is developing that larger biblical theme, not directly explaining every feature of this seventh-year law.
