Passage

Genesis 43:1-15

The Return to Egypt with Benjamin

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now the famine was still severe in the land. 2 So when Jacob’s sons had eaten all the grain they had brought from Egypt, their father said to them, “Go back and buy us a little more food.”

3 But Judah replied, “The man solemnly warned us, ‘You will not see my face again unless your brother is with you.’ 4 If you will send our brother with us, we will go down and buy food for you. 5 But if you will not send him, we will not go; for the man told us, ‘You will not see my face again unless your brother is with you.’”

6 “Why did you bring this trouble upon me?” Israel asked. “Why did you tell the man you had another brother?”

7 They replied, “The man questioned us in detail about ourselves and our family: ‘Is your father still alive? Do you have another brother?’ And we answered him accordingly. How could we possibly know that he would say, ‘Bring your brother here’?”

8 And Judah said to his father Israel, “Send the boy with me, and we will go at once, so that we may live and not die—neither we, nor you, nor our children. 9 I will guarantee his safety. You may hold me personally responsible. If I do not bring him back and set him before you, then may I bear the guilt before you all my life. 10 If we had not delayed, we could have come and gone twice by now.”

11 Then their father Israel said to them, “If it must be so, then do this: Put some of the best products of the land in your packs and carry them down as a gift for the man—a little balm and a little honey, spices and myrrh, pistachios and almonds. 12 Take double the silver with you so that you may return the silver that was put back into the mouths of your sacks. Perhaps it was a mistake. 13 Take your brother as well, and return to the man at once. 14 May God Almighty grant you mercy before the man, that he may release your other brother along with Benjamin. As for me, if I am bereaved, I am bereaved.”

15 So the men took these gifts, along with double the amount of silver, and Benjamin as well. Then they hurried down to Egypt and stood before Joseph.

What is the context?

The famine continues, and the grain brought from Egypt runs out. Jacob asks his sons to buy more, but Judah reminds him that they cannot appear before the Egyptian ruler without Benjamin. The family cannot avoid the earlier demand simply by waiting or making another purchase.

Jacob asks why they disclosed the existence of another brother. They reply that the ruler questioned them about their father and family and that they could not have anticipated his demand. Judah then offers to take personal responsibility for Benjamin's return. Unlike Reuben's offer involving his sons' lives, Judah places the continuing responsibility on himself.

This is a significant development for the man who proposed selling Joseph in Genesis 37. Judah now promises to protect the other son of Rachel so that the whole household, including its children, may live. His promise will be tested when Benjamin later faces danger.

Jacob finally agrees. He sends gifts from the land, additional silver for food, and the money that had been returned, allowing for the possibility of a mistake. He prays that God Almighty will grant them mercy before the ruler and bring back both Simeon and Benjamin. He acknowledges the possibility of bereavement even as he permits the journey.

The brothers take the gifts, double the silver, and Benjamin, and go down to stand before Joseph. Practical preparations and an appeal for God's mercy accompany a decision Jacob had earlier refused to make.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly recognize Judah's new willingness to accept responsibility and Jacob's difficult step of allowing Benjamin to go. Wesley's notes on Genesis 43:9, 11–14 connect Judah's care for Benjamin with a changed response to his earlier wrong and read Jacob's prayer as an appeal for mercy amid uncertainty.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God's care for the promised family includes more than keeping it alive. The men who once abandoned Joseph must now take responsibility for another brother. Judah's commitment gives the story a direction toward restored relationships, while the family's need brings it back to the person God has placed in Egypt to help it.

That movement fits the wider call for repentance to bear fruit. In Luke 3:8, John calls Abraham's descendants to changed conduct instead of resting on their ancestry. Here, too, belonging to the promise family does not remove the need for a different way of treating one another. Judah's words begin to put responsibility where his earlier proposal had put another brother at risk.
