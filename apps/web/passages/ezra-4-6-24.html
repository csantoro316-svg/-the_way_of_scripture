Passage

Ezra 4:6-24

How do the letters against Jerusalem fit the story?

Berean Standard Bible

6 At the beginning of the reign of Xerxes, an accusation was lodged against the people of Judah and Jerusalem. 7 And in the days of Artaxerxes king of Persia, Bishlam, Mithredath, Tabeel, and the rest of his associates wrote a letter to Artaxerxes. It was written in Aramaic and then translated. 8 Rehum the commander and Shimshai the scribe wrote the letter against Jerusalem to King Artaxerxes as follows: 9 From Rehum the commander, Shimshai the scribe, and the rest of their associates—the judges and officials over Tripolis, Persia, Erech and Babylon, the Elamites of Susa, 10 and the rest of the peoples whom the great and honorable Ashurbanipal deported and settled in the cities of Samaria and elsewhere west of the Euphrates.

11 (This is the text of the letter they sent to him.) To King Artaxerxes, From your servants, the men west of the Euphrates: 12 Let it be known to the king that the Jews who came from you to us have returned to Jerusalem and are rebuilding that rebellious and wicked city. They are restoring its walls and repairing its foundations. 13 Let it now be known to the king that if that city is rebuilt and its walls are restored, they will not pay tribute, duty, or toll, and the royal treasury will suffer. 14 Now because we are in the service of the palace and it is not fitting for us to allow the king to be dishonored, we have sent to inform the king 15 that a search should be made of the record books of your fathers. In these books you will discover and verify that the city is a rebellious city, harmful to kings and provinces, inciting sedition from ancient times. That is why this city was destroyed. 16 We advise the king that if this city is rebuilt and its walls are restored, you will have no dominion west of the Euphrates.

17 Then the king sent this reply: To Rehum the commander, Shimshai the scribe, and the rest of your associates living in Samaria and elsewhere in the region west of the Euphrates: Greetings. 18 The letter you sent us has been translated and read in my presence. 19 I issued a decree, and a search was conducted. It was discovered that this city has revolted against kings from ancient times, engaging in rebellion and sedition. 20 And mighty kings have ruled over Jerusalem and exercised authority over the whole region west of the Euphrates; and tribute, duty, and toll were paid to them. 21 Now, therefore, issue an order for these men to stop, so that this city will not be rebuilt until I so order. 22 See that you do not neglect this matter. Why allow this threat to increase and the royal interests to suffer? 23 When the text of the letter from King Artaxerxes was read to Rehum, Shimshai the scribe, and their associates, they went immediately to the Jews in Jerusalem and forcibly stopped them.

24 Thus the construction of the house of God in Jerusalem ceased, and it remained at a standstill until the second year of the reign of Darius king of Persia.

What is the context?

The chapter records an accusation in the reign of Xerxes and a letter to Artaxerxes. The letter concerns Jerusalem’s walls and the claim that a rebuilt city will refuse taxes and threaten Persian control west of the Euphrates.

The king orders an archive search, receives a report of Jerusalem’s earlier rebellions and commands the rebuilding of the city to stop until he issues another order. The officials then use force to stop the work.

It helps to distinguish the city and its walls from the temple project. Xerxes and Artaxerxes belong to a later period than the Darius named at the chapter’s end; the sequence of Persian kings is given in the USCCB introduction to Ezra. On the reading that these letters illustrate later opposition, verse 24 returns to the earlier interruption of temple building, which lasts until Darius’s second year.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ over the timeline. Wesley and Gill identify the royal names in a way that puts the letters before Darius, treating the chapter as a continuing sequence. They connect the order against the city with the stopping of temple work.

The Cambridge commentary instead identifies the rulers as Xerxes and Artaxerxes I and reads verses 6–23 as later examples of opposition before the account returns to the temple in verse 24. The reference to city walls is an important part of that reading.

Henry emphasizes the letters’ use of political suspicion to obstruct the work. Past rebellion becomes the basis for predicting future disloyalty. Whatever view is taken of the timeline, the royal reply answers the accusations presented to the king; it does not establish that the returnees had actually planned the future tax revolt alleged against them.
