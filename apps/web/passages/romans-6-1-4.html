Passage

Romans 6:1-4

Why does grace call us into a new life rather than more sin?

Berean Standard Bible

1 What then shall we say? Shall we continue in sin so that grace may increase? 2 Certainly not! How can we who died to sin live in it any longer? 3 Or aren’t you aware that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into His death? 4 We were therefore buried with Him through baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may walk in newness of life.

What is the context?

Paul has said that grace abounds where sin increases. He now rejects the conclusion that people should continue in sin to make grace greater. His answer begins with their changed relationship to sin: they have died to it.

He brings baptism into the explanation. Those baptized into Christ Jesus have been baptized into His death and buried with Him, so that they may walk in newness of life as Christ was raised. Death and resurrection are connected with the life believers are now called to live.

The verb baptize comes from baptizō (bap-TID-zoh), βαπτίζω. Thayer’s entry includes dipping, immersion and washing, and explains the use here in terms of fellowship with Christ and His death. The word and the passage’s burial imagery matter, while the differences over baptism’s mode and effect require more than a bare word definition.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see union with Christ’s death and resurrection as the foundation of the new life Paul describes. Where traditions differ is how they explain baptism’s part in that union.

The Catholic Church teaches that baptism brings forgiveness of sins and new birth in the Holy Spirit; see the Catechism, paragraphs 1262–1266. This is a work of God’s grace through the sacrament, rather than a claim that water has independent power.

The Reformed Westminster Confession 28.1–6 calls baptism a sign and seal of grace, incorporation into Christ and new life. It allows pouring or sprinkling and baptism of believers’ children, while denying that every baptized person is necessarily regenerated or that grace is tied to the exact moment of administration.

The 1689 Baptist Confession 29.1–4 describes a sign of fellowship with Christ in His death and resurrection, remission of sins and walking in new life. It limits baptism to those professing repentance and faith, and requires immersion. This represents churches that receive that confession, rather than every Baptist’s personal explanation.

The Methodist Articles XVI–XVII describe sacraments as signs through which God works to strengthen faith, and baptism as a sign of new birth as well as profession. They retain infant baptism. Wesley on verse 3 speaks of being joined to Christ in baptism through faith. These readings share the call to new life while differing over recipients, mode and the relation between the sign and God’s work.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Colossians 2:11–13 likewise connects being buried with Christ in baptism and raised through faith in God’s power. The One who raised Jesus brings people from death to life. The connection comes from the New Testament itself.

Galatians 3:26–29 joins faith, being clothed with Christ in baptism and belonging to one family in Him. The new life is more than a private improvement project: it belongs to God’s work of gathering people into Christ across former divisions.

What is our position?

The Baptism study understands this passage as death and resurrection with Christ enacted in the believer’s baptism. Our position favors immersion because it expresses burial and rising, and treats baptism as a real, faith-filled act of obedience rather than an empty performance. The study also distinguishes this act from earning salvation: forgiveness and the gift of the Spirit come through faith, and the water has no power apart from Christ.
