Passage

Revelation 10:8-11

Why does John eat a scroll that tastes sweet but turns bitter?

Berean Standard Bible

8 Then the voice that I had heard from heaven spoke to me again, saying, “Go, take the small scroll that lies open in the hand of the angel standing on the sea and on the land.” 9 And I went to the angel and said, “Give me the small scroll.” “Take it and eat it,” he said. “It will make your stomach bitter, but in your mouth it will be as sweet as honey.” 10 So I took the small scroll from the angel’s hand and ate it, and it was as sweet as honey in my mouth. But when I had eaten it, my stomach turned bitter.

11 And they told me, “You must prophesy again about many peoples and nations and tongues and kings.”

What is the context?

The voice from heaven now tells John to take the open scroll from the angel. John goes to him and asks for it. He is then told to eat it, with a warning that it will taste like honey but make his stomach bitter.

John obeys, and both parts happen as promised. The sweetness does not cancel the bitterness, and the bitterness does not make the message untrue. His experience joins the pleasure of receiving a message from God with the difficulty of what that message contains and what delivering it may involve.

The scene ends with a renewed task: John must prophesy again about many peoples, nations, languages and kings. Eating the scroll prepares for speaking. He receives the message before being sent to deliver it, and the scope reaches well beyond one local community.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The main explanations share a common picture: John must take God’s message to heart before proclaiming it. Wesley connects the sweetness with the good things promised and the bitterness with coming evils. Gill includes the joy of God’s kingdom alongside suffering and resistance to the message. Walvoord likewise emphasizes both the preciousness of God’s word and the grief associated with its judgments and the messenger’s trials.

The USCCB notes connect the sweetness with God’s people finally overcoming and the bitterness with their suffering. These explanations place the emphasis somewhat differently, but they do not require a denomination-by-denomination division. The central movement is the same: John receives the message, experiences both sweetness and bitterness, and is commissioned to speak again.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The direct scriptural parallel is Ezekiel 2:8–10 and 3:1–11. Ezekiel receives a scroll containing lamentation, mourning and woe, yet finds it sweet as honey. He is then sent to speak God’s words to Israel, whether they listen or refuse. Revelation presents John in that recognizable pattern of a prophet receiving a message before carrying it to others.

There is also a difference in the stated scope. Ezekiel is sent to the house of Israel; John is told to prophesy about many peoples, nations, languages and kings. God’s dealings with the world include making His message known across human boundaries. The messenger’s responsibility is to receive and faithfully deliver it, including the parts that bring sorrow as well as those that give hope.
