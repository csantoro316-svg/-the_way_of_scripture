Passage

Ezekiel 9:5-11

Why does judgment begin at the sanctuary?

Berean Standard Bible

5 And as I listened, He said to the others, “Follow him through the city and start killing; do not show pity or spare anyone! 6 Slaughter the old men, the young men and maidens, the women and children; but do not go near anyone who has the mark. Now begin at My sanctuary.” So they began with the elders who were before the temple. 7 Then He told them, “Defile the temple and fill the courts with the slain. Go forth!” So they went out and began killing throughout the city.

8 While they were killing, I was left alone. And I fell facedown and cried out, “Oh, Lord GOD, when You pour out Your wrath on Jerusalem, will You destroy the entire remnant of Israel?” 9 He replied, “The iniquity of the house of Israel and Judah is exceedingly great. The land is full of bloodshed, and the city is full of perversity. For they say, ‘The LORD has forsaken the land; the LORD does not see.’ 10 But as for Me, I will not look on them with pity, nor will I spare them. I will bring their deeds down upon their own heads.” 11 Then the man clothed in linen with the writing kit at his side reported back, “I have done as You commanded.”

What is the context?

The armed figures are ordered to follow the man with the writing kit. They must spare everyone who bears the mark, but the judgment otherwise reaches across the city, without the protection that age, status or closeness to the temple might seem to offer. It begins with the elders at the sanctuary.

This is the execution of judgment within the vision, continuing the exposure of the temple’s corruption. The place intended for worship is filled with the slain. The sanctuary cannot shield the conduct that has been exposed there.

Ezekiel falls facedown and pleads for the remnant of Israel. He is overwhelmed by the prospect of destruction, rather than pleased at the suffering he sees.

The Lord answers by naming the land’s bloodshed and the city’s injustice. The people have claimed that He has abandoned the land and does not see. He declares that their own deeds will come upon them. The man in linen then reports that his task has been completed.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley connect the starting place with the seriousness of sin among those closest to the sanctuary. Religious standing does not cancel responsibility to God. Wesley also makes clear that the killing is being shown in a vision.

Henry joins the warning with an appeal to keep pleading for others. Ezekiel’s response supports that concern: even after seeing the offenses, he asks whether the whole remnant will be destroyed.

The order to spare the marked people belongs to the particular judgment shown here. It does not mean that faithful people never suffer, nor does this account pronounce the eternal destiny of each person depicted in the vision.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

First Peter 4:12–19 says that judgment begins with God’s household. Peter applies this to Christians facing a fiery trial and calls them to entrust themselves to their faithful Creator while doing good. His words develop the theme of accountability among God’s people; they do not make Christian suffering identical to Jerusalem’s punishment.

Second Peter 2:4–9 recalls earlier judgments and God’s rescue of Noah and Lot. Lot’s distress over the wrongdoing around him also resembles the grief of those marked in Ezekiel’s vision. Peter’s conclusion is that the Lord knows how to rescue the godly and hold the unrighteous for judgment.

These connections hold together the truths the vision presents: God sees wrongdoing, He knows those who belong to Him, and His people can entrust themselves to Him even when judgment is frightening.
