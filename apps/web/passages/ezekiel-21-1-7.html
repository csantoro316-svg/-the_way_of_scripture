Passage

Ezekiel 21:1-7

Why must Ezekiel groan over the coming news?

Berean Standard Bible

1 And the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, set your face against Jerusalem and preach against the sanctuaries. Prophesy against the land of Israel 3 and tell her that this is what the LORD says: ‘I am against you, and I will draw My sword from its sheath and cut off from you both the righteous and the wicked. 4 Because I will cut off both the righteous and the wicked, My sword will be unsheathed against everyone from south to north. 5 Then all flesh will know that I, the LORD, have taken My sword from its sheath, not to return it again.’

6 But you, son of man, groan! Groan before their eyes with a broken heart and bitter grief. 7 And when they ask, ‘Why are you groaning?’ you are to say, ‘Because of the news that is coming. Every heart will melt, and every hand will go limp. Every spirit will faint, and every knee will turn to water.’ Yes, it is coming and it will surely happen, declares the Lord GOD.”

What is the context?

The forest-fire warning is now stated directly against Jerusalem, its sanctuaries and the land of Israel. The Lord pictures the judgment as His sword drawn from its sheath.

The calamity reaches both righteous and wicked, from south to north. Its scope prevents anyone from assuming that the collapse of the land will leave ordinary life untouched.

Ezekiel must groan publicly with a broken heart and bitter grief. When people ask why, he is to tell them about the news that is coming: hearts will melt, hands go limp and strength fail.

The prophet’s response makes the warning personal. He does not announce the devastation as a detached observer or treat it as something to celebrate.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads this as the explanation of the preceding chapter’s forest picture. He emphasizes that someone announcing judgment should also grieve for those facing ruin.

Wesley notes that public calamities can involve righteous people along with others. The passage’s sweeping language describes the devastation of the land; being personally faithful does not mean that a person’s community cannot fall into crisis.
