Passage

Matthew 16:24-28

Following Jesus When It Costs Your Life

Berean Standard Bible

24 Then Jesus told His disciples, “If anyone wants to come after Me, he must deny himself and take up his cross and follow Me. 25 For whoever wants to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake will find it. 26 What will it profit a man if he gains the whole world, yet forfeits his soul? Or what can a man give in exchange for his soul?

27 For the Son of Man will come in His Father’s glory with His angels, and then He will repay each one according to what he has done. 28 Truly I tell you, some who are standing here will not taste death before they see the Son of Man coming in His kingdom.”

What is the context?

After correcting Peter's opposition to the cross, Jesus addresses His disciples about following Him. They must deny themselves, take up their cross, and follow. The road He has just announced shapes the life of those who belong to Him.

The call reaches beyond giving up an occasional comfort. Jesus speaks of losing one's life for His sake and asks what the whole world could be worth if a person forfeits the soul. Allegiance to Him changes what is worth protecting and what can be surrendered.

The Greek word ψυχή, psychē (psoo-KHAY), appears in both the life and soul sayings. Thayer's entry includes life, the self, and the soul among its uses, and specifically connects verse 25 with the contrast between present life and life in God's kingdom. The repeated word ties the sayings together; the stakes reach beyond possessions or success.

Jesus then speaks of the Son of Man coming in the Father's glory and repaying each person according to what they have done. He also promises that some standing there will see Him coming in His kingdom before they die. The Greek word βασιλεία, basileia (bah-see-LAY-ah), can describe royal rule as well as a kingdom, according to Thayer's entry. Readers differ over which event shows that royal rule in verse 28.

The next scene, Matthew 17:1–9, comes six days later: Peter, James, and John see Jesus in glory on the mountain. Later the disciples also witness His resurrection and carry His message. The call to costly following is given alongside assurance that His glory and authority are real.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand this as a serious call to follow Jesus even when faithfulness costs deeply. Wesley on Matthew 16:24–27 connects self-denial with doing God's will and treats the warning about life as reaching into eternity. The Catholic Catechism, 618, likewise places the disciple's taking up the cross within sharing the path of Christ's self-giving.

Readers explain verse 28 in different ways. John Chrysostom's Homily 56 on Matthew connects it with the following Transfiguration: the disciples receive a glimpse of the glory Jesus has promised. The US Catholic bishops' note on Matthew 16:28 instead emphasizes His rule becoming visible after the resurrection.

Wesley's note on Matthew 16:28 connects it with the powerful growth of Christ's church and the judgment on Jerusalem. These explanations differ over the event in view, while recognizing a promise that some of Jesus' hearers will witness His kingly authority.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus connects His own self-giving and the disciple's path in John 12:23–28. He compares His coming death with a grain of wheat that dies and bears much fruit, then speaks of losing life in this world, following Him, and the Father's honor for His servant. God's saving work gives the pattern for a life of faithful service.

Hebrews 2:14–18 adds that His victory frees people held in slavery by the fear of death. The call to risk everything for Him rests on more than determination alone. The Savior has entered the danger and brings His people a hope that death cannot finally take away.

In 2 Peter 1:16–19, the mountain vision is remembered as eyewitness testimony to Jesus' majesty and as support for proclaiming His power and coming. His followers are given reason to trust the glory promised beyond suffering. They are called to live by that hope while they follow Him now.
