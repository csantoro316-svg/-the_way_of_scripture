Passage

Exodus 20:1-17

What kind of life did God call Israel to live?

Berean Standard Bible

1 And God spoke all these words: 2 “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. 3 You shall have no other gods before Me. 4 You shall not make for yourself an idol in the form of anything in the heavens above, on the earth below, or in the waters beneath. 5 You shall not bow down to them or worship them; for I, the LORD your God, am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on their children to the third and fourth generations of those who hate Me, 6 but showing loving devotion to a thousand generations of those who love Me and keep My commandments. 7 You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain, for the LORD will not leave anyone unpunished who takes His name in vain.

8 Remember the Sabbath day by keeping it holy. 9 Six days you shall labor and do all your work, 10 but the seventh day is a Sabbath to the LORD your God, on which you must not do any work—neither you, nor your son or daughter, nor your manservant or maidservant or livestock, nor the foreigner within your gates. 11 For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth and the sea and all that is in them, but on the seventh day He rested. Therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day and set it apart as holy.

12 Honor your father and mother, so that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you. 13 You shall not murder. 14 You shall not commit adultery. 15 You shall not steal. 16 You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor. 17 You shall not covet your neighbor’s house. You shall not covet your neighbor’s wife, or his manservant or maidservant, or his ox or donkey, or anything that belongs to your neighbor.”

What is the context?

God begins by identifying Himself as the LORD who brought Israel out of slavery. The commandments are spoken to people He has already rescued. They describe the loyalty and conduct that belong to life with Him under the covenant.

The opening commands call Israel to worship the LORD alone, without making an idol to bow before or serve. His jealousy means that He requires their undivided loyalty. The warning about the third and fourth generations stands beside the much longer reach of His loving devotion to a thousand generations. Ezekiel 18:19–20 also teaches this: a son who does what is right is not condemned for his father’s guilt.

The command about God’s name concerns its misuse. The Hebrew word שָׁוְא, shav (SHAHV), includes emptiness and falsehood in Brown–Driver–Briggs. Invoking God’s name falsely or treating it as an empty expression dishonors the One whose name is being used.

The Sabbath sets apart a day of rest in remembrance of God’s completed work of creation. The rest extends through the household to servants, foreigners and livestock. Honoring father and mother comes with a promise concerning life in the land God is giving Israel.

The remaining commands protect life, marriage, another person’s possessions and truthful dealings with neighbors. The final command reaches into desire itself. The verb חָמַד, chamad (khah-MAHD), can mean desire or take pleasure in; Brown–Driver–Briggs identifies the selfish desire for another person’s belongings in this passage. God addresses what people want, as well as what they do.

The chapter presents these words together without numbering them individually. Deuteronomy 4:12–13 explicitly calls them the Ten Commandments and recalls their place in the covenant and on the two stone tablets.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The numbering differs across traditions. The Jewish ordering described by Chabad begins with God identifying Himself as Israel’s God. The Catholic Catechism, 2066, and Luther’s Small Catechism join the prohibitions of other gods and images and count two commands about coveting. The Reformed Westminster Larger Catechism, 103–110, counts other gods and images separately. Each numbering system arranges the commands within this passage.

Christians also differ over images in worship. The Catholic Church’s Catechism, 2129–2132, permits honoring sacred images because the honor is directed to the person portrayed; it distinguishes this from the adoration due to God alone and grounds the practice in the Son becoming human. The Reformed Larger Catechism, question 109, forbids making representations of God and worshiping God through them.

The Sabbath’s Christian application also differs. The Westminster Confession, 21.7–8, teaches a continuing weekly Sabbath now observed on Sunday. The Catholic Catechism, 2174–2176, describes Sunday as fulfilling the Sabbath through Christ’s resurrection. The Lutheran Large Catechism retains Sunday for shared worship and hearing God’s word while saying no day is inherently better than another. The Seventh-day Adventist Church’s Sabbath belief maintains the seventh day as the continuing appointed day.

On the continuing moral commands, the Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 19, and the Methodist Articles, article VI, distinguish these duties from Israel’s ceremonial and civil rules. Both teach that Christians remain bound to moral obedience. The Catholic Catechism, 2068–2072, likewise teaches the commandments’ continuing obligation.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus addresses these commands in Matthew 5:17–30. He says He has come to fulfill the Law and the Prophets, and brings anger, reconciliation and lust into the discussion of murder and adultery. God’s purpose includes a heart and relationships brought under His instruction.

Romans 13:8–10 explicitly names the commands against adultery, murder, stealing and coveting, and gathers them under love for the neighbor. Ephesians 6:1–4 also carries the command to honor parents into Christian households, while instructing fathers to nurture children rather than provoke them. The commands become concrete instruction for a people learning to live in love.

In the new-covenant promise recalled in Hebrews 10:15–18, God puts His laws in hearts and minds and remembers sins no more. The chapter joins that promise to Christ’s completed offering. Scripture’s larger movement is toward forgiveness and a people whose life is changed from within.
