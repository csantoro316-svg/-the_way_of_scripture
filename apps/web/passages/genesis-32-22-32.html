Passage

Genesis 32:22-32

Jacob Wrestles with God

Berean Standard Bible

22 During the night Jacob got up and took his two wives, his two maidservants, and his eleven sons, and crossed the ford of the Jabbok. 23 He took them and sent them across the stream, along with all his possessions.

24 So Jacob was left all alone, and there a man wrestled with him until daybreak. 25 When the man saw that he could not overpower Jacob, he struck the socket of Jacob’s hip and dislocated it as they wrestled. 26 Then the man said, “Let me go, for it is daybreak.” But Jacob replied, “I will not let you go unless you bless me.”

27 “What is your name?” the man asked. “Jacob,” he replied.

28 Then the man said, “Your name will no longer be Jacob, but Israel, because you have struggled with God and with men, and you have prevailed.”

29 And Jacob requested, “Please tell me your name.” But he replied, “Why do you ask my name?” Then he blessed Jacob there.

30 So Jacob named the place Peniel, saying, “Indeed, I have seen God face to face, and yet my life was spared.”

31 The sun rose above him as he passed by Penuel, and he was limping because of his hip. 32 Therefore to this day the Israelites do not eat the tendon attached to the socket of the hip, because the socket of Jacob’s hip was struck near that tendon.

What is the context?

During the night, Jacob sends his family and possessions across the Jabbok and is left alone. A man wrestles with him until daybreak. The account introduces the struggle abruptly and lets its meaning become clearer through the encounter, rather than explaining the man's identity at the outset.

Jacob's hip is dislocated, yet he holds on and asks for a blessing. He receives the name Israel, with the explanation that he has struggled with God and with men and prevailed. The blessing comes with an injury: at sunrise he goes on limping, changed by what has happened.

Jacob names the place Peniel because he has seen God face to face and survived. The name is פְּנִיאֵל, Pĕnîʾēl (peh-nee-AYL), face of God; Brown–Driver–Briggs records this meaning. His words identify the encounter as far more than an unexplained fight with a traveler.

Hosea 12:3–6 later describes Jacob struggling with God and the angel, weeping and seeking favor. That explanation brings pleading and dependence into view alongside the physical wrestling. Genesis closes by connecting Jacob's injured hip with an Israelite food restriction that preserves the memory of the event.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see an encounter with God that also teaches dependence and persistent prayer. Wesley's notes on Genesis 32:24–29 emphasize faith and supplication; the Catholic Catechism, paragraph 2573, likewise treats the scene as a picture of prayer's struggle and perseverance.

Christians differ over the figure's identity. Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 58, identifies the One who wrestles with Jacob with the divine One who later becomes human in Christ. A different Christian approach understands God to be acting through an angelic representative, consistent with Augustine's broader explanation of the patriarchs' divine appearances in On the Trinity 3.11.27.

A distinct Jewish reading appears in Rashi's commentary on Genesis 32:25, which identifies the wrestler as Esau's guardian angel. These are particular interpretations within their traditions; the passage's importance in prayer and Jacob's changed life is not dependent on every reader giving the same account of the figure.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hosea does more than recall a remarkable night. After speaking of Jacob's pleading, Hosea 12:6 calls God's people to return to Him, maintain love and justice, and wait for Him. The ancestor's experience becomes a call for the people bearing his name to seek God faithfully.

Jacob also leaves both blessed and weakened. That combination fits the wider theme expressed in 2 Corinthians 12:7–10, where Christ's sufficient grace meets Paul in weakness. The accounts differ, but both direct attention away from strength as the foundation of security and toward the God whose grace sustains His people.
