Passage

Judges 7:1-8

Why does God make Gideon’s army smaller?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Early in the morning Jerubbaal (that is, Gideon) and all the men with him camped beside the spring of Harod. And the camp of Midian was north of them in the valley near the hill of Moreh. 2 Then the LORD said to Gideon, “You have too many men for Me to deliver Midian into their hands, lest Israel glorify themselves over Me, saying, ‘My own hand has saved me.’ 3 Now, therefore, proclaim in the hearing of the men: ‘Whoever is fearful and trembling may turn back and leave Mount Gilead.’” So twenty-two thousand of them turned back, but ten thousand remained.

4 Then the LORD said to Gideon, “There are still too many men. Take them down to the water, and I will sift them for you there. If I say to you, ‘This one shall go with you,’ he shall go. But if I say, ‘This one shall not go with you,’ he shall not go.” 5 So Gideon brought the men down to the water, and the LORD said to him, “Separate those who lap the water with their tongues like a dog from those who kneel to drink.” 6 And the number of those who lapped the water with their hands to their mouths was three hundred men; all the others knelt to drink. 7 Then the LORD said to Gideon, “With the three hundred men who lapped the water I will save you and deliver the Midianites into your hand. But all the others are to go home.” 8 So Gideon sent the rest of the Israelites to their tents but kept the three hundred men, who took charge of the provisions and rams’ horns of the others. And the camp of Midian lay below him in the valley.

What is the context?

Gideon’s men gather near the spring of Harod, with Midian’s camp below them in the valley. God says the army is too large for the rescue He intends, and gives the reason: Israel might claim that its own strength had saved it. The reduction is meant to make the source of the victory clear.

The fearful are first allowed to leave, as Deuteronomy 20:1–8 had provided for Israel’s armies. Twenty-two thousand depart and ten thousand remain. God then separates them by how they drink at the water. The three hundred who lap with their hands to their mouths are kept; the others return to their tents.

The testing uses the Hebrew verb צָרַף, tsaraph (tsah-RAHF), which can mean to refine metal or to test. Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically identifies the testing of warriors here. God determines whom Gideon is to take and promises to save through those three hundred. The passage explains the choice’s purpose, while leaving much about the men themselves unstated.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Henry, and Gill agree on the central purpose: God removes the basis for Israel to boast in its own strength. Dependence on Him is to shape how the people understand the rescue.

The drinking test receives different explanations. Wesley sees the men as hardy and eager to act, taking only a quick drink. Gill discusses both strong-warrior and weak-warrior readings, then emphasizes that the test reduces the number and makes God’s choice known to Gideon. Their explanations should be distinguished from the stated reason for the smaller army; the narrative does not give a full character description of either group.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God’s power made known through human weakness remains a theme in the New Testament. In Second Corinthians 12:7–10, Paul asks for a painful burden to be removed and instead receives Christ’s assurance that His grace is sufficient and His power is perfected in weakness.

The connection reaches beyond winning a battle. Paul learns to rely on Christ even while difficulty remains. God’s purpose can be served through people whose limitations are still real, with the glory belonging to Him rather than to their apparent strength.
