Passage

Genesis 12:10-20

Abram and Sarai in Egypt

Berean Standard Bible

10 Now there was a famine in the land. So Abram went down to Egypt to live there for a while because the famine was severe. 11 As he was about to enter Egypt, he said to his wife Sarai, “Look, I know that you are a beautiful woman, 12 and when the Egyptians see you, they will say, ‘This is his wife.’ Then they will kill me but will let you live. 13 Please say you are my sister, so that I will be treated well for your sake, and on account of you my life will be spared.”

14 So when Abram entered Egypt, the Egyptians saw that the woman was very beautiful. 15 When Pharaoh’s officials saw Sarai, they commended her to him, and she was taken into the palace of Pharaoh. 16 He treated Abram well on her account, and Abram acquired sheep and cattle, male and female donkeys, menservants and maidservants, and camels.

17 The LORD, however, afflicted Pharaoh and his household with severe plagues because of Abram’s wife Sarai. 18 So Pharaoh summoned Abram and asked, “What have you done to me? Why didn’t you tell me she was your wife? 19 Why did you say, ‘She is my sister,’ so that I took her as my wife? Now then, here is your wife. Take her and go!”

20 Then Pharaoh gave his men orders concerning Abram, and they sent him away with his wife and all his possessions.

What is the context?

A severe famine interrupts Abram's life in the land God has promised. He goes to Egypt to stay for a while, but fear of being killed because of Sarai's beauty leads him to conceal their marriage. His stated purpose is his own survival. The plan exposes Sarai to the very danger he is trying to escape for himself: she is taken into Pharaoh's household.

Abram receives livestock and servants because of Sarai, yet these gains come while husband and wife are separated. Material increase does not make the situation a success. The LORD intervenes with plagues on Pharaoh's household because of Sarai, and Pharaoh confronts Abram with the deception before sending them away together.

The passage reports Abram's fear, the taking of Sarai, God's intervention, and Pharaoh's rebuke. It does not tell us the nature of the plagues or describe what happened to Sarai inside the palace. The force of the account is already clear without filling in those missing details: the man who has received God's promise endangers his household through a fearful attempt to protect himself.

How is this passage commonly understood?

A common Christian reading sees a serious failure of trust in Abram's deception and God's care in Sarai's deliverance. Wesley's notes on Genesis 12:13–18 stress that even a man known for faith can fall into distrust, and that Pharaoh's rebuke exposes the wrong Abram has done.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

There is a useful parallel with the later departure of Abram's descendants from Egypt. Here a famine takes the family to Egypt, Pharaoh's household suffers plagues, and the family leaves with its possessions. In Exodus 12:29–36, judgment on Egypt is followed by Pharaoh sending Israel away, and the people depart with goods. The resemblance helps us recognize a pattern of God bringing His people out of danger; it does not make every detail of the two stories identical.

God's intervention also preserves the family through whom the promised blessing will continue. Galatians 3:8–14 shows the wider reach of that promise in Christ. The future blessing of the nations does not depend on pretending that Abram is consistently brave or honest. Genesis lets us see both his failure and God's continuing work.

What is our position?

Our Prayer and Faith study reads Abram's deception in Egypt as a failure to trust God under pressure. His attempt to protect himself endangers Sarai, and God's intervention, rather than the success of his scheme, brings her back. The study calls us to depend on God instead of using deception to secure the outcome we want.
