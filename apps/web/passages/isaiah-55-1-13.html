Passage

Isaiah 55:1-13

What does God offer to people who cannot pay?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters; and you without money, come, buy, and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without cost! 2 Why spend money on that which is not bread, and your labor on that which does not satisfy? Listen carefully to Me, and eat what is good, and your soul will delight in the richest of foods. 3 Incline your ear and come to Me; listen, so that your soul may live. I will make with you an everlasting covenant— My loving devotion promised to David. 4 Behold, I have made him a witness to the nations, a leader and commander of the peoples. 5 Surely you will summon a nation you do not know, and nations who do not know you will run to you. For the LORD your God, the Holy One of Israel, has bestowed glory on you.”

6 Seek the LORD while He may be found; call on Him while He is near. 7 Let the wicked man forsake his way and the unrighteous man his thoughts; let him return to the LORD, that He may have compassion, and to our God, for He will freely pardon. 8 “For My thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways My ways,” declares the LORD. 9 “For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so My ways are higher than your ways and My thoughts than your thoughts.

10 For just as rain and snow fall from heaven and do not return without watering the earth, making it bud and sprout, and providing seed to sow and food to eat, 11 so My word that proceeds from My mouth will not return to Me empty, but it will accomplish what I please, and it will prosper where I send it. 12 You will indeed go out with joy and be led forth in peace; the mountains and hills will burst into song before you, and all the trees of the field will clap their hands. 13 Instead of the thornbush, the cypress will grow, and instead of the brier, the myrtle will spring up; this will make a name for the LORD, an everlasting sign, never to be destroyed.”

What is the context?

The invitation is to thirsty people, including those without money. They are called to receive water, wine, milk and satisfying food without payment. The question about wasted money and labor exposes the pursuit of things that cannot give the life being offered.

The way to receive is to listen and come to the Lord. He promises an everlasting covenant and His loving devotion pledged to David. The horizon includes nations who did not know the one to whom they now run.

The invitation also calls for a response: seek the Lord, call on Him, leave wicked ways and unrighteous thoughts, and return to Him. Compassion and abundant pardon are offered. The declaration that His thoughts and ways are higher follows that promise of mercy.

Rain and snow supply the comparison for His word. They water the earth and produce seed and food; His word likewise accomplishes the purpose for which He sends it. The chapter closes with a joyful departure and a transformed landscape that becomes a lasting sign of His work.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read the food and drink as the blessings of salvation freely offered through Christ. The invitation contrasts God’s gift with the exhaustion of seeking lasting satisfaction elsewhere.

Both join the promise of pardon with repentance. Returning involves a change of direction and thought, while the scale of God’s mercy exceeds the limited forgiveness people often offer each other.

They understand the effective word as God bringing His saving promises to their intended result. The rain comparison therefore encourages confidence in what He has promised, rather than treating the passage as a guarantee for every plan a person attaches to a Bible verse.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acts 13:32–39 directly quotes the promised blessings of David while explaining the resurrection of Jesus. David died and his body decayed, but the One God raised did not see decay.

Paul then proclaims forgiveness of sins through Jesus and declares that everyone who believes is justified through Him. Isaiah’s covenant promise is thus connected to the risen Son of David and the forgiveness announced in His name.

This gives the invitation a clear place in God’s larger purpose: the dependable mercy He promised is proclaimed through the risen Christ, and people are called to receive what they could not purchase for themselves.
