Passage

Exodus 22:16-31

What did faithfulness to God look like in everyday life?

Berean Standard Bible

16 If a man seduces a virgin who is not pledged in marriage and sleeps with her, he must pay the full dowry for her to be his wife. 17 If her father absolutely refuses to give her to him, the man still must pay an amount comparable to the bridal price of a virgin.

18 You must not allow a sorceress to live. 19 Whoever lies with an animal must surely be put to death. 20 If anyone sacrifices to any god other than the LORD alone, he must be set apart for destruction.

21 You must not exploit or oppress a foreign resident, for you yourselves were foreigners in the land of Egypt. 22 You must not mistreat any widow or orphan. 23 If you do mistreat them, and they cry out to Me in distress, I will surely hear their cry. 24 My anger will be kindled, and I will kill you with the sword; then your wives will become widows and your children will be fatherless.

25 If you lend money to one of My people among you who is poor, you must not act as a creditor to him; you are not to charge him interest. 26 If you take your neighbor’s cloak as collateral, return it to him by sunset, 27 because his cloak is the only covering he has for his body. What else will he sleep in? And if he cries out to Me, I will hear, for I am compassionate.

28 You must not blaspheme God or curse the ruler of your people. 29 You must not hold back offerings from your granaries or vats. You are to give Me the firstborn of your sons. 30 You shall do likewise with your cattle and your sheep. Let them stay with their mothers for seven days, but on the eighth day you are to give them to Me. 31 You are to be My holy people. You must not eat the meat of a mauled animal found in the field; you are to throw it to the dogs.

What is the context?

These instructions bring together sexual conduct, worship, treatment of vulnerable people and the honor owed to God. A man who seduces an unbetrothed woman cannot simply walk away from the consequences. The marriage payment remains due even if her father refuses the marriage. Sorcery, sexual relations with an animal and sacrifice to other gods carry severe penalties under Israel’s covenant law.

Verse 20’s expression “set apart for destruction” comes from חָרַם, charam (khah-RAHM), a Hebrew verb for placing something under a ban or devoting it. In this case it means being devoted to death, not merely being excluded from a gathering (Brown–Driver–Briggs). The wording underlines how seriously the covenant treats worship of another god.

The same God who requires Israel’s exclusive worship forbids them to exploit a foreign resident, widow, orphan or poor borrower. Their own experience in Egypt must shape how they treat foreigners. A creditor must return a poor neighbor’s cloak before night because the person needs it for warmth. God hears the cry of the mistreated and warns the oppressor of judgment. His compassion is given as the reason for protecting the borrower.

The closing instructions require respect for God and rulers, prompt offerings and a distinct way of life. Giving God the firstborn sons must be read with Exodus 13:11–16, which requires their redemption. The instruction does not call for sacrificing children. The rules for firstborn livestock and meat from a mauled animal also belong to the people’s calling to be holy—set apart to God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Interpreters commonly recognize that devotion to God and justice toward other people belong together here. On lending, Wesley and Calvin understand the interest prohibition as protecting people who borrow out of need. Both distinguish that protection from a ban on every possible interest-bearing arrangement; neither allows profit to excuse exploiting a poor neighbor. These are their readings of the law’s continuing moral purpose, rather than a claim that every Christian tradition has given the same account of lending.

A smaller difference concerns the opening of verse 28. The BSB understands it as a prohibition of blaspheming God. The Catholic Bible’s note likewise reads it as dishonoring God’s authority. Wesley and Calvin take the word there as referring to judges or magistrates, alongside the ruler mentioned next. Both readings require honor for rightful authority; they differ over the first term’s immediate reference.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament takes up both the protection of vulnerable people and the call to honor others. James 1:26–27 joins care for widows and orphans with a life kept faithful to God. James 5:1–6 warns those who enrich themselves by withholding workers’ wages: the workers’ cries have reached the Lord. The God who heard Israel in Egypt still hears people harmed by those with power.

In Acts 23:1–5, Paul explicitly quotes this passage’s command about speaking of a ruler, even after being struck unjustly. 1 Peter 2:11–17 describes honorable conduct as part of God’s people’s witness among the nations. That honor does not make every human demand right: in Acts 5:27–32, the apostles obey God when authorities order them to stop speaking about Jesus. The larger purpose is a people whose loyalty to God shows in compassion, integrity and respect.
