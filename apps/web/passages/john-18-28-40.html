Passage

John 18:28-40

What kind of king stands before Pilate?

Berean Standard Bible

28 Then they led Jesus away from Caiaphas into the Praetorium. By now it was early morning, and the Jews did not enter the Praetorium, to avoid being defiled and unable to eat the Passover. 29 So Pilate went out to them and asked, “What accusation are you bringing against this man?” 30 “If He were not a criminal,” they replied, “we would not have handed Him over to you.” 31 “You take Him and judge Him by your own law,” Pilate told them. “We are not permitted to execute anyone,” the Jews replied. 32 This was to fulfill the word that Jesus had spoken to indicate the kind of death He was going to die.

33 Pilate went back into the Praetorium, summoned Jesus, and asked Him, “Are You the King of the Jews?” 34 “Are you saying this on your own,” Jesus asked, “or did others tell you about Me?” 35 “Am I a Jew?” Pilate replied. “Your own people and chief priests handed You over to me. What have You done?” 36 Jesus answered, “My kingdom is not of this world; if it were, My servants would fight to prevent My arrest by the Jews. But now My kingdom is not of this realm.” 37 “Then You are a king!” Pilate said. “You say that I am a king,” Jesus answered. “For this reason I was born and have come into the world, to testify to the truth. Everyone who belongs to the truth listens to My voice.”

38 “What is truth?” Pilate asked. And having said this, he went out again to the Jews and told them, “I find no basis for a charge against Him. 39 But it is your custom that I release to you one prisoner at the Passover. So then, do you want me to release to you the King of the Jews?” 40 “Not this man,” they shouted, “but Barabbas!” (Now Barabbas was an insurrectionist.)

What is the context?

The leaders bring Jesus from Caiaphas to Pilate’s headquarters early in the morning. They remain outside because of their concern about ritual defilement and eating the Passover. Pilate goes out to ask for the charge against Him. John records their statement that they are not permitted to execute anyone and connects the proceedings with Jesus’ prediction of the kind of death He would die.

Inside, Pilate asks whether Jesus is the King of the Jews. Jesus answers that His kingdom is not of this world and points to the fact that His servants are not fighting to prevent His arrest. His kingship does not depend on the means by which worldly rulers hold power.

Jesus speaks of coming into the world to testify to the truth and of those who belong to the truth listening to His voice. Pilate asks what truth is and then goes out again, declaring that he finds no basis for a charge against Jesus.

Pilate offers to release Him under the Passover custom, but the people calling for a release ask for Barabbas instead. The BSB identifies Barabbas as an insurrectionist. The episode places the man declared innocent beside a prisoner associated with the kind of violence Jesus has refused.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand Jesus to affirm His kingship while distinguishing it from a revolt for worldly power. Wesley on verses 36–37 emphasizes its spiritual character and witness to truth. Calvin on verse 36 likewise emphasizes its heavenly character and explicitly distinguishes not being of this world from having no presence in it. Their central point is shared; this passage should not be made to settle every later question about governments or force.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John explicitly connects the movement toward Roman execution with Jesus’ prediction. John 12:32–33 had connected His being lifted up with the manner of His death. His witness before Pilate is also remembered in 1 Timothy 6:13–16, where it becomes part of the call to faithful service while awaiting His appearing.

The kingship belongs to God’s larger purpose. Daniel 7:13–14 describes the Son of Man receiving lasting dominion over all peoples. Jesus standing before a governor is therefore not the whole measure of His authority.

The choice of Barabbas provides another meaningful contrast. Mark 15:6–15 explicitly records the guilty prisoner being released and Jesus being handed over. This is a narrative parallel to the exchange described in 2 Corinthians 5:18–21: the sinless Christ bears sin on behalf of others so that they may be reconciled to God. The parallel concerns undeserved release at His cost; it is not a claim that the text records Barabbas’s conversion.
