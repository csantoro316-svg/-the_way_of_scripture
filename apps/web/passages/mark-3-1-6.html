Passage

Mark 3:1-6

Doing Good on the Sabbath

Berean Standard Bible

1 Once again Jesus entered the synagogue, and a man with a withered hand was there. 2 In order to accuse Jesus, they were watching to see if He would heal on the Sabbath. 3 Then Jesus said to the man with the withered hand, “Stand up among us.”

4 And He asked them, “Which is lawful on the Sabbath: to do good or to do evil, to save life or to destroy it?” But they were silent. 5 Jesus looked around at them with anger and sorrow at their hardness of heart. Then He said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” So he stretched it out, and it was restored. 6 At this, the Pharisees went out and began plotting with the Herodians how they might kill Jesus.

What is the context?

The Sabbath dispute continues in a synagogue, where a man has a withered hand. Some people watch Jesus to see whether He will heal him so that they can accuse Him. Luke 6:6–11 identifies the observers as scribes and Pharisees and says Jesus knows their thoughts. Mark does not say they brought the man there as a trap.

Jesus brings the man before everyone and asks whether the Sabbath calls for doing good or evil, saving life or destroying it. His question makes the man's need central. In Matthew 12:9–14, He also asks whether they would rescue a sheep from a pit on the Sabbath, then points to the greater value of a person.

Their silence is followed by Jesus' anger and sorrow at their hardness of heart. The word πώρωσις, pōrōsis (POH-roh-sis), can describe stubbornness or dulled understanding; Thayer's lexicon specifically lists this verse. The problem is their resistance to what is good, rather than lack of information. His anger appears alongside grief over them.

Jesus tells the man to stretch out his hand, and it is restored. The Pharisees then leave and begin planning with the Herodians to kill Him. The ending sharpens His earlier question: He has done good and restored a man, while they turn toward taking a life.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see the healing as showing that care for a person belongs within the Sabbath's purpose. Calvin on Mark 3:5 and Wesley's note on the same verse also read Jesus' anger together with His grief, rather than as careless or selfish rage.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God's earlier call in Micah 6:8 joins justice, mercy, and humble life with Him. Jesus puts that concern into action here. Knowing religious requirements while refusing mercy misses what those requirements are meant to serve. James 4:17 likewise warns that knowing the good we should do and failing to do it is sin.

The plot also points ahead to the cost of Jesus' saving work. He will explain that He came to serve and give His life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:42–45). Acts 4:24–30 later places the rulers' conspiracy against Him within God's larger purpose, while John 10:17–18 makes clear that He lays down His life willingly. This early threat belongs to the road toward that self-giving; His death is more than His enemies' success.
