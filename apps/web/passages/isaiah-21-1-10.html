Passage

Isaiah 21:1-10

What does the watchman announce about Babylon?

Berean Standard Bible

1 This is the burden against the Desert by the Sea: Like whirlwinds sweeping through the Negev, an invader comes from the desert, from a land of terror. 2 A dire vision is declared to me: “The traitor still betrays, and the destroyer still destroys. Go up, O Elam! Lay siege, O Media! I will put an end to all her groaning.” 3 Therefore my body is filled with anguish. Pain grips me, like the pains of a woman in labor. I am bewildered to hear, I am dismayed to see. 4 My heart falters; fear makes me tremble. The twilight I desired has turned to horror. 5 They prepare a table, they lay out a carpet, they eat, they drink! Rise up, O princes, oil the shields!

6 For this is what the Lord says to me: “Go, post a lookout and have him report what he sees. 7 When he sees chariots with teams of horsemen, riders on donkeys, riders on camels, he must be alert, fully alert.” 8 Then the lookout shouted: “Day after day, my lord, I stand on the watchtower; night after night I stay at my post. 9 Look, here come the riders, horsemen in pairs.” And one answered, saying: “Fallen, fallen is Babylon! All the images of her gods lie shattered on the ground!” 10 O my people, crushed on the threshing floor, I tell you what I have heard from the LORD of Hosts, the God of Israel.

What is the context?

The message opens with a desert storm and a distressing vision. Elam and Media are summoned to advance, while the speaker is seized by anguish and fear. The name of the city under judgment is withheld until the watchman’s report.

A scene of eating and drinking is interrupted by a call to the princes to rise and oil their shields. The Lord then tells the prophet to post a lookout. The repeated attention to watching, by day and night, prepares for the announcement that follows.

The riders arrive, and the report is unmistakable: Babylon has fallen, and the images of its gods lie shattered. The city’s religious objects cannot preserve its power.

The closing words address a people crushed on the threshing floor. The prophet assures them that the message comes from the Lord, the God of Israel. The news of Babylon’s fall is therefore also a message to people who have suffered under oppression.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry, Wesley and Gill connect the vision with Babylon’s defeat through the Medes and Persians. The NABRE notes also link the interrupted feast with Daniel 5.

The final threshing-floor image is understood differently. Henry and Gill apply it to God’s afflicted people, receiving assurance of deliverance. Wesley considers Babylon itself the people being threshed, while understanding the report as a message given for Israel’s benefit.

Henry and Gill also connect Babylon’s fall with the later Babylon of Revelation. This extends the passage’s significance beyond one empire without requiring every detail of the first vision to have the same setting as the later book.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 18:2–8 takes up the announcement of Babylon’s fall. It describes a power marked by corrupt wealth and influence and calls God’s people away from sharing in its sins.

The connection places the watchman’s announcement within Scripture’s larger account of God judging the powers that oppress and corrupt. The same Lord who exposes the failure of Babylon’s idols also calls His people to faithfulness while they await His judgment.
