Passage

Acts 7:9-16

What does Joseph’s rejection show about God’s purposes?

Berean Standard Bible

9 Because the patriarchs were jealous of Joseph, they sold him as a slave into Egypt. But God was with him 10 and rescued him from all his troubles. He granted Joseph favor and wisdom in the sight of Pharaoh king of Egypt, who appointed him ruler over Egypt and all his household.

11 Then famine and great suffering swept across Egypt and Canaan, and our fathers could not find food. 12 When Jacob heard that there was grain in Egypt, he sent our fathers on their first visit. 13 On their second visit, Joseph revealed his identity to his brothers, and his family became known to Pharaoh. 14 Then Joseph sent for his father Jacob and all his relatives, seventy-five in all. 15 So Jacob went down to Egypt, where he and our fathers died. 16 Their bones were carried back to Shechem and placed in the tomb that Abraham had bought from the sons of Hamor at Shechem for a price he paid in silver.

What is the context?

Stephen turns from the patriarchs as recipients of God’s promise to their jealousy of Joseph. They sell their brother into Egypt, yet God is with him. Rejection by his own family does not prevent God from giving him favor, wisdom and responsibility.

The famine then brings the brothers to the man they had rejected. Joseph makes himself known on their second visit and sends for Jacob and the family. God’s work outside the land preserves the family through which His promises will continue.

The account closes this part of the story with the move to Egypt and a reference to the family’s later burial in the land. Some details invite comparison with Genesis and Joshua, especially the number of people and the burial purchases.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand Joseph’s story here as an example of God working through a person his own people first reject. The repeated contrast between rejection and God’s choice prepares for Stephen’s later charge concerning Jesus.

The family count is seventy-five in Acts, while Genesis 46:27 in the BSB gives seventy. Seventy-five also appears in the Greek Genesis text shown in the Apostolic Bible Polyglot, Genesis 46:27. Stephen’s number is therefore attested in the Greek form of the earlier account; readers should notice which text they are comparing.

The burial reference raises a different question. Genesis 23:16–20 describes Abraham buying the burial site near Hebron from Ephron, and Genesis 50:12–13 places Jacob’s burial there. Joshua 24:32 places Joseph’s bones at Shechem, on land Jacob bought from Hamor’s sons. Acts 7:16 instead names Abraham in its reference to the Shechem purchase.

Wesley on verse 16 proposes that Stephen compresses the two burial accounts into one brief summary. Calvin on verse 16 instead thinks the name Abraham should read Jacob. These are proposed explanations of the difficulty; the places and purchasers in the earlier accounts should still be compared carefully.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Genesis 50:20 gives Joseph’s own explanation of the family’s history: their evil intent was met by God’s purpose to preserve many lives. Stephen’s account highlights that pattern of human rejection within a larger saving purpose.

The pattern prepares for the speech’s conclusion in Acts 7:52, where Stephen names the betrayal of the Righteous One. Joseph’s rejection and later role in preserving his people provide a meaningful comparison with Jesus. The passage does not require assigning a separate prediction to every visit or detail of Joseph’s story.
