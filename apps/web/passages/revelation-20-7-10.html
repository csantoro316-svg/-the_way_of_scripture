Passage

Revelation 20:7-10

How does Satan’s final rebellion end?

Berean Standard Bible

7 When the thousand years are complete, Satan will be released from his prison, 8 and will go out to deceive the nations in the four corners of the earth—Gog and Magog—to assemble them for battle. Their number is like the sand of the seashore. 9 And they marched across the broad expanse of the earth and surrounded the camp of the saints and the beloved city. But fire came down from heaven and consumed them.

10 And the devil who had deceived them was thrown into the lake of fire and sulfur, into which the beast and the false prophet had already been thrown. There they will be tormented day and night forever and ever.

What is the context?

The release announced in verse 3 now takes place. Satan again deceives the nations, gathering a vast force from the four corners of the earth. John calls them Gog and Magog, names drawn from Ezekiel’s account of an attack on God’s people.

The army surrounds the camp of the saints and the beloved city. Fire comes down from heaven and consumes the attackers. As in chapter 19, the gathered opposition does not become a close contest between equal powers. The vision moves swiftly to its defeat.

The devil is then thrown into the lake of fire and sulfur, where the beast and false prophet have already been thrown. John describes their torment as day and night forever and ever. The temporary imprisonment and release are over; this scene gives no further release for the deceiver.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The final defeat of Satan is shared ground, while the setting depends on the reading of the thousand years. Walvoord places the rebellion after Christ’s future earthly reign. The Catholic USCCB notes describe a symbolic final attack at the end of the period between Christ’s resurrection and the end of the world.

The connection with Ezekiel is also read differently. Wesley understands John as referring back to the war described in Ezekiel 38–39. Walvoord distinguishes the two battles while recognizing the repeated names and pattern of attack. The shared imagery establishes a connection; whether both accounts describe one event is a further question.

There is a difference over the lasting punishment. Wesley reads the torment as continuing without interruption, and the Reformed Westminster Confession 33.2 teaches enduring conscious punishment of the wicked. The Seventh-day Adventist Church’s Belief 27 and its accompanying explanation teach permanent destruction of Satan and the wicked, understanding the punishment’s permanence as the meaning of its lasting forever. This differs from reading the torment itself as unending. The passage’s wording is retained in full above.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The attack recalls Ezekiel 38:1–9 and 14–23, where Gog leads a great force against God’s people and God intervenes so that the nations will know Him. Revelation expands the gathering to the nations in the four corners of the earth. The connection places this final assault within the Bible’s account of God preserving His people and making His authority known.

The description of the beloved city also recalls God’s love for Zion in Psalm 78:67–69. The city is surrounded, yet the surrounding army does not determine its future.

Paul’s promise in Romans 16:20 looks toward God crushing Satan under the feet of His people. Revelation gives a final scene of the deceiver’s defeat. That hope directs attention to God’s faithfulness after a long history of deception and opposition.
