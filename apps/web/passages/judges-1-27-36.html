Passage

Judges 1:27-36

What happens when the tribes settle for unfinished obedience?

Berean Standard Bible

27 At that time Manasseh failed to drive out the inhabitants of Beth-shean, Taanach, Dor, Ibleam, Megiddo, and their villages; for the Canaanites were determined to dwell in that land. 28 When Israel became stronger, they pressed the Canaanites into forced labor, but they never drove them out completely. 29 Ephraim also failed to drive out the Canaanites living in Gezer; so the Canaanites continued to dwell among them in Gezer. 30 Zebulun failed to drive out the inhabitants of Kitron and Nahalol; so the Canaanites lived among them and served as forced laborers.

31 Asher failed to drive out the inhabitants of Acco, Sidon, Ahlab, Achzib, Helbah, Aphik, and Rehob. 32 So the Asherites lived among the Canaanite inhabitants of the land, because they did not drive them out. 33 Naphtali failed to drive out the inhabitants of Beth-shemesh and Beth-anath. So the Naphtalites also lived among the Canaanite inhabitants of the land, but the inhabitants of Beth-shemesh and Beth-anath served them as forced laborers.

34 The Amorites forced the Danites into the hill country and did not allow them to come down into the plain. 35 And the Amorites were determined to dwell in Mount Heres, Aijalon, and Shaalbim. But when the house of Joseph grew in strength, they pressed the Amorites into forced labor. 36 And the border of the Amorites extended from the Ascent of Akrabbim to Sela and beyond.

What is the context?

The closing section repeats a sobering pattern: the tribes do not drive out the remaining inhabitants. In some areas Israel becomes strong enough to impose forced labor yet leaves the people in place. In others, Israelite groups live among the Canaanites, and the Danites are pressed back into the hill country.

The Hebrew term מַס, mas (MAHS), concerns forced labor or a body of laborers. Brown–Driver–Briggs explicitly includes these verses and the labor bands imposed on Israel in Egypt. The passage describes required work, not merely a friendly arrangement to live together.

This is not explained only by a lack of strength. Verse 28 says Israel became stronger but still did not drive the inhabitants out. The next chapter gives the covenant issue plainly: the people have failed to obey God’s voice, and the surrounding gods will become a snare (Judges 2:1–5).

There is also a distinction between the gradual conquest God had allowed and Israel’s disobedience. Deuteronomy 7:20–23 speaks of the nations being driven out little by little. Judges describes the people accepting arrangements that leave God’s instructions unfulfilled, and then shows the consequences for their worship.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the pattern as a mixture of fear, unwillingness to finish the work, and the attraction of benefiting from others’ labor. Wesley also connects failure to take possession with distrust of God. These explanations see the central problem as disobedience and compromised worship, rather than superior technology or numbers alone.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The wider story asks for more than people who have received a gift but continue to turn away from the Giver. Titus 2:11–14 describes Christ giving Himself to redeem a people from lawlessness, purify them as His own, and make them eager for good works. Grace trains His people to live faithfully.

Jesus’ commission also sets out the New Testament mission: make disciples of all nations and teach them to obey Him (Matthew 28:18–20). The Bible’s story moves toward a people drawn from the nations who live under His rule. The failures in Judges help us see the need for the rescue and changed life that Christ brings.
