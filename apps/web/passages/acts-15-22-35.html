Passage

Acts 15:22-35

How does the council’s letter bring clarity and encouragement?

Berean Standard Bible

22 Then the apostles and elders, with the whole church, decided to select men from among them to send to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas. They chose Judas called Barsabbas and Silas, two leaders among the brothers, 23 and sent them with this letter: The apostles and the elders, your brothers, To the brothers among the Gentiles in Antioch, Syria, and Cilicia: Greetings. 24 It has come to our attention that some went out from us without our authorization and unsettled you, troubling your minds by what they said. 25 So we all agreed to choose men to send to you along with our beloved Barnabas and Paul, 26 men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. 27 Therefore we are sending Judas and Silas to tell you in person the same things we are writing.

28 It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us not to burden you with anything beyond these essential requirements: 29 You must abstain from food sacrificed to idols, from blood, from the meat of strangled animals, and from sexual immorality. You will do well to avoid these things. Farewell.

30 So the men were sent off and went down to Antioch, where they assembled the congregation and delivered the letter. 31 When the people read it, they rejoiced at its encouraging message. 32 Judas and Silas, who themselves were prophets, said much to encourage and strengthen the brothers. 33 After spending some time there, they were sent off by the brothers in peace to return to those who had sent them. 35 But Paul and Barnabas remained at Antioch, along with many others, teaching and preaching the word of the Lord.

What is the context?

The apostles and elders, with the whole church, choose Judas Barsabbas and Silas to accompany Barnabas and Paul. Their letter addresses Gentile believers in Antioch, Syria and Cilicia. It explains that the unsettling teachers had gone out without the senders’ authorization.

The letter honors Barnabas and Paul as people who have risked their lives for Jesus. Judas and Silas will confirm its message in person. The decision is attributed to the Holy Spirit and the gathered leaders, and the four requirements are stated without imposing circumcision.

When the letter is read in Antioch, the believers rejoice at its encouragement. Judas and Silas also speak at length to strengthen them. The visitors are sent back in peace, while Paul and Barnabas remain with others to teach and preach.

The BSB’s main text moves from verse 33 to verse 35. Its footnote records a reading about Silas remaining that some manuscripts include as verse 34. That wording is not part of the main quotation on this page.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that the letter rejects the demand for circumcision and calls the Gentile believers to a faithful life. They differ over whether the food restrictions are continuing rules or directions especially suited to that setting.

The Catholic Church’s Council of Florence, session 11, explains the restrictions on sacrificial food, blood and strangled meat as serving fellowship between Jews and Gentiles in that period, with their force ending when the reason for them ceased. Calvin on verses 19–20 likewise treats the food restrictions as measures to avoid offense and protect fellowship.

Wesley on verse 29 takes the prohibition on eating blood as continuing, connecting it with the command to Noah. The Orthodox canonical tradition also retains a prohibition: the Council in Trullo, canon 67, forbids eating animal blood. The Orthodox Church in America’s account of that council explains its recognized place in Orthodox church law.

The food question should be distinguished from permission to commit sexual immorality. 1 Corinthians 6:18–20 separately commands believers to flee it. The different readings of the food instructions do not turn that command into a temporary social courtesy.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ephesians 4:1–6 calls believers to preserve the Spirit’s unity in peace, grounded in one body and one Lord. The council’s decision takes practical form through a letter, trusted messengers, teaching and the removal of an unauthorized burden.

That unity concerns people who are now addressed as brothers across the Jewish and Gentile boundary. The response in Antioch is joy and encouragement: clarity about the gospel helps the community continue its life and witness together.

What is our position?

Who Shepherds the Church? points to the whole church sharing in the decision in verse 22 and to the apostles and elders sending the letter in verse 23. It reads these as shared responsibility among recognized leaders and the community. The study therefore describes James’s contribution as part of that common work, rather than the whole decision being his solitary act.
