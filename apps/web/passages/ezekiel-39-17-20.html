Passage

Ezekiel 39:17-20

Why are birds and wild animals invited to a feast?

Berean Standard Bible

17 And as for you, son of man, this is what the Lord GOD says: Call out to every kind of bird and to every beast of the field: ‘Assemble and come together from all around to the sacrificial feast that I am preparing for you, a great feast on the mountains of Israel. There you will eat flesh and drink blood. 18 You will eat the flesh of the mighty and drink the blood of the princes of the earth as though they were rams, lambs, goats, and bulls—all the fattened animals of Bashan. 19 At the sacrifice I am preparing, you will eat fat until you are gorged and drink blood until you are drunk. 20 And at My table you will eat your fill of horses and riders, of mighty men and warriors of every kind,’ declares the Lord GOD.

What is the context?

Ezekiel is told to summon birds and wild animals to a great feast on Israel’s mountains. The food is the fallen army, including its mighty men, princes, horses and riders.

The description uses the language of sacrifice and a table prepared by the Lord. Those who came with power and high rank are compared to animals supplied for a feast. Their status cannot protect them from judgment.

This is a summons to scavenging creatures within a prophecy of defeat. It is not an instruction for God’s people to eat human flesh or drink blood. The disturbing picture strips the invading forces of the glory they sought.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the feast as a picture of God’s judgment on the ambitious and violent. Wesley likewise treats the invitation as a picture whose meaning is the death of the warriors and princes.

The sacrificial language portrays their defeat under God’s judgment. It does not turn their deaths into an offering that forgives the sins of other people.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A close parallel appears in Revelation 19:17–21. Birds are summoned to eat the flesh of kings, commanders, mighty men, horses and riders as the forces gathered against Christ are defeated.

The shared picture points to the collapse of earthly power before God. Revelation makes Christ’s victory central: the beast and false prophet are captured, and the armies cannot prevail against the One they oppose. The parallel is strong without requiring every detail in Ezekiel and Revelation to be placed on one agreed timetable.
