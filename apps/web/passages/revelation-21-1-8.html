Passage

Revelation 21:1-8

What will life with God be like?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and earth had passed away, and the sea was no more. 2 I saw the holy city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. 3 And I heard a loud voice from the throne saying: “Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man, and He will dwell with them. They will be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God. 4 ‘He will wipe away every tear from their eyes,’ and there will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the former things have passed away.”

5 And the One seated on the throne said, “Behold, I make all things new.” Then He said, “Write this down, for these words are faithful and true.” 6 And He told me, “It is done! I am the Alpha and the Omega, the Beginning and the End. To the thirsty I will give freely from the spring of the water of life. 7 The one who overcomes will inherit all things, and I will be his God, and he will be My son. 8 But to the cowardly and unbelieving and abominable and murderers and sexually immoral and sorcerers and idolaters and all liars, their place will be in the lake that burns with fire and sulfur. This is the second death.”

What is the context?

After the judgment, John sees a new heaven and a new earth. The former heaven and earth have passed away, and the sea is no more. The holy city, the new Jerusalem, comes down from God, prepared like a bride for her husband.

The voice from the throne explains the heart of the scene: God will dwell with His people. He wipes away their tears, and death, mourning, crying and pain will be no more. The promise reaches the griefs that have marked their lives throughout the book.

The One on the throne declares that He makes all things new and tells John to write because His words are faithful and true. He identifies Himself as the Alpha and Omega, the Beginning and the End. He gives the water of life freely to the thirsty, and promises an inheritance and the relationship of a son to the one who overcomes.

The closing warning names the second death as the fate of the cowardly, unbelieving and those characterized by the evils listed. The comfort and invitation stand alongside a serious distinction between life with God and the judgment described in the previous chapter.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Walvoord understand this as the lasting new creation after the final judgment. Gill places the chapter’s vision within the thousand-year reign instead. Their difference concerns where John’s description belongs in the sequence of visions.

The Catholic Catechism 1042–1048 describes the universe as renewed and transformed, with humanity and creation brought to their fulfillment in Christ. Walvoord emphasizes a new creation replacing the former heaven and earth. Both readings affirm a lasting home with God, while explaining its relationship to the present world differently.

The sea receives different emphases. Walvoord reads its absence as a feature of the new world. The USCCB notes connect its disappearance with the removal of the dragon’s home. These explanations draw attention respectively to the world John sees and to the meaning of the imagery within Revelation.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise of God dwelling among His people recalls Ezekiel 37:26–28, where His dwelling, His people and His enduring covenant belong together. Revelation brings that relationship to the center of the new creation.

Isaiah 25:6–9 joins the swallowing up of death with God wiping tears from every face. Isaiah 65:17–19 speaks of new heavens and a new earth, Jerusalem’s joy and an end to weeping. John’s vision draws these promises together into a picture of God’s faithful care.

The freely given water also recalls Isaiah 55:1–3 and Jesus’ offer of living water in John 4:10–14. Life with God is presented as His gift to the thirsty, with an invitation to receive what He gives.
