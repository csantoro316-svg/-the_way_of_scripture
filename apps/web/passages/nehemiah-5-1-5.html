Passage

Nehemiah 5:1-5

Why are families crying out against their own countrymen?

Berean Standard Bible

1 About that time there was a great outcry from the people and their wives against their fellow Jews. 2 Some were saying, “We and our sons and daughters are numerous. We must get grain in order to eat and stay alive.” 3 Others were saying, “We are mortgaging our fields, our vineyards, and our homes to get grain during the famine.” 4 Still others were saying, “We have borrowed money to pay the king’s tax on our fields and vineyards. 5 We and our children are just like our countrymen and their children, yet we are subjecting our sons and daughters to slavery. Some of our daughters are already enslaved, but we are powerless to redeem them because our fields and vineyards belong to others.”

What is the context?

The account turns from threats outside the city to an outcry within the community. Some families need grain to feed their many children. Others mortgage fields, vineyards and homes during famine, while others borrow to pay the king’s taxes.

The consequences reach the children: sons and daughters are being brought into slavery, and families cannot redeem them because their land is already in other hands. The complaint stresses that these are fellow people and fellow children, not lives of lesser worth.

The Law provides the relevant background. Exodus 22:25–27 protects poor borrowers, while Leviticus 25:35–43 calls for support of a destitute countryman and forbids harsh treatment of those whom God brought out of Egypt.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes compassion for the oppressed and the disgrace of exploiting fellow people. Gill reads the complaints through debt, famine and the loss of the means to recover the children. Their shared membership in the people makes the hardship a concern for the whole community.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The Law connects care for the poor with God’s own redemption: those He brought out of Egypt are not to be treated harshly (Leviticus 25:35–43). Freedom received from God is meant to shape life together.

In the New Testament, Galatians 5:13–15 joins freedom with serving one another in love. 1 John 3:16–18 grounds that love in Jesus laying down His life and asks how love can remain where a person with resources closes his heart to a brother in need. The larger purpose is a people whose practical treatment of one another reflects the God who has loved and redeemed them.
