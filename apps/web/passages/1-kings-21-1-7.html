Passage

1 Kings 21:1-7

Why does Naboth refuse Ahab’s offer for his vineyard?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Some time after these events, Naboth the Jezreelite owned a vineyard in Jezreel next to the palace of Ahab king of Samaria. 2 So Ahab said to Naboth, “Give me your vineyard to use as a vegetable garden, since it is next to my palace. I will give you a better vineyard in its place—or if you prefer, I will give you its value in silver.” 3 But Naboth replied, “The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers.” 4 So Ahab went to his palace, sullen and angry because Naboth the Jezreelite had told him, “I will not give you the inheritance of my fathers.” He lay down on his bed, turned his face away, and refused to eat.

5 Soon his wife Jezebel came in and asked, “Why are you so sullen that you refuse to eat?” 6 Ahab answered, “Because I spoke to Naboth the Jezreelite and told him, ‘Give me your vineyard for silver, or if you wish, I will give you another vineyard in its place.’ And he replied, ‘I will not give you my vineyard!’” 7 But his wife Jezebel said to him, “Do you not reign over Israel? Get up, eat some food, and be cheerful, for I will get you the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite.”

What is the context?

Naboth owns a vineyard beside Ahab’s palace in Jezreel. The king wants it for a vegetable garden and offers either a better vineyard or payment in silver.

Naboth refuses in the LORD’s name because the vineyard is his ancestral inheritance. The background is the concern for family land in Leviticus 25:23–28 and Numbers 36:7–9: the land belongs to God, and Israel’s inherited holdings are not simply possessions to transfer without regard to that gift.

Ahab returns home sullen and angry, lies down and refuses food. Jezebel responds by questioning whether he really rules Israel and promises to obtain the vineyard for him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill understand Naboth’s refusal as concern for God’s law and his family’s inheritance. They recognize that temporary sale could occur under the law, while a transfer into royal possession could put the family’s recovery of the land in doubt.

Henry focuses on Ahab’s discontent: the resources of a kingdom do not satisfy him while this one desired property remains unavailable. The conflict begins with the king’s unwillingness to accept a limit.
