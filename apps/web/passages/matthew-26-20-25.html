Passage

Matthew 26:20-25

One at the Table Will Betray Him

Berean Standard Bible

20 When evening came, Jesus was reclining with the twelve disciples. 21 And while they were eating, He said to them, “Truly I tell you, one of you will betray Me.” 22 They were deeply grieved and began to ask Him one after another, “Surely not I, Lord?”

23 Jesus answered, “The one who has dipped his hand into the bowl with Me will betray Me. 24 The Son of Man will go just as it is written about Him, but woe to that man by whom He is betrayed. It would be better for him if he had not been born.” 25 Then Judas, who would betray Him, said, “Surely not I, Rabbi?” Jesus answered, “You have said it yourself.”

What is the context?

Jesus reclines at the meal with the Twelve and announces that one of them will betray Him. The disciples are deeply grieved and each asks whether he could be the one. The betrayal is not coming only from an openly hostile group outside; it reaches into the company sharing His table.

Jesus identifies His betrayer as one who has dipped his hand into the bowl with Him. He then holds together two truths: the Son of Man will go as Scripture has said, and the man who betrays Him bears terrible responsibility. God's purpose does not make betrayal an innocent act.

Judas also asks whether Jesus means him, addressing Him as Rabbi. Jesus answers that Judas has said it himself. Matthew has already told the reader about the agreement for thirty pieces of silver, so his question is heard against that knowledge.

John 13:18–30 gives further details: the disciple beside Jesus asks who it is, Jesus gives Judas a dipped morsel, and Judas goes out into the night. John also says the others do not understand why Jesus has told him to act quickly. Matthew's shorter account does not supply a complete seating plan or describe Judas' departure.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see the shared bowl as making the betrayal especially grievous, and Jesus' warning as affirming Judas' responsibility despite Scripture's fulfillment. Wesley on 26:24–25 and Walvoord on 26:20–25 both retain that distinction between God's purpose and the betrayer's wrongdoing.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John explicitly connects the shared meal and betrayal with Scripture. Psalm 41:9 describes the pain of betrayal by a trusted companion who shared bread. In John 13:18–20, Jesus applies that passage to what is happening and says He is telling His disciples beforehand so that they will believe when it happens. The betrayal will wound them, but it does not mean their Lord has been taken by surprise.

The same account moves from betrayal to the disciples' continuing mission: receiving the person Jesus sends is receiving Him and the One who sent Him. Human treachery does not bring God's work to an end. The meal that reveals betrayal also leads toward the self-giving through which Jesus will bring forgiveness.
