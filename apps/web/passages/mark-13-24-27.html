Passage

Mark 13:24-27

What will the Son of Man do?

Berean Standard Bible

24 But in those days, after that tribulation: ‘The sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light; 25 the stars will fall from the sky, and the powers of the heavens will be shaken.’ 26 At that time they will see the Son of Man coming in the clouds with great power and glory. 27 And He will send out the angels to gather His elect from the four winds, from the ends of the earth to the ends of heaven.

What is the context?

After warning about false messiahs and misleading signs, Jesus describes the Son of Man coming with power and glory. He places this scene after the tribulation just described. The focus moves from frightening claims on earth to His own authority and the gathering of His people.

The darkened sun and moon and the falling stars recall the prophets’ descriptions of God’s judgment. Isaiah uses such language when announcing judgment on Babylon and on the nations. These scriptural connections help us hear the gravity of the scene without turning the passage into a scientific explanation of how the heavens will change. (Isaiah 13:9–13; Isaiah 34:1–4)

The Son of Man coming with the clouds recalls Daniel’s vision of One receiving lasting dominion from God. Jesus then sends angels to gather His elect from every direction. The scene emphasizes His authority and their being gathered to Him; Mark does not describe the fate of every other person in these four verses. (Daniel 7:9–14)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians broadly read this as an announcement of Christ’s coming in divine power and the gathering of His people. The US Catholic bishops’ note links the Son of Man with Daniel’s heavenly figure; Dallas Theological Seminary describes Christ’s glorious return after the tribulation within its expectation of a future reign on earth. The timing and arrangement of these events differ among readers, as the surrounding passage discussions explain. (USCCB, note on Mark 13:26; Dallas Theological Seminary, Article XX)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The gathering belongs to a much older promise of God restoring His scattered people. Isaiah pictures Israelites gathered one by one from distant places to worship the Lord. Jesus’ words echo that hope on a sweeping scale, with His messengers gathering from the ends of earth and heaven. The connection is the promised gathering; it need not make every detail of Isaiah and Mark identical. (Isaiah 27:12–13)

Paul sets Christ’s coming within God’s purpose to defeat every enemy, including death. Those who belong to Christ are raised, and His reign brings the destruction of hostile powers. The hope is therefore larger than escape from a crisis: God’s victorious king brings His people into the life secured by His resurrection. (1 Corinthians 15:20–28)
