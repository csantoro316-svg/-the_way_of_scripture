Passage

Isaiah 22:15-25

What does the key of David say about entrusted authority?

Berean Standard Bible

15 This is what the Lord GOD of Hosts says: “Go, say to Shebna, the steward in charge of the palace: 16 What are you doing here, and who authorized you to carve out a tomb for yourself here—to chisel your tomb in the height and cut your resting place in the rock? 17 Look, O mighty man! The LORD is about to shake you violently. He will take hold of you, 18 roll you into a ball, and sling you into a wide land. There you will die, and there your glorious chariots will remain—a disgrace to the house of your master. 19 I will remove you from office, and you will be ousted from your position.

20 On that day I will summon My servant, Eliakim son of Hilkiah. 21 I will clothe him with your robe and tie your sash around him. I will put your authority in his hand, and he will be a father to the dwellers of Jerusalem and to the house of Judah. 22 I will place on his shoulder the key to the house of David. What he opens no one can shut, and what he shuts no one can open.

23 I will drive him like a peg into a firm place, and he will be a throne of glory for the house of his father. 24 So they will hang on him all the glory of his father’s house: the descendants and the offshoots—all the lesser vessels, from bowls to every kind of jar. 25 In that day, declares the LORD of Hosts, the peg driven into a firm place will give way; it will be sheared off and fall, and the load upon it will be cut down.” Indeed, the LORD has spoken.

What is the context?

The Lord sends the prophet to Shebna, the steward in charge of the palace. The rebuke challenges the grand tomb he is cutting for himself. Instead of securing his place in Jerusalem, he will be removed from office and die in a distant land.

Eliakim son of Hilkiah is appointed in his place. The robe, sash and authority pass to him, and he is to care for Jerusalem and Judah as a father. The key of David’s house expresses authority to open and shut.

Eliakim is then pictured as a peg fixed firmly enough to carry his family’s honor and dependents. But the final verse announces that a firmly fixed peg will fall, taking its load with it. Readers differ over which official is in view at that closing point.

Second Kings 18:17–18 later names Eliakim as palace administrator and Shebnah as scribe among the officials who meet the Assyrian delegation. It confirms the change of roles without giving a full account of Shebna’s later fate.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry, Wesley and Gill understand the key as entrusted authority and see Eliakim’s appointment as a contrast to Shebna’s pride. For the final fallen peg, Wesley and Gill return to Shebna. The Cambridge commentary instead reads it as the eventual fall of Eliakim’s family’s position.

The key also has a place in differing Christian accounts of church authority. A Catholic reading is illustrated by Benedict XVI’s homily of 29 June 2012, which connects Eliakim’s key with the keys promised to Peter in Matthew 16. The Catholic Church teaches that Peter’s pastoral office continues in the bishops under the Pope’s primacy (Catechism 881–882).

The Reformed Westminster Confession 30.1–2 places the keys of the kingdom in the hands of church officers; its chapter 25.6 rejects papal headship. The 1689 Baptist Confession 26.4 likewise affirms Christ’s supreme authority and rejects papal headship. Those confessions state their teaching about the church; they do not give a separate detailed explanation of every verse in Isaiah 22.

These differences concern the later application of entrusted authority. The direct New Testament use of the key of David for Christ himself appears in Revelation 3:7.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 3:7–13 directly places Isaiah’s language on Jesus’ lips: He holds the key of David, and no one can reverse His opening and shutting. He addresses a church with little strength that has kept His word.

The connection carries the image from an appointed royal steward to the Lord who governs access in His kingdom. The church’s security rests in His authority, rather than its own power.

The same message promises a lasting place in God’s temple to the one who overcomes. Isaiah’s picture of entrusted authority reaches forward, through Revelation’s own use of it, to Christ’s authority to keep and welcome His people.
