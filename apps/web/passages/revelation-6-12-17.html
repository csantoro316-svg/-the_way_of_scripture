Passage

Revelation 6:12-17

Who can stand when the day of judgment comes?

Berean Standard Bible

12 And I watched as the Lamb opened the sixth seal, and there was a great earthquake, and the sun became black like sackcloth of goat hair, and the whole moon turned blood red, 13 and the stars of the sky fell to the earth like unripe figs dropping from a tree shaken by a great wind. 14 The sky receded like a scroll being rolled up, and every mountain and island was moved from its place.

15 Then the kings of the earth, the nobles, the commanders, the rich, the mighty, and every slave and free man hid in the caves and among the rocks of the mountains. 16 And they said to the mountains and the rocks, “Fall on us and hide us from the face of the One seated on the throne, and from the wrath of the Lamb. 17 For the great day of Their wrath has come, and who is able to withstand it?”

What is the context?

The sixth seal brings a great earthquake and a vision of upheaval in the heavens and on earth. The sun becomes black, the moon turns blood red, stars fall, the sky recedes like a scroll, and mountains and islands are moved. The imagery strips away the things people normally regard as stable.

The language has close biblical precedents. Isaiah 34:1–4 pictures judgment with the skies rolled up and the stars falling like leaves. Hosea 10:8 pictures people calling on mountains and hills to cover them. John’s scene draws these images into the vision of the Lamb opening the seals.

People from every rank seek shelter: rulers and commanders, wealthy and powerful people, slaves and free people. Their differences in status do not remove their fear. They ask to be hidden from the One seated on the throne and from the Lamb’s wrath.

The chapter ends with their question about who can withstand the great day. Chapter 7 follows with the sealing of God’s servants and a multitude standing before His throne. That next scene matters when reading the question: this chapter is not the vision’s final word about those who belong to Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

There is a substantial difference over how the upheaval is fulfilled. Gill reads the imagery chiefly as the overthrow of pagan rule and worship in the Roman Empire. Wesley understands it as a vision of judgment shown to the wicked dead. Walvoord favors future physical disturbances in heaven and on earth. The USCCB notes emphasize prophetic pictures of the cosmic upheaval attending divine judgment. These readings share the seriousness of judgment while differing about the events pictured.

The text’s own comparisons—sackcloth, falling figs and a rolled scroll—should remain visible in each explanation. It does not provide a scientific account of every event or identify a particular modern eclipse. Its stated focus is the fear of facing God and the Lamb, not a calculation that enables readers to date the end.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus takes up the cry for the mountains to fall in Luke 23:27–31, warning the women of Jerusalem about judgment to come. Revelation uses that same biblical language to show the failure of every earthly refuge before God.

The Lamb praised for His redeeming death in chapter 5 is also named here in connection with judgment. The vision holds those truths together. God’s purpose includes both rescue through Christ and the reckoning with evil that His people have asked Him to bring. The question of who can stand leads into the following chapter’s account of His servants and their hope.
