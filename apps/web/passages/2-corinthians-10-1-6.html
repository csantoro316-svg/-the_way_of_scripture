Passage

2 Corinthians 10:1-6

What does it mean to take every thought captive?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now by the mildness and gentleness of Christ, I appeal to you—I, Paul, who am humble when face to face with you, but bold when away. 2 I beg you that when I come I may not need to be as bold as I expect toward those who presume that we live according to the flesh.

3 For though we live in the flesh, we do not wage war according to the flesh. 4 The weapons of our warfare are not the weapons of the flesh. Instead, they have divine power to demolish strongholds. 5 We demolish arguments and every presumption set up against the knowledge of God; and we take captive every thought to make it obedient to Christ. 6 And we will be ready to punish every act of disobedience, as soon as your obedience is complete.

What is the context?

Paul turns from the collection to challenges against his ministry. Some people regard him as unimpressive in person and bold only from a distance. He appeals with Christ’s mildness and gentleness, asking them to respond before his arrival makes a firmer response necessary.

He acknowledges ordinary human life while explaining that his ministry draws on God’s power. The warfare picture describes a struggle against proud reasoning that stands against the knowledge of God. Paul names the strongholds he means: arguments and presumptions that resist that knowledge.

Taking every thought captive belongs first to this account of Paul and his fellow workers bringing opposition into obedience to Christ. The goal is that people’s thinking and allegiance come under Christ’s rule. Reading it in that setting helps us see why Paul speaks of strongholds being demolished and obedience replacing resistance.

The paragraph also contains a warning about disobedience. Paul is ready to act once the Corinthians’ obedience is complete. He begins with an appeal, giving them opportunity to respond, while making clear that persistent resistance will be addressed.

There is a personal application in allowing Christ’s truth to shape our thinking. That application grows from Paul’s stated goal of obedience to Him, with the gospel guiding what we accept and how we live.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the warfare as spiritual and the victory as bringing resistance to God’s truth into obedience to Christ. Wesley’s notes on verses 3–5 emphasize spiritual means and the mind yielding to Christ; the USCCB notes on verses 1–6 keep the imagery within Paul’s defense of his ministry and confidence in divine power.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Acts 26:16–18, Paul recounts Christ sending him to open people’s eyes, turning them from darkness to light and from Satan’s power to God. That commission gives a larger setting for the struggle described here: people are being called into a new allegiance through the gospel.

1 Corinthians 1:18–25 identifies the message of the crucified Christ as God’s power and wisdom. Together, these passages show why Paul can appeal with Christ’s gentleness and still trust that his message has power to change lives.
