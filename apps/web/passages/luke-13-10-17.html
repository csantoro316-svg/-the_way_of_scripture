Passage

Luke 13:10-17

Why does Jesus call the Sabbath a fitting day to set this woman free?

Berean Standard Bible

10 One Sabbath Jesus was teaching in one of the synagogues, 11 and a woman there had been disabled by a spirit for eighteen years. She was hunched over and could not stand up straight. 12 When Jesus saw her, He called her over and said, “Woman, you are set free from your disability.” 13 Then He placed His hands on her, and immediately she straightened up and began to glorify God.

14 But the synagogue leader was indignant that Jesus had healed on the Sabbath. “There are six days for work,” he told the crowd. “So come and be healed on those days and not on the Sabbath.” 15 “You hypocrites!” the Lord replied. “Does not each of you on the Sabbath untie his ox or donkey from the stall and lead it to water? 16 Then should not this daughter of Abraham, whom Satan has kept bound for eighteen long years, be released from her bondage on the Sabbath day?” 17 When Jesus said this, all His adversaries were humiliated. And the whole crowd rejoiced at all the glorious things He was doing.

What is the context?

While teaching in a synagogue on the Sabbath, Jesus sees a woman who has been disabled by a spirit for eighteen years. She is bent over and cannot stand straight. He calls her forward, announces her release and places His hands on her. She immediately straightens and glorifies God.

The synagogue leader objects to healing on the Sabbath and tells the crowd to come on the other six days. Jesus answers by pointing to an ordinary act they allow: untying an ox or donkey to lead it to water.

His comparison moves from care for an animal to the release of this daughter of Abraham. Satan has bound her for eighteen years; the Sabbath is an appropriate day for her freedom. His adversaries are humiliated, while the crowd rejoices.

The Greek verb λύω, lyō (LOO-oh), links the comparison: it can mean to untie what is fastened or release someone who is bound. Thayer specifically lists both the animal’s untying in verse 15 and the woman’s release in verse 16. The word helps show the connection Jesus makes between their accepted care and the healing they object to. (Thayer, Greek 3089)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly understand Jesus as exposing a double standard and defending the woman’s release as fitting the Sabbath. The Catholic bishops’ notes emphasize the movement from untying animals to freeing her, while Wesley stresses her greater worth as a person and a daughter of Abraham. (USCCB notes on Luke 13:15–16; Wesley on Luke 13:16)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah joins the loosening of oppressive bonds and care for people in need with honoring the Sabbath. That combination offers a wider Scriptural parallel to the freedom and mercy Jesus defends here. The Sabbath is not set against the good of the person God sees. (Isaiah 58:6–14)

Colossians describes God rescuing people from the dominion of darkness and bringing them into His Son’s kingdom. Jesus explicitly describes this woman’s condition as Satan’s bondage and His healing as release. Her restoration is a concrete instance of the deliverance His mission brings, without making every illness the result of a particular person’s sin. (Luke 13:16; Colossians 1:13–14)
