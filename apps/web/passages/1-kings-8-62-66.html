Passage

1 Kings 8:62-66

How does the dedication become a shared celebration?

Berean Standard Bible

62 Then the king and all Israel with him offered sacrifices before the LORD. 63 And Solomon offered as peace offerings to the LORD 22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep. So the king and all the Israelites dedicated the house of the LORD. 64 On that same day the king consecrated the middle of the courtyard in front of the house of the LORD, and there he offered the burnt offerings, the grain offerings, and the fat of the peace offerings, since the bronze altar before the LORD was too small to contain all these offerings.

65 So at that time Solomon and all Israel with him—a great assembly of people from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of Egypt—kept the feast before the LORD our God for seven days and seven more days—fourteen days in all. 66 On the fifteenth day Solomon sent the people away. So they blessed the king and went home, joyful and glad in heart for all the good things that the LORD had done for His servant David and for His people Israel.

What is the context?

The king and the people offer sacrifices together. The peace offerings include twenty-two thousand oxen and one hundred twenty thousand sheep. With more offerings than the bronze altar can hold, Solomon consecrates the middle of the courtyard for this occasion.

The gathering includes people from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of Egypt. The celebration lasts fourteen days. At its close the people bless the king and go home joyful because of the LORD’s goodness to David and Israel.

The account ends with that shared gratitude. The temple has been built, the ark brought in, God’s glory made known, and the people called to wholehearted faithfulness.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Gill distinguishes the seven days of dedication from the seven days of the Feast of Tabernacles. He also connects the feasting with the portions of the peace offerings shared by the worshippers.

Henry emphasizes how the people leave: their joy gives way to grateful life at home. The scale of the gathering matters in the narrative, while its closing emphasis is the goodness of God that the people celebrate.
