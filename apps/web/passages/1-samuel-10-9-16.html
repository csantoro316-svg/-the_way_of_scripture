Passage

1 Samuel 10:9-16

What changes in Saul when the signs come true?

Berean Standard Bible

9 As Saul turned to leave Samuel, God changed Saul’s heart, and all the signs came to pass that day. 10 When Saul and his servant arrived at Gibeah, a group of prophets met him. Then the Spirit of God rushed upon him, and he prophesied along with them. 11 When all those who had formerly known Saul saw him prophesying with the prophets, they asked one another, “What has happened to the son of Kish? Is Saul also among the prophets?” 12 Then a man who lived there replied, “And who is their father?” So the saying became a proverb: “Is Saul also among the prophets?” 13 And when Saul had finished prophesying, he went up to the high place.

14 Now Saul’s uncle asked him and his servant, “Where did you go?” “To look for the donkeys,” Saul replied. “When we saw they were not to be found, we went to Samuel.” 15 “Tell me,” Saul’s uncle asked, “what did Samuel say to you?” 16 And Saul replied, “He assured us that the donkeys had been found.” But Saul did not tell his uncle what Samuel had said about the kingship.

What is the context?

As Saul leaves Samuel, God changes his heart, and the announced signs take place that day. The account then describes the most public sign: the Spirit of God comes upon him, and he prophesies with the group at Gibeah. People who know him are surprised to see this.

Their question about Saul being among the prophets becomes a proverb. The episode presents something unexpected in a familiar person and credits God with the change. It does not quote the contents of his prophesying or describe the age, dress or appearance of the other prophets.

When Saul’s uncle asks about the journey, Saul reports that Samuel assured him the donkeys had been found. He does not disclose what Samuel said about kingship. The narrator marks that silence, leaving his full reasons unstated.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The significance of the changed heart is an important interpretive question. Wesley explains the change in terms of courage, wisdom and other qualities suited to Saul’s new office. Gill explicitly understands it as preparation for governing, rather than the new heart of conversion.

Henry also cautions against assuming that this surprising religious experience settles Saul’s lasting spiritual condition. His reading distinguishes becoming different in this situation from being made new in the fullest spiritual sense. The text itself states that God changes Saul and empowers his prophesying; conclusions about his final standing before God require a wider interpretation of his story.

On Saul’s silence to his uncle, Wesley sees modesty and a commitment to keeping the matter private. That is an explanation of the omission, rather than an inward motive directly reported by Saul. The scene does not require either immediate condemnation of deceit or a complete verdict on his humility.
