Passage

Jonah 3:5-10

How do the Ninevites respond to the warning?

Berean Standard Bible

5 And the Ninevites believed God. They proclaimed a fast and dressed in sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least. 6 When word reached the king of Nineveh, he got up from his throne, took off his royal robe, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes.

7 Then he issued a proclamation in Nineveh: “By the decree of the king and his nobles: Let no man or beast, herd or flock, taste anything at all. They must not eat or drink. 8 Furthermore, let both man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and have everyone call out earnestly to God. Let each one turn from his evil ways and from the violence in his hands. 9 Who knows? God may turn and relent; He may turn from His fierce anger, so that we will not perish.” 10 When God saw their actions—that they had turned from their evil ways—He relented from the disaster He had threatened to bring upon them.

What is the context?

The Ninevites believe God, proclaim a fast and put on sackcloth. The response reaches the king, who leaves his throne, removes his robe and sits in ashes.

His decree includes fasting and sackcloth for people and animals, earnest prayer, and turning from evil and violence. The visible signs of mourning accompany a demand for changed conduct. He hopes God may relent so that they do not perish.

God sees that they have turned from their evil ways and does not bring the threatened disaster. This accords with the principle stated in Jeremiah 18:7–10: when a nation turns from the evil for which it was warned, God may relent from the announced judgment.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Cambridge emphasize the change of life, especially the turning from evil. Fasting and sackcloth express repentance, but the account specifically identifies their changed conduct when describing God’s response.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus explicitly returns to this event in Matthew 12:38–42. He says the people of Nineveh will condemn His generation at the judgment because they repented at Jonah’s preaching, while One greater than Jonah stands before His hearers.

The Ninevites’ response becomes a challenge to people receiving a greater revelation through Jesus. God’s warning calls for a real response, and the mercy shown to this foreign city makes continued refusal to listen all the more serious. The connection leads beyond admiration for Nineveh’s repentance to hearing the Son Himself.
