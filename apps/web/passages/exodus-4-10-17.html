Passage

Exodus 4:10-17

What if Moses did not feel able to speak?

Berean Standard Bible

10 “Please, Lord,” Moses replied, “I have never been eloquent, neither in the past nor since You have spoken to Your servant, for I am slow of speech and tongue.” 11 And the LORD said to him, “Who gave man his mouth? Or who makes the mute or the deaf, the sighted or the blind? Is it not I, the LORD? 12 Now go! I will help you as you speak, and I will teach you what to say.”

13 But Moses replied, “Please, Lord, send someone else.” 14 Then the anger of the LORD burned against Moses, and He said, “Is not Aaron the Levite your brother? I know that he can speak well, and he is now on his way to meet you. When he sees you, he will be glad in his heart. 15 You are to speak to him and put the words in his mouth. I will help both of you to speak, and I will teach you what to do. 16 He will speak to the people for you. He will be your spokesman, and it will be as if you were God to him. 17 But take this staff in your hand so you can perform signs with it.”

What is the context?

Moses turns from the people’s possible unbelief to his own difficulty speaking. The Hebrew description of his mouth and tongue uses a word meaning heavy, which Brown–Driver–Briggs explains here as slow or difficult speech. Moses says the difficulty has remained even since God began speaking with him.

God answers as the Creator of the mouth, hearing and sight. He tells Moses to go and promises to help him speak and teach him what to say. Moses then asks God to send someone else, and the LORD becomes angry.

Even then, God provides Aaron to speak for him. Moses will give Aaron the words, Aaron will address the people, and God will help them both. The comparison between Moses and God in verse 16 describes how the message passes through Moses to Aaron. Moses is to take the staff as well; the calling and the signs remain his responsibility.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The shared reading understands Moses’ final request as an attempt to be excused from the task. Calvin and Wesley both emphasize that God’s promised help answers his weakness, while his continuing refusal brings God’s displeasure. Aaron’s assistance allows the work to go forward.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The exchange has a close parallel in Jeremiah 1:6–9. Jeremiah says he does not know how to speak; God sends him, promises His presence and gives him words. In both callings, the messenger’s confidence rests on the God who sends him.

1 Corinthians 2:1–5 brings that theme into the preaching of Christ. Paul recalls coming in weakness and fear, so that the Corinthians’ faith would rest on God’s power. Across these settings, God’s message reaches people through servants who depend on Him.
