Passage

2 Corinthians 1:15-22

Did changed plans mean that Paul’s message could not be trusted?

Berean Standard Bible

15 Confident of this, I planned to visit you first, so that you might receive a double blessing. 16 I wanted to visit you on my way to Macedonia, and to return to you from Macedonia, and then to have you help me on my way to Judea. 17 When I planned this, did I do it carelessly? Or do I make my plans by human standards, so as to say “Yes, yes” and also “No, no”?

18 But as surely as God is faithful, our message to you is not “Yes” and “No.” 19 For the Son of God, Jesus Christ, who was proclaimed among you by me and Silvanus and Timothy, was not “Yes” and “No,” but in Him it has always been “Yes.” 20 For all the promises of God are “Yes” in Christ. And so through Him, our “Amen” is spoken to the glory of God.

21 Now it is God who establishes both us and you in Christ. He anointed us, 22 placed His seal on us, and put His Spirit in our hearts as a pledge of what is to come.

What is the context?

Paul explains that he had intended to visit Corinth on his way to Macedonia and again on his return, before traveling toward Judea. The change raised a question about his reliability. Had he made the plans carelessly, saying yes while meaning no?

His answer points beyond the itinerary to the message he, Silvanus and Timothy preached. Jesus Christ is not an uncertain promise that God makes and then takes back. God’s promises find their yes in Him, and believers respond through Him with their Amen to God’s glory.

God is also the one who establishes both the workers and the congregation in Christ. He has anointed them, placed His seal on them and given His Spirit in their hearts. Paul places himself alongside the Corinthians as someone dependent on God’s work.

The word translated pledge is arrabōn (ar-rah-BOHN), ἀρραβών: a first payment that assures the rest will follow; see Thayer’s entry. The Spirit is God’s gift and assurance of what is to come, not a human achievement Paul uses to elevate himself above them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as a distinction between a changed travel plan and the reliability of the gospel. Wesley’s notes on verses 18–22 emphasize the certainty of God’s promises in Christ and the Spirit as the beginning of the fullness still awaited. The USCCB notes on verses 18–22 likewise center the paragraph on God’s faithfulness and the Spirit’s assurance. The paragraph does not turn every human intention into an unconditional divine promise.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ephesians 1:9–14 sets the Spirit’s seal and pledge within God’s purpose to bring all things in heaven and earth together in Christ. Those who hear and believe the gospel receive the promised Spirit while awaiting their inheritance.

That wider statement shows why Paul grounds confidence in Christ rather than in a perfect travel schedule. The future God promises rests on His purpose and His gift. The Spirit gives believers a present share in the life whose completion they still await.
