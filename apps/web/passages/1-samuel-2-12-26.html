Passage

1 Samuel 2:12-26

What is the difference between holding an office and serving God?

Berean Standard Bible

12 Now the sons of Eli were wicked men; they had no regard for the LORD 13 or for the custom of the priests with the people. When any man offered a sacrifice, the servant of the priest would come with a three-pronged meat fork while the meat was boiling 14 and plunge it into the pan or kettle or cauldron or cooking pot. And the priest would claim for himself whatever the meat fork brought up. This is how they treated all the Israelites who came to Shiloh. 15 Even before the fat was burned, the servant of the priest would come and say to the man who was sacrificing, “Give the priest some meat to roast, because he will not accept boiled meat from you, but only raw.” 16 And if any man said to him, “The fat must be burned first; then you may take whatever you want,” the servant would reply, “No, you must give it to me right now. If you refuse, I will take it by force!” 17 Thus the sin of these young men was severe in the sight of the LORD, for they were treating the LORD’s offering with contempt.

18 Now Samuel was ministering before the LORD—a boy wearing a linen ephod. 19 Each year his mother would make him a little robe and bring it to him when she went with her husband to offer the annual sacrifice. 20 And Eli would bless Elkanah and his wife, saying, “May the LORD give you children by this woman in place of the one she dedicated to the LORD.” Then they would go home. 21 So the LORD attended to Hannah, and she conceived and gave birth to three sons and two daughters. Meanwhile, the boy Samuel grew up in the presence of the LORD.

22 Now Eli was very old, and he heard about everything his sons were doing to all Israel and how they were sleeping with the women who served at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting. 23 “Why are you doing these things?” Eli said to his sons. “I hear about your wicked deeds from all these people. 24 No, my sons; it is not a good report I hear circulating among the LORD’s people. 25 If a man sins against another man, God can intercede for him; but if a man sins against the LORD, who can intercede for him?” But they would not listen to their father, since the LORD intended to put them to death. 26 And the boy Samuel continued to grow in stature and in favor with the LORD and with man.

What is the context?

Eli’s sons hold priestly office, but the narrator says they have no regard for the LORD. Their servants seize meat from worshipers and demand it before the fat is burned. The threat of force turns worship into an opportunity to take what they want. Their conduct expresses contempt for the LORD’s offering.

Samuel appears in deliberate contrast: he serves before the LORD while still a boy. Hannah continues to care for him, bringing a robe each year. Her dedication has not ended her relationship with him. The LORD also gives her three more sons and two daughters, while Samuel grows in His presence.

The linen ephod is a garment used in worship, not proof that Samuel has become high priest. Second Samuel 6:14 also describes David wearing a linen ephod. Samuel’s service matters here more than any claim to the highest office.

Eli hears of the abuses, including his sons sleeping with the women who serve at the entrance. He rebukes them, but they will not listen. The narrator also says that the LORD intends to put them to death. The closing sentence returns to Samuel growing in favor with God and people, setting his course beside theirs.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley both regard Eli’s response as inadequate: a verbal warning has not restrained ongoing abuses. The later accusation in verse 29 also includes Eli in benefiting from the offerings. This is a concrete failure involving his own household and authority, rather than proof that every parent causes every wrong choice an adult child makes.

On verse 25, Wesley reads the sons’ refusal as the culmination of their prolonged hardening, followed by God giving them over to judgment. Gill also understands a divine judgment that leaves them unresponsive, and retains the connection between their refusal and God’s intention to put them to death. These readings take the narrator’s explanation seriously without treating the sons as innocent people merely caught in a misunderstanding.

Gill does not read Eli’s question about who can intercede as a denial that Christ can save sinners. He distinguishes the severity of these offenses against God’s worship from the hope of mediation through Jesus. Eli’s warning should therefore be read in its immediate setting, rather than turned into a claim that no sin against God can ever be forgiven.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The description of Samuel growing in stature and favor has a close New Testament parallel. Luke 2:40–52 describes Jesus growing, being found in the temple, and continuing in favor with God and people. Both accounts give a glimpse of a child’s life in relation to God before the work for which he will later be known.

The parallel does not make Samuel equal to Jesus or erase their different callings. It helps place Samuel’s quiet growth within the larger story of God preparing His servants. Luke’s account identifies a greater calling: Jesus speaks of His Father’s house while continuing to live obediently with His parents.
