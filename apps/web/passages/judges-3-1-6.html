Passage

Judges 3:1-6

What will Israel do when its faith is tested?

Berean Standard Bible

1 These are the nations that the LORD left to test all the Israelites who had not known any of the wars in Canaan, 2 if only to teach warfare to the subsequent generations of Israel, especially to those who had not known it formerly: 3 the five rulers of the Philistines, all the Canaanites, the Sidonians, and the Hivites who lived in the mountains of Lebanon from Mount Baal-hermon to Lebo-hamath. 4 These nations were left to test the Israelites, to find out whether they would keep the commandments of the LORD, which He had given their fathers through Moses.

5 Thus the Israelites continued to live among the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites. 6 And they took the daughters of these people in marriage, gave their own daughters to their sons, and served their gods.

What is the context?

The previous chapter explained why the remaining nations would become a test for Israel. These verses name the peoples involved and describe a generation that had not experienced the earlier wars in Canaan. They would learn warfare, and their response would show whether they would obey the LORD’s commands.

The test concerns loyalty as well as fighting. Israel settles among the surrounding peoples, intermarriages follow, and the Israelites serve their gods. This is the danger named in Deuteronomy 7:1–6: family ties would draw God’s people away from Him into the worship of other gods.

The passage identifies that turning away as the failure. It does not make ancestry alone a measure of faithfulness. Elsewhere, Rahab acknowledges the LORD as God, and Ruth commits herself to Him (Joshua 2:8–14; Ruth 1:14–18). Here, however, the Israelites adopt the worship they had been warned against.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill take the teaching of warfare seriously as practical preparation for a generation without that experience. Wesley also emphasizes their need to stay close to God and depend on His help. Military experience and obedience belong together in this reading; practical skill does not replace trust in God.

Henry draws a further lesson for believers: pressures can reveal what is in the heart and strengthen faith, while comfortable acceptance of unfaithful worship can be more dangerous than open opposition. That is his application to Christian life; Israel’s particular commands in the land remain the immediate setting.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The Bible returns to the question of what testing can produce in God’s people. James 1:2–8 teaches that the testing of faith develops perseverance and calls believers to seek God’s wisdom. First Peter 1:3–9 places trials within the living hope given through Jesus’ resurrection: faith is being proved while believers look toward His appearing and the promised inheritance.

These later passages show a purpose beyond getting through a difficult moment. God forms people who trust Him and remain faithful. The setting has changed from Israel’s wars to life following Christ, but the call to trust and obey continues.
