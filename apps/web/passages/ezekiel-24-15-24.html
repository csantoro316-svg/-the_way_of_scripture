Passage

Ezekiel 24:15-24

Why is Ezekiel told not to mourn when his wife dies?

Berean Standard Bible

15 Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 16 “Son of man, behold, I am about to take away the desire of your eyes with a fatal blow. But you must not mourn or weep or let your tears flow. 17 Groan quietly; do not mourn for the dead. Put on your turban and strap your sandals on your feet; do not cover your lips or eat the bread of mourners.” 18 So I spoke to the people in the morning, and in the evening my wife died. And the next morning I did as I had been commanded.

19 Then the people asked me, “Won’t you tell us what these things you are doing mean to us?” 20 So I answered them, “The word of the LORD came to me, saying: 21 Tell the house of Israel that this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘I am about to desecrate My sanctuary, the pride of your power, the desire of your eyes, and the delight of your soul. And the sons and daughters you left behind will fall by the sword.’

22 Then you will do as I have done: You will not cover your lips or eat the bread of mourners. 23 Your turbans will remain on your heads and your sandals on your feet. You will not mourn or weep, but you will waste away because of your sins, and you will groan among yourselves. 24 ‘Thus Ezekiel will be a sign for you; you will do everything that he has done. When this happens, you will know that I am the Lord GOD.’

What is the context?

The Lord tells Ezekiel that the desire of his eyes will be taken away, and verse 18 identifies the loss as his wife’s death. Ezekiel is commanded to groan quietly while refraining from the mourning actions listed in the passage. His love and loss are not treated as unimportant.

The people ask what his behavior means for them. The Lord answers by naming what they will lose: His sanctuary, which is the desire of their eyes, and the sons and daughters left behind in Jerusalem. Ezekiel’s personal sorrow becomes a sign of the disaster approaching the community.

They too will groan among themselves while wasting away because of their sins. The explanation makes the connection between the sign and the city’s loss; it does not accuse Ezekiel’s wife of a particular sin or tell us her thoughts during her final day.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Cambridge understands the lack of outward mourning as a picture of people stunned by the severity of Jerusalem’s fall. Wesley emphasizes the restrictions of captivity as an explanation for grief that cannot be openly expressed. The passage itself states the sign and the coming behavior without explaining every circumstance that will prevent ordinary mourning.

Henry draws a broader lesson about grief held together with trust in God. This particular command to Ezekiel should not become a rule that faithful people must never cry: John 11:32–36 describes Jesus weeping at the death of Lazarus.
