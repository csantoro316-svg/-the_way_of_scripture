Passage

Matthew 7:1-5

Seeing Clearly Before Correcting Others

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Do not judge, or you will be judged. 2 For with the same judgment you pronounce, you will be judged, and with the measure you use, it will be measured to you.

3 Why do you look at the speck in your brother’s eye but fail to notice the beam in your own eye? 4 How can you say to your brother, ‘Let me take the speck out of your eye,’ while there is still a beam in your own eye? 5 You hypocrite! First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye.

What is the context?

Jesus now addresses how His followers regard the faults of other people. The warning begins with judgment and the standard a person uses. Someone who passes judgment is also accountable: the measure used for others will be applied to the person using it.

The verb for judging is κρίνω, krinō (KREE-noh). Thayer's Greek Lexicon gives a range of uses for forming or passing judgment and identifies harsh, unfair faultfinding in this verse. The surrounding illustration shows the problem Jesus is exposing: attending closely to someone else's fault while failing to face one's own.

A tiny speck and a large beam make the contrast vivid. The person offering help cannot see clearly because of the much larger obstruction in his own eye. His claim to help another is hollow while he leaves his own problem untouched. Jesus calls that hypocrisy.

The final instruction gives an order: remove the beam first, then help with the brother's speck. The aim includes genuine help after honest self-examination. Later in this same chapter, Matthew 7:15–20 also calls for recognizing false prophets by their fruit. Wise judgment and humble correction have a place alongside this warning against hypocrisy.

Luke 6:37–42 places a similar warning with forgiveness, generosity, and the same eye illustration. The concern is how people extend mercy and offer help while recognizing their own need.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand these verses as forbidding harsh, hypocritical judgment and requiring honest self-examination before correcting someone else. Wesley's notes on Matthew 7:1–5 stress knowledge and love; the Catholic Catechism, 2477–2478, rejects assuming someone's guilt without sufficient grounds; the Reformed Westminster Larger Catechism, question 145, condemns rash, harsh, and partial judgments.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God's mercy changes the way people look at those who have failed. In 1 Timothy 1:12–17, Paul recalls his own violence and unbelief, then explains that Christ showed him mercy. His life becomes an example of Christ's patience for others who will believe. Remembering received mercy gives a reason for humility toward another person's need.

Galatians 6:1–5 brings self-examination and help for another person together. A person caught in sin is to be restored gently, while those helping watch themselves and carry one another's burdens. Paul connects this with fulfilling the law of Christ. His saving mercy forms a people who restore one another with humility.
