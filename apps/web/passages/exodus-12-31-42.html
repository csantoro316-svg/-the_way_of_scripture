Passage

Exodus 12:31-42

How did Israel finally leave Egypt?

Berean Standard Bible

31 Then Pharaoh summoned Moses and Aaron by night and said, “Get up, leave my people, both you and the Israelites! Go, worship the LORD as you have requested. 32 Take your flocks and herds as well, just as you have said, and depart! And bless me also.” 33 And in order to send them out of the land quickly, the Egyptians urged the people on. “For otherwise,” they said, “we are all going to die!” 34 So the people took their dough before it was leavened, carrying it on their shoulders in kneading bowls wrapped in clothing. 35 Furthermore, the Israelites acted on Moses’ word and asked the Egyptians for articles of silver and gold, and for clothing. 36 And the LORD gave the people such favor in the sight of the Egyptians that they granted their request. In this way they plundered the Egyptians.

37 The Israelites journeyed from Rameses to Succoth with about 600,000 men on foot, besides women and children. 38 And a mixed multitude also went up with them, along with great droves of livestock, both flocks and herds. 39 Since their dough had no leaven, the people baked what they had brought out of Egypt into unleavened loaves. For when they had been driven out of Egypt, they could not delay and had not prepared any provisions for themselves. 40 Now the duration of the Israelites’ stay in Egypt was 430 years. 41 At the end of the 430 years, to the very day, all the LORD’s divisions went out of the land of Egypt. 42 Because the LORD kept a vigil that night to bring them out of the land of Egypt, this same night is to be a vigil to the LORD, to be observed by all the Israelites for the generations to come.

What is the context?

During the night Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron and orders Israel to leave, taking the families, flocks and herds. He asks for a blessing as well. The Egyptians urge the people to go quickly, afraid that further death will follow if they remain.

Israel leaves in haste, carrying dough before it has been leavened. They also receive silver, gold and clothing from the Egyptians, whose favor the LORD has given them. Their departure with possessions recalls the promise in Genesis 15:13–14.

The journey begins from Rameses to Succoth. The account numbers about 600,000 men, besides women and children, and includes a mixed multitude with them. Their unleavened bread is tied directly to the urgency of leaving before they could prepare provisions.

The account gives 430 years for Israel’s stay in Egypt and emphasizes that the departure takes place to the very day. The night is to be remembered as a vigil to the LORD, who watched over them to bring them out. The ending turns the event into an observance for generations to come.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The chronology is read in two main ways. Keil and Delitzsch understand the 430 years as the time spent in Egypt and allow that genealogies may omit intermediate generations. Calvin and Wesley count the period from the patriarchs’ sojourn through the Exodus, drawing on Galatians 3:17. The BSB retains the statement that Israel’s stay in Egypt was 430 years. These explanations differ over how that statement relates to the other chronological passages.

The shared emphasis is God’s faithfulness in bringing the promised people out. The account also links the haste, the unleavened bread and the possessions they receive with the circumstances of their departure.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Genesis 15:13–18 had joined oppression, judgment of the enslaving nation, departure with possessions and the promised land. The Exodus brings those promises into view as events begin to unfold.

Galatians 3:15–18 places the promise in a still larger setting. Paul identifies the promised seed with Christ and argues that the law given later does not cancel God’s earlier promise. Beyond the chronological question, the New Testament directs attention to the continuing faithfulness of God and the inheritance He grants through His promise.
