Passage

Exodus 37:10-16

What is the table prepared to hold?

Berean Standard Bible

10 He also made the table of acacia wood two cubits long, a cubit wide, and a cubit and a half high. 11 He overlaid it with pure gold and made a gold molding around it. 12 And he made a rim around it a handbreadth wide and put a gold molding on the rim. 13 He cast four gold rings for the table and fastened them to the four corners at its four legs. 14 The rings were placed close to the rim, to serve as holders for the poles used to carry the table. 15 He made the poles of acacia wood for carrying the table and overlaid them with gold. 16 He also made the utensils for the table out of pure gold: its plates and dishes, as well as its bowls and pitchers for pouring drink offerings.

What is the context?

The table is made of acacia wood overlaid with gold, with a rim, rings, carrying poles, and gold utensils. Like the ark, it is prepared to travel with Israel. Its place is in the Holy Place, where the Bread of the Presence is to be kept before the LORD, as directed in Exodus 25:23–30.

Leviticus 24:5–9 explains the continuing service: twelve loaves are arranged before God, replaced each Sabbath, and eaten by Aaron and his sons in a holy place. The table therefore belongs to an ongoing relationship of worship and provision, not simply the furnishing of an empty room.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley compares the priestly bread with the fellowship offered at Christ’s table in the new covenant. He sees the later table as a fuller enjoyment of what the earlier sanctuary held at a distance.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

At the Passover meal, Jesus shares bread and a cup with His apostles and speaks of His body given for them and the new covenant in His blood (Luke 22:14–20). Later in the same conversation, He promises His faithful disciples a place to eat and drink at His table in His kingdom (Luke 22:28–30).

This provides a meaningful parallel with the sanctuary table. God’s purpose includes fellowship with His people, and Jesus connects that fellowship with His self-giving and the coming kingdom. The comparison follows the larger theme of a people welcomed before God.
