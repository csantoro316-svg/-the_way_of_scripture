Passage

Luke 17:11-19

Why does Jesus draw attention to the man who returns?

Berean Standard Bible

11 While Jesus was on His way to Jerusalem, He was passing between Samaria and Galilee. 12 As He entered one of the villages, He was met by ten lepers. They stood at a distance 13 and raised their voices, shouting, “Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!” 14 When Jesus saw them, He said, “Go, show yourselves to the priests.” And as they were on their way, they were cleansed.

15 When one of them saw that he was healed, he came back, praising God in a loud voice. 16 He fell facedown at Jesus’ feet in thanksgiving to Him—and he was a Samaritan. 17 “Were not all ten cleansed?” Jesus asked. “Where then are the other nine? 18 Was no one found except this foreigner to return and give glory to God?” 19 Then Jesus said to him, “Rise and go; your faith has made you well!”

What is the context?

On His way to Jerusalem, Jesus meets ten men with leprosy between Samaria and Galilee. They stand at a distance and ask for mercy. The BSB’s footnote explains that leper refers to someone with a skin disease; the passage does not identify a modern medical diagnosis.

Jesus tells them to show themselves to the priests, and they are cleansed as they go. Leviticus required separation while such a disease made someone unclean and assigned priests the examination and cleansing process. Jesus’ instruction fits that Scriptural setting. (Leviticus 13:45–46; Leviticus 14:1–11)

One man returns, praises God and falls at Jesus’ feet in thanks. Luke then identifies him as a Samaritan. Jesus asks about the other nine and draws attention to this foreigner who has returned to give God glory.

Jesus’ final words link the man’s faith with being made well. The Greek verb is σῴζω, sōzō (SOH-zoh), which can mean save, rescue or heal. Thayer includes this verse among its uses for healing; the BSB footnote also offers has saved you. The word’s range is useful, but does not by itself settle every question about the other men. (Thayer, Greek 4982)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly emphasize thanksgiving, faith and the surprising example set by the Samaritan. The Catholic bishops’ notes understand the final saying as pointing to salvation through faith, beyond the cleansing all ten received. That reading gives weight both to the shared healing and to Jesus’ particular response to the man who returns. (USCCB notes on Luke 17:11–19)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Earlier in Luke, Jesus recalls Naaman, the Syrian whom Elisha healed. The grateful Samaritan provides a further example of God’s mercy being received by someone described as an outsider. This is a parallel within Luke’s account, rather than a claim that the two men are identical figures or that every person in Israel rejects Jesus. (Luke 4:24–27)

Psalm 107 calls those God has rescued and gathered from different lands to give Him thanks. The Samaritan’s return fits that larger response to mercy: the person helped by God openly praises Him. As Luke’s story continues into Acts, the witness to Jesus is commissioned to reach Samaria and the ends of the earth. (Psalm 107:1–3; Acts 1:6–8)
