Passage

1 Samuel 21:1-7

Why does the priest give David bread reserved for the priests?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then David came to Nob, to Ahimelech the priest. And when Ahimelech met David, he trembled and asked him, “Why are you alone? Why is no one with you?” 2 “The king has given me a mission,” David replied. “He told me no one is to know about the mission on which I am sending you. And I have directed my young men to meet me at a certain place. 3 Now then, what do you have on hand? Give me five loaves of bread, or whatever can be found.”

4 “There is no common bread on hand,” the priest replied, “but there is some consecrated bread—provided that the young men have kept themselves from women.” 5 David answered, “Women have indeed been kept from us, as is usual when I set out. And the bodies of the young men are holy even on common missions. How much more so today!” 6 So the priest gave him the consecrated bread, since there was no bread there but the Bread of the Presence, which had been removed from before the LORD and replaced with hot bread on the day it was taken away. 7 Now one of Saul’s servants was there that day, detained before the LORD. And his name was Doeg the Edomite, the chief shepherd for Saul.

What is the context?

David comes to Ahimelech the priest at Nob after separating from Jonathan. The priest is alarmed to see him without the company he expects. David says the king has sent him on a secret mission and that his young men are elsewhere. The reader knows he is fleeing from Saul, so the explanation hides the danger surrounding his visit.

David asks for five loaves or whatever food is available. Ahimelech has no ordinary bread on hand, but he has consecrated bread. He asks whether the young men have kept themselves from women, and David assures him that they are holy. The concern is ritual fitness for receiving the sacred food.

The Bread of the Presence had been removed and replaced with fresh bread. Leviticus 24:5–9 directs that twelve loaves be set before the LORD each Sabbath and that the bread belong to Aaron and his sons to eat in a holy place. Ahimelech gives this bread to David in his need.

The account also names a witness: Doeg the Edomite, Saul’s chief shepherd, is there, detained before the LORD. His presence will become important in the next chapter. Here, his reason for being detained is not explained.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Henry read David’s claim of a royal mission as a lie driven by fear. They distinguish his genuine need from the means he uses to obtain help. David’s earlier courage does not make him beyond criticism when he is afraid.

Henry and Gill understand Ahimelech’s gift as mercy responding to necessity. They connect it with Jesus’ later use of this event: the bread’s sacred purpose does not make a hungry person’s need unimportant.

Wesley discusses whether David’s reference to holy vessels concerns bodies or equipment; the BSB uses bodies. The practical issue in the exchange is whether David and the young men are fit to receive the bread. It need not become a judgment that ordinary marriage is sinful.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus Himself brings this event into His teaching in Matthew 12:1–8. When His hungry disciples are accused over picking grain on the Sabbath, He points to David and his companions eating the consecrated bread. He then speaks of the priests’ Sabbath service and declares that One greater than the temple is present.

His conclusion brings mercy and His own authority together: the innocent should not be condemned, and the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath. The story of David’s need helps explain the work of the greater King. God’s holy purposes are not served by using religious observance to overlook people who need mercy.

Jesus’ use of the bread incident does not require approval of David’s misleading account to Ahimelech. It directs our attention to the mercy shown in feeding him and to Jesus’ authority to explain the purpose of the law.
