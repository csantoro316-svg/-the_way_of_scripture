Passage

2 Samuel 13:15-22

Who listens to Tamar after the assault?

Berean Standard Bible

15 Then Amnon hated Tamar with such intensity that his hatred was greater than the love he previously had. “Get up!” he said to her. “Be gone!” 16 “No,” she replied, “sending me away is worse than this great wrong you have already done to me!” But he refused to listen to her. 17 Instead, he called to his attendant and said, “Throw this woman out and bolt the door behind her!” 18 So Amnon’s attendant threw her out and bolted the door behind her. Now Tamar was wearing a robe of many colors, because this is what the king’s virgin daughters wore. 19 And Tamar put ashes on her head and tore her robe. And putting her hand on her head, she went away crying aloud.

20 Her brother Absalom said to her, “Has your brother Amnon been with you? Be quiet for now, my sister. He is your brother. Do not take this thing to heart.” So Tamar lived as a desolate woman in the house of her brother Absalom. 21 When King David heard all this, he was furious. 22 And Absalom never said a word to Amnon, either good or bad, because he hated Amnon for violating his sister Tamar.

What is the context?

Amnon’s desire turns into intense hatred. He orders Tamar away and refuses to listen when she protests that sending her out adds another great wrong. His attendant puts her outside and bolts the door.

Tamar tears the robe that marks her place among the king’s virgin daughters, puts ashes on her head and goes away crying aloud. Absalom asks whether Amnon has been with her, tells her to be quiet for now, and takes her into his house. She remains there desolate.

David hears and is furious, but this account reports no punishment of Amnon by him. Absalom says nothing to Amnon while hating him for what he has done. Tamar’s distress is visible while the men’s responses leave the wrong unresolved.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands Tamar’s second protest as a response to added cruelty and public disgrace. Her words do not make the rape a small matter; they show how Amnon compounds the harm by throwing her out.

Henry and Wesley criticize David’s failure to deal adequately with his son. Gill also observes that the account records anger without reporting punishment. Their explanations of David’s motives vary; the absence of a reported action should not become certainty about every private thought he had.

Absalom provides a home, but his instruction to keep quiet is his response within this story. It is not a command from God to people who have been assaulted. Tamar’s desolation describes what she suffers, not a loss of her human worth or a share in Amnon’s guilt.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Psalm 34:18 speaks of the LORD being near to the brokenhearted. 2 Corinthians 1:3–4 calls God the Father of compassion and describes His comfort flowing through His people to others in trouble.

This broader theme supplies something the responses around Tamar leave painfully incomplete: wounded people need compassionate care rather than dismissal. God’s people are called to share the comfort they receive, with attention to the person who is suffering.

This is a connection through Scripture’s account of God’s character and His people’s calling. It is not a claim that Tamar’s suffering was good or that a promise of comfort removes the need to face the wrong done to her.
