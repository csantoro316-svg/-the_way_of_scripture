Passage

Genesis 13:1-9

Abram and Lot Part Ways

Berean Standard Bible

1 So Abram went up out of Egypt into the Negev—he and his wife and all his possessions—and Lot was with him. 2 And Abram had become extremely wealthy in livestock and silver and gold.

3 From the Negev he journeyed from place to place toward Bethel, until he came to the place between Bethel and Ai where his tent had formerly been pitched, 4 to the site where he had built the altar. And there Abram called on the name of the LORD.

5 Now Lot, who was traveling with Abram, also had flocks and herds and tents. 6 But the land was unable to support both of them while they stayed together, for they had so many possessions that they were unable to coexist. 7 And there was discord between the herdsmen of Abram and the herdsmen of Lot. At that time the Canaanites and the Perizzites were also living in the land.

8 So Abram said to Lot, “Please let there be no contention between you and me, or between your herdsmen and my herdsmen. After all, we are kinsmen. 9 Is not the whole land before you? Now separate yourself from me. If you go to the left, I will go to the right; if you go to the right, I will go to the left.”

What is the context?

Abram returns from Egypt with Sarai, Lot, and his possessions. He travels back to the place between Bethel and Ai where he had built an altar, and there he calls on the LORD again. The return brings the story back to his earlier life of worship in the promised land after the danger and deception in Egypt.

Both Abram and Lot have large flocks and herds. Their wealth creates a practical problem: the available land cannot support them together, and their herdsmen begin to quarrel. Other peoples already live in the land as well. The dispute shows that having more possessions can bring pressures that prosperity alone cannot solve.

Abram addresses the conflict before it becomes a lasting breach between them. He appeals to their family relationship and offers Lot the choice of direction, agreeing to take the other way. Separation here is a practical way to preserve peace. Abram does not have to win the first choice in order to keep living in the land God has promised.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read Abram's proposal as a generous effort to protect family peace when their herds can no longer share the same ground. Wesley's introduction to Genesis 13 and notes on verses 6–7 describe the shortage of room and the wisdom of ending a quarrel that could expose the family to danger and public shame.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Abram's willingness to give up the first choice gives a concrete example of the Bible's call to make peace. Jesus blesses peacemakers in Matthew 5:9, and Romans 12:17–21 calls believers to pursue peace as far as it depends on them, leaving judgment to God. Abram takes responsibility for reducing conflict instead of insisting that his own interests come first.

The next part of the chapter will show that letting Lot choose does not put God's promise beyond reach. Abram can act generously while trusting God for the future. The lesson is not that every peaceful choice produces immediate material gain, but that faith gives us reasons to pursue peace beyond what we can secure for ourselves.
