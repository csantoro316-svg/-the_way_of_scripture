Passage

Revelation 14:14-20

What do the harvest and the winepress reveal about judgment?

Berean Standard Bible

14 And I looked and saw a white cloud, and seated on the cloud was One like the Son of Man, with a golden crown on His head and a sharp sickle in His hand. 15 Then another angel came out of the temple, crying out in a loud voice to the One seated on the cloud, “Swing Your sickle and reap, because the time has come to harvest, for the crop of the earth is ripe.” 16 So the One seated on the cloud swung His sickle over the earth, and the earth was harvested.

17 Then another angel came out of the temple in heaven, and he too had a sharp sickle. 18 Still another angel, with authority over the fire, came from the altar and called out in a loud voice to the angel with the sharp sickle, “Swing your sharp sickle and gather the clusters of grapes from the vine of the earth, because its grapes are ripe.” 19 So the angel swung his sickle over the earth and gathered the grapes of the earth, and he threw them into the great winepress of God’s wrath. 20 And the winepress was trodden outside the city, and the blood that flowed from it rose as high as the bridles of the horses for a distance of 1,600 stadia.

What is the context?

John sees a white cloud and One like the Son of Man seated upon it, wearing a golden crown and holding a sharp sickle. An angel comes from the temple and announces that the earth’s crop is ripe. The sickle is swung, and the earth is harvested.

A second harvest follows. Another angel with a sickle receives a call from an angel at the altar to gather the ripe grapes of the earth. The grapes are thrown into the great winepress of God’s wrath. This second picture explicitly identifies the harvest with judgment.

The winepress is trodden outside the city, and blood flows as high as the horses’ bridles over the distance John records. The picture is deliberately overwhelming. It conveys the severity and reach of judgment rather than giving readers a description of an ordinary agricultural harvest.

The two scenes bring the chapter’s warnings to a climax. The time for reaping is announced from God’s temple and altar. Earth’s apparent strength does not put it beyond His judgment.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The identity of the first reaper is disputed. Wesley understands an angel in human form, sent by Christ. Walvoord understands Christ Himself. The phrase Son of Man and the crowned figure support the latter reading; the message delivered by another angel has led some readers toward the former.

The first harvest is also explained differently. Wesley understands a gracious gathering of the righteous, followed by judgment on the wicked in the grape harvest. The USCCB notes likewise distinguish the gathering of the elect from the doom of the ungodly. Walvoord favors two pictures of judgment, with the second presenting its climax. The text explicitly names wrath in the winepress scene, while leaving the first harvest less fully explained.

The blood and distance are understood with different degrees of literal detail. The main readings recognize an extraordinary picture of judgment. A proposed battlefield, channel of blood or physical calculation should be distinguished from what John actually records.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Joel 3:12–14 places the sickle, ripe harvest and overflowing winepress together in a scene of judgment on the nations. That close parallel helps explain why Revelation uses harvest language for the moment when God deals with evil.

Isaiah 63:1–6 also pictures the LORD’s judgment through a winepress and blood-stained garments. Revelation’s scene belongs to that larger biblical picture of God acting against wickedness. The judgment answers the apparent success of the beast; its authority and violence will not continue forever.
