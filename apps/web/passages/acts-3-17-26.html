Passage

Acts 3:17-26

How does Peter connect repentance with God’s promises?

Berean Standard Bible

17 And now, brothers, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did your leaders. 18 But in this way God has fulfilled what He foretold through all the prophets, saying that His Christ would suffer. 19 Repent, then, and turn back, so that your sins may be wiped away, 20 that times of refreshing may come from the presence of the Lord, and that He may send Jesus, the Christ, who has been appointed for you. 21 Heaven must take Him in until the time comes for the restoration of all things, which God announced long ago through His holy prophets.

22 For Moses said, ‘The Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers. You must listen to Him in everything He tells you. 23 Everyone who does not listen to Him will be completely cut off from among his people.’ 24 Indeed, all the prophets from Samuel on, as many as have spoken, have proclaimed these days. 25 And you are sons of the prophets and of the covenant God made with your fathers when He said to Abraham, ‘Through your offspring all the families of the earth will be blessed.’ 26 When God raised up His Servant, He sent Him first to you to bless you by turning each of you from your wicked ways.”

What is the context?

Peter acknowledges that the people and their leaders acted in ignorance. He does not treat that as a reason they need no forgiveness. He calls them to repent and turn back, placing the invitation alongside what God has fulfilled through the Messiah’s suffering.

He speaks of sins being wiped away, times of refreshing, and Jesus’s return in connection with the restoration God promised through the prophets. The Greek noun ἀποκατάστασις, apokatastasis (ah-poh-kah-TAH-stah-sis), means restoration. Thayer’s lexicon discusses Acts 3:21 in relation to the restoration of God’s order, including the physical creation. The word gives the idea of things being put right; it does not by itself settle every detail of the future.

Peter then appeals to Moses’s promise of a prophet the people must hear, and to God’s covenant with Abraham. He identifies the listeners as heirs of those promises and describes the blessing as turning people away from their wicked ways. The promises call for a response to Jesus, not merely pride in possessing a religious heritage.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read the passage as a call to repentance and forgiveness, grounded in the promises God fulfills through Jesus. They differ over the scope and timing of the refreshing and restoration.

Calvin on verse 21 understands Christ’s death as securing restoration, with its effects already unfolding and its fullness awaiting His return. Wesley on verses 19–21 likewise places the promises within the course of the New Testament and its coming completion.

Thomas Constable’s notes on verses 19–21 emphasize Israel accepting its Messiah before the beginning of His earthly kingdom. That reading sees a national dimension alongside the individual call to forgiveness. These explanations should be recognized as the commentators’ readings, while retaining Peter’s explicit call to turn back and listen to Jesus.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Peter applies Deuteronomy 18:15–19 to the call to hear Jesus. God’s people are to listen to the prophet He raises up, rather than reject Him while claiming loyalty to Moses.

The promise to bless the families of the earth through Abraham’s offspring appears in Genesis 22:18. Galatians 3:7–9 also explains the blessing of the nations in connection with faith. Peter’s address begins with his own people, yet the promise he quotes reaches the families of the earth. The blessing includes the changed direction of people’s lives through God’s Servant.
