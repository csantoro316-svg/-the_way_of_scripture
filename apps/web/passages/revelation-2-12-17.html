Passage

Revelation 2:12-17

Can a church stand firm under pressure and still accept harmful teaching?

Berean Standard Bible

12 To the angel of the church in Pergamum write: These are the words of the One who holds the sharp, double-edged sword. 13 I know where you live, where the throne of Satan sits, yet you hold fast to My name. You did not deny your faith in Me, even in the days of My faithful witness Antipas, who was killed among you where Satan dwells.

14 But I have a few things against you, because some of you hold to the teaching of Balaam, who taught Balak to place a stumbling block before the Israelites so they would eat food sacrificed to idols and commit sexual immorality. 15 In the same way, some of you also hold to the teaching of the Nicolaitans. 16 Therefore repent! Otherwise I will come to you shortly and wage war against them with the sword of My mouth.

17 He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches. To the one who overcomes, I will give the hidden manna. I will also give him a white stone inscribed with a new name, known only to the one who receives it.

What is the context?

Jesus addresses Pergamum as the One with the sharp, double-edged sword. The image comes from Revelation 1:16 and returns in the warning that He will confront the false teachers with the sword of His mouth. He has authority to judge the teaching and conduct of His church.

The church has remained loyal to His name, even when His witness Antipas was killed among them. The letter describes their surroundings as the place of Satan’s throne, but does not identify a particular building or explain how Antipas died.

The same church nevertheless tolerates teaching compared with Balaam’s. Numbers 25:1–3 describes Israel joining in idolatrous meals and sexual immorality; Numbers 31:16 connects that unfaithfulness with Balaam’s counsel. Jesus says a similar trap is being set here. The warning about the Nicolaitans follows alongside it.

The call to repent addresses the church, while the threatened confrontation falls on those promoting the corrupt teaching. Courage against outside pressure does not excuse accepting corruption within. The promise of hidden manna, a white stone and a new name gives those who overcome something better than participation in the practices Jesus condemns.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill both connect the warning with Balaam’s influence on Israel and read the call to repentance seriously. Their wider settings differ: Wesley reads the local church’s correction, while Gill also applies Pergamum to the church’s growing worldly power after Constantine. That historical application is Gill’s reading, rather than an explanation shared by all Protestant churches.

The white stone has several explanations. Wesley suggests acquittal: a favorable verdict pictured by a white voting stone. Gill discusses that reading but favors an association with priestly acceptance and the stones connected with Israel’s high priest. The USCCB note suggests a name-bearing amulet, used here to picture victory and joy. These are proposals about the image’s background; the letter itself does not explain the custom.

The Greek word psephos means a small, smooth stone or pebble. Thayer’s entry records its use in voting and discusses the acquittal explanation, while acknowledging disagreement. A proposed ancient custom should help explain the promise without being treated as something John explicitly states. The giver, the new name and the personal nature of the gift are clear in the passage.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The manna recalls God feeding Israel in the wilderness and commanding that some be preserved for later generations; see Exodus 16:31–35. Jesus takes up that history in John 6:30–35, directing people to the Father’s gift of true bread from heaven and identifying Himself as the bread of life. This is a scriptural parallel that helps explain the promise: God provides lasting life for His people. Against the pressure to seek satisfaction at an idol’s table, Christ holds out provision that belongs to Him.

What is our position?

Our study The Berean Standard calls for teaching to be tested against Scripture, even when the speaker has a respected name. That principle applies to Pergamum’s warning: loyalty to Christ includes examining what a church accepts and teaches. The church’s courageous witness does not remove that responsibility.
