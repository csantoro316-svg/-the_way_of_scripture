Passage

Luke 4:1-13

How does Jesus remain faithful under testing?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then Jesus, full of the Holy Spirit, returned from the Jordan and was led by the Spirit into the wilderness, 2 where for forty days He was tempted by the devil. He ate nothing during those days, and when they had ended, He was hungry. 3 The devil said to Him, “If You are the Son of God, tell this stone to become bread.” 4 But Jesus answered, “It is written: ‘Man shall not live on bread alone.’”

5 Then the devil led Him up to a high place and showed Him in an instant all the kingdoms of the world. 6 “I will give You authority over all these kingdoms and all their glory,” he said. “For it has been relinquished to me, and I can give it to anyone I wish. 7 So if You worship me, it will all be Yours.” 8 But Jesus answered, “It is written: ‘Worship the Lord your God and serve Him only.’”

9 Then the devil led Him to Jerusalem and set Him on the pinnacle of the temple. “If You are the Son of God,” he said, “throw Yourself down from here. 10 For it is written: ‘He will command His angels concerning You to guard You carefully, 11 and they will lift You up in their hands, so that You will not strike Your foot against a stone.’” 12 But Jesus answered, “It also says, ‘Do not put the Lord your God to the test.’” 13 When the devil had finished every temptation, he left Him until an opportune time.

What is the context?

After His baptism, Jesus enters the wilderness full of the Holy Spirit. He is tempted by the devil during forty days without food. The testing follows the Father’s declaration that He is the beloved Son, and the devil’s challenges press on what it means for Him to live as that Son.

The first challenge concerns hunger and the use of His power to make bread. Next comes an offer of the world’s kingdoms in exchange for worship. Luke’s final scene moves to Jerusalem, where the devil quotes Scripture to urge Jesus to throw Himself from the temple and demand God’s protection.

Jesus answers each challenge from Deuteronomy. His responses concern dependence on God, worship and service owed to God alone, and refusing to put God to the test. The devil’s use of Psalm 91 shows why quoting Scripture is not enough: a proposed action must be read against what God actually commands. (Deuteronomy 8:1–5; Deuteronomy 6:10–18; Psalm 91)

The Greek word translated worship is προσκυνέω, proskyneō (pros-kooh-NEH-oh), meaning to bow down in homage or worship. Here the proposed act is bound to allegiance: the devil offers authority in return for Jesus acknowledging him. Jesus’ answer joins worship with serving God alone. (Thayer’s lexicon)

The Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness, while the devil urges disobedience. James likewise distinguishes endurance under trial from the claim that God entices someone to sin. The passage ends with the devil withdrawing until another opportunity; the testing belongs to Jesus’ continuing mission. (James 1:12–18)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian traditions commonly understand this as the obedient Son resisting the devil and remaining faithful to His Father through real testing. The Catholic Catechism explicitly connects His victory with Adam and Israel, while the Reformed confession affirms the Son’s real humanity and sinless obedience. (Catechism 538–540; Westminster 8.2–4)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The wilderness and Jesus’ answers form a supported parallel with Israel’s testing. Deuteronomy recalls forty years in which Israel learned dependence through hunger and God’s provision. Its warning against testing God points back to Massah, where the people questioned whether the Lord was with them. Jesus remains faithful in the very matters to which these passages speak. (Deuteronomy 8:1–5; Deuteronomy 6:16; Exodus 17:1–7)

The wider connection with Adam is explicit in Paul’s teaching: Adam’s disobedience brought sin and death, while Christ’s obedience brings grace and life. Luke has just traced Jesus’ human family back to Adam. His faithful response to testing belongs within the saving life of the Son who comes to rescue humanity. (Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:45–49)

This also reaches His people’s need for help. Hebrews describes Jesus as tested yet without sin and able to help those being tested, then invites believers to approach God confidently for mercy and grace. Paul also uses Israel’s wilderness failures as warnings and points to God’s faithfulness in providing a way to endure temptation. The account leads to reliance on this faithful Savior and attentive obedience. (Hebrews 2:14–18; Hebrews 4:14–16; 1 Corinthians 10:1–13)

What is our position?

Worship as the Early Church Knew It examines the demand to bow to Satan in Matthew’s account of the same testing. The study understands the act as allegiance: bowing would acknowledge his authority, and Jesus answers that worship and service belong to God alone. This supplies our position on the demand for worship and Jesus’ response; the passage’s other questions remain open for further study.
