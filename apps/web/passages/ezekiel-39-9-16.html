Passage

Ezekiel 39:9-16

Why are the weapons burned and the dead carefully buried?

Berean Standard Bible

9 Then those who dwell in the cities of Israel will go out, kindle fires, and burn up the weapons—the bucklers and shields, the bows and arrows, the clubs and spears. For seven years they will use them for fuel. 10 They will not gather wood from the countryside or cut it from the forests, for they will use the weapons for fuel. They will loot those who looted them and plunder those who plundered them, declares the Lord GOD.

11 And on that day I will give Gog a burial place in Israel, the Valley of the Travelers, east of the Sea. It will block those who travel through, because Gog and all his hordes will be buried there. So it will be called the Valley of Hamon-gog. 12 For seven months the house of Israel will be burying them in order to cleanse the land. 13 All the people of the land will bury them, and it will bring them renown on the day I display My glory, declares the Lord GOD.

14 And men will be employed to continually pass through the land to cleanse it by burying the invaders who remain on the ground. At the end of the seven months they will begin their search. 15 As they pass through the land, anyone who sees a human bone will set up a pillar next to it, until the gravediggers have buried it in the Valley of Hamon-gog. 16 (Even the city will be named Hamonah.) And so they will cleanse the land.

What is the context?

Those living in Israel’s cities will use the invaders’ weapons as fuel for seven years. Instead of carrying off Israel’s possessions, the attackers leave behind resources for the people they intended to plunder. The reversal is made explicit: the plundered become those who take the spoil.

Gog and his forces receive a burial place in the land they invaded. The people spend seven months burying the dead to cleanse the land, followed by a continuing search for remains. Anyone finding a human bone marks it so that the buriers can collect it.

The concern with cleansing has a background in Numbers 19:11–22, where contact with a corpse, human bone or grave brings ritual uncleanness. Here, even overlooked remains are to be dealt with. The victory includes putting the land in order after the devastation.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Cambridge explains the weapons as fuel and the continuing search as care for remains missed during the first burial work. Wesley draws attention to the reversal: Gog receives a place in Israel, but as a grave rather than a conquered possession.

Henry also draws a lesson about persistent work to remove the reproach left in the land. The readers differ in how far they apply the details as pictures of spiritual renewal, but agree that the aftermath displays the completeness of God’s victory.

The passage gives seven years for burning weapons and seven months for the initial burials. Those details deserve attention when considering a proposed timetable; they do not, by themselves, establish the date of the battle or a particular modern technology.
