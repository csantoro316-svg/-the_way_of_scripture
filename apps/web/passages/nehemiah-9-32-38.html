Passage

Nehemiah 9:32-38

What do the people ask for while acknowledging that God is just?

Berean Standard Bible

32 So now, our God, the great and mighty and awesome God who keeps His gracious covenant, do not view lightly all the hardship that has come upon us, and upon our kings and leaders, our priests and prophets, our ancestors and all Your people, from the days of the kings of Assyria until today. 33 You are just in all that has befallen us, because You have acted faithfully, while we have acted wickedly. 34 Our kings and leaders and priests and fathers did not obey Your law or listen to Your commandments and warnings that You gave them. 35 For even while they were in their kingdom, with the abundant goodness that You had given them, and in the spacious and fertile land that You had set before them, they would not serve You or turn from their wicked ways.

36 So here we are today as slaves in the land You gave our fathers to enjoy its fruit and goodness— here we are as slaves! 37 Its abundant harvest goes to the kings You have set over us because of our sins. And they rule over our bodies and our livestock as they please. We are in great distress. 38 In view of all this, we make a binding agreement, putting it in writing and sealing it with the names of our leaders, Levites, and priests.”

What is the context?

The prayer reaches the community’s present distress. The people ask the great and faithful God not to regard their hardships as a small matter, naming the suffering of rulers, priests, prophets, ancestors and the whole people.

They acknowledge that God has dealt faithfully while they have acted wickedly. Even in the land given to their fathers, they remain subject to kings who take its produce and rule over their bodies and livestock.

The prayer ends with a binding written agreement, sealed with the names of leaders, Levites and priests. Confession is to be followed by a recorded commitment to obey; the names and promises are set out in the next chapter.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the appeal as dependence on undeserved mercy, with responsibility for sin honestly acknowledged. Gill explains the continuing servitude in relation to Persian rule and understands the written agreement as a binding promise to observe God’s Law. Returning to the land has not yet removed every form of hardship or foreign control.
