Passage

Genesis 2:1-3

The Seventh Day

Berean Standard Bible

1 Thus the heavens and the earth were completed in all their vast array. 2 And by the seventh day God had finished the work He had been doing; so on that day He rested from all His work.

3 Then God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it, because on that day He rested from all the work of creation that He had accomplished.

What is the context?

These verses finish the creation account that began in Genesis 1:1. The six days of work are complete. God rests, blesses the seventh day, and sets it apart as holy. The next passage, Genesis 2:4-25, takes a closer look at human life in the garden. We should read these three verses with the chapter before them, even though they appear at the start of a new chapter.

God rests because His work is finished. He has not worn Himself out; Isaiah 40:28 tells us that the Creator does not grow tired. The seventh day also ends differently from the first six: there is no closing statement about evening and morning. That is worth noticing, but the missing statement alone cannot tell us how long the day lasted.

There is no command to Adam here telling him to keep a weekly day of rest. Later, Exodus 20:8-11 gives Israel that command and points back to God's work and rest at creation. Both passages matter. Genesis tells us what God did; Exodus tells Israel what to do because of it. Christians differ over how that command applies to us today.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that God finished His work and set apart the seventh day. The main question is what that means for us: does God still require a particular day for rest and worship, and if so, which day?

Where do Christians differ? The Westminster Confession, 21.7-8, and the 1689 Baptist Confession, 22.7-8, teach that God still requires one day in every seven for worship and rest. These church statements of belief say that the day was Saturday before Christ's resurrection and Sunday afterward. Churches that follow them often call Sunday the Christian Sabbath. This does not mean that every Presbyterian or Baptist church teaches the same thing.

John Wesley's notes on Genesis 2:2-3 describe God's rest as an example for us to stop working and take delight in our Creator. The Methodist Articles, Article VI, teach that Christians are not required to follow all the old laws about Israel's worship practices, while God's commands about right and wrong still apply. That statement does not itself settle which weekly day Christians should observe.

The Catholic Church teaches that Sunday should be set apart for worship and rest. Sunday celebrates Jesus' resurrection and what the Sabbath pointed toward: our lasting rest in God. Its Catechism, 2174-2185, connects the two while distinguishing Sunday from the Saturday Sabbath.

The Lutheran Large Catechism, Third Commandment, 79-87, teaches that we need time to hear God's Word, worship, and rest. It says that God does not require Christians to do this on one particular day of the week. Sunday is the church's regular day for gathering.

The Seventh-day Adventist Church teaches that God set apart the seventh day for everyone at creation and that we should still keep it. Its statement of belief about the Sabbath identifies Saturday as the day for rest, worship, and serving others. The day remembers God's creation and what He has done to save us through Christ. In this reading, the Sabbath has not moved to Sunday.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 4:3-11 quotes God's rest on the seventh day and tells its readers that the promise of entering His rest still stands. Joshua had led Israel into the promised land, but that was not the full rest God had promised. Hebrews urges its readers to trust and obey God rather than repeat the unbelief described in Psalm 95.

That connection gives these opening verses a place in the larger story. God finished His work at creation, and He calls His people to share in His rest. The rest we see at the beginning of the Bible helps us understand the rest He still promises His people.
