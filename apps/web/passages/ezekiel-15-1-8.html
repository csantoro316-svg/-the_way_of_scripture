Passage

Ezekiel 15:1-8

What does the burned vine show Jerusalem about its condition?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, how does the wood of the vine surpass any other branch among the trees in the forest? 3 Can wood be taken from it to make something useful? Or can one make from it a peg on which to hang utensils? 4 No, it is cast into the fire for fuel. The fire devours both ends, and the middle is charred. Can it be useful for anything? 5 Even when it was whole, it could not be made useful. How much less can it ever be useful when the fire has consumed it and charred it!

6 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Like the wood of the vine among the trees of the forest, which I have given to the fire for fuel, so I will give up the people of Jerusalem. 7 And I will set My face against them. Though they may have escaped the fire, yet another fire will consume them. And when I set My face against them, you will know that I am the LORD. 8 Thus I will make the land desolate, because they have acted unfaithfully,’ declares the Lord GOD.”

What is the context?

The Lord gives Ezekiel a short comparison. Is wood from a vine better than wood from a forest tree? Can it be made into a useful object, even a peg on which to hang utensils?

The picture becomes more pointed when the vine wood is burned at both ends and charred in the middle. If it was unsuitable for the work before, the fire has not improved it.

The Lord applies the image to Jerusalem’s people. As vine wood is given to the fire, they will be given up to judgment. Escaping one fire will not protect them from another.

The final verse gives the reason: they have acted unfaithfully. The warning removes the idea that simply being the Lord’s people makes their conduct unimportant or puts the city beyond His judgment.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley connect the vine’s value with fruitfulness. A vine is valued for its fruit, rather than its usefulness as timber, so its identity alone is no reason to boast.

Both apply the warning to an outward claim of belonging to God that produces no faithful response. Henry also directs readers toward Christ as the source of the fruit they need to bear. Their emphasis is a warning against empty religious confidence, not a statement that a person’s worth depends on social usefulness or achievement.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John 15:1–11 uses a related vine-and-branches picture. Jesus identifies Himself as the true vine and calls His disciples to remain in Him, because branches cannot bear fruit by themselves.

The passage includes both pruning that leads to more fruit and the warning of a branch that withers and is burned. Jesus connects remaining in His love with keeping His commandments, and His stated aim includes His disciples’ joy.

John does not explicitly quote Ezekiel 15. The parallel develops the question of fruitfulness by directing attention to Christ: the life that honors the Father depends on remaining in the Son. The warnings should be heard together with that invitation to receive life from Him.
