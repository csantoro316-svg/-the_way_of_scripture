Passage

Matthew 26:36-46

Gethsemane: Honest Sorrow and the Father’s Will

Berean Standard Bible

36 Then Jesus went with His disciples to a place called Gethsemane, and He told them, “Sit here while I go over there and pray.” 37 He took with Him Peter and the two sons of Zebedee and began to be sorrowful and deeply distressed. 38 Then He said to them, “My soul is consumed with sorrow to the point of death. Stay here and keep watch with Me.” 39 Going a little farther, He fell facedown and prayed, “My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me. Yet not as I will, but as You will.”

40 Then Jesus returned to the disciples and found them sleeping. “Were you not able to keep watch with Me for one hour?” He asked Peter. 41 “Watch and pray so that you will not enter into temptation. For the spirit is willing, but the body is weak.” 42 A second time He went away and prayed, “My Father, if this cup cannot pass unless I drink it, may Your will be done.” 43 And again Jesus returned and found them sleeping, for their eyes were heavy. 44 So He left them and went away once more and prayed a third time, saying the same thing.

45 Then He returned to the disciples and said, “Are you still sleeping and resting? Look, the hour is near, and the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners. 46 Rise, let us go! See, My betrayer is approaching!”

What is the context?

After warning that His disciples will scatter, Jesus brings them to Gethsemane. He leaves some sitting while He goes to pray, taking Peter and the two sons of Zebedee with Him. He tells the three that His sorrow reaches to the point of death and asks them to remain and keep watch with Him.

Jesus goes farther, falls facedown, and asks the Father whether the cup can pass from Him. In the same prayer He submits His will to the Father's. Cup is ποτήριον, potērion (poh-TAY-ree-on), a drinking vessel also used as a picture of what someone must undergo. Thayer's entry specifically places this verse among uses referring to Christ's suffering. In John 18:11, Jesus likewise describes accepting the cup the Father has given Him.

The disciples repeatedly fall asleep. Jesus calls them to watch and pray so they will not enter temptation, acknowledging both their willing spirit and their bodily weakness. This follows their strong promises of loyalty and comes just before the arrest that will test them.

Jesus prays a second and third time. The second prayer accepts that if the cup cannot pass without His drinking it, the Father's will is to be done. Matthew says the third repeats the same thing. He then announces the approaching betrayal and rises to meet it. His anguish is real, and so is His willing movement toward what lies ahead.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as real human anguish joined with Jesus' willing obedience, rather than either pretend suffering or sinful rebellion. The Catholic Catechism, 612, and the Westminster Confession, 8.4, both affirm His willing acceptance of suffering for our salvation.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The cup belongs to Scripture's larger account of judgment and rescue. Psalm 75:7–8 pictures the wicked drinking from the LORD's cup, while Isaiah 53:4–6 describes the servant bearing others' iniquities and the punishment that brings peace. 1 Peter 2:20–25 explicitly applies the sin-bearing servant's suffering to Christ, who entrusts Himself to the righteous Judge. This supports the connection between His suffering and salvation without making the Greek word cup alone carry the whole explanation.

Hebrews 5:7–10 speaks of Jesus' prayers with cries and tears, His obedience through suffering, and His becoming the source of eternal salvation. Hebrews 4:14–16 then gives comfort to people who struggle: their high priest understands weakness, has remained without sin, and invites them to seek mercy and help. Gethsemane belongs to the saving obedience of the Son who also understands the people He helps.

What is our position?

What Does Prayer Look Like? reads Gethsemane as a model of honest surrender. Jesus brings the real cost of His suffering before the Father and places the outcome entirely in His hands. Expressing grief is not treated as a failure of faith.

The study also emphasizes that Jesus prays three times. Continued asking can belong with surrender when the Father's will governs it. Prayer and Faith likewise reads this scene as prayer rooted in trust and submission to God, rather than a way to secure the outcome we choose. These studies supply our position on prayer here; they do not make every detail of Jesus' suffering a claim about what a believer will experience.
