Passage

1 Corinthians 13:1-3

Why aren’t impressive gifts and sacrifices enough?

Berean Standard Bible

1 If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am only a ringing gong or a clanging cymbal. 2 If I have the gift of prophecy and can fathom all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have absolute faith so as to move mountains, but have not love, I am nothing. 3 If I give all I possess to the poor and exult in the surrender of my body, but have not love, I gain nothing.

What is the context?

Paul’s description of love continues his correction of the church’s use of gifts. He imagines speech of the greatest possible reach, prophecy and knowledge of the widest possible scope, extraordinary faith, and extreme generosity or bodily sacrifice. None supplies what is missing if love is absent.

The repeated result is devastating: impressive speech becomes noise, the gifted person is nothing, and the giver gains nothing. Paul is not denying the value of speaking, knowing, believing or giving. He is exposing the failure of those activities when separated from love.

The BSB’s wording in verse 3 refers to exulting in the surrender of one’s body—taking pride in that surrender. Its footnote records the alternative reading about being burned. The main warning is plain in either case: even an extraordinary sacrifice does not make love unnecessary.

This section therefore asks a question that external results alone cannot answer: are other people being loved? Gifts and costly actions can attract admiration while leaving that central requirement unmet.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand Paul’s examples as showing the necessity of love, rather than dismissing service or gifts. They differ over the reference to the tongues of angels.

Calvin’s comment on verse 1 treats it as deliberately heightened language: even the most extraordinary speech would be worthless without love. The Assemblies of God’s paper on baptism in the Holy Spirit allows that Spirit-inspired speech may include heavenly or angelic language. That question does not change Paul’s stated test: love remains necessary.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In John 13:34–35, Jesus gives His disciples the command to love one another as He has loved them. Their love will identify them as His disciples.

Paul’s warning belongs within that purpose. The church is to make its Lord known through a shared life shaped by His love. Spectacular ability or sacrifice cannot substitute for the very quality Jesus says will mark His people.
