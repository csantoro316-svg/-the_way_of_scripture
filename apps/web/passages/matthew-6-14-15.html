Passage

Matthew 6:14-15

Receiving and Extending Forgiveness

Berean Standard Bible

14 For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. 15 But if you do not forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive yours.

What is the context?

Jesus returns to the request for forgiveness in the prayer He has just taught. In verse 12 the speaker asks the Father to forgive debts while also forgiving debtors. These two verses explain the seriousness of that connection: receiving mercy and extending mercy cannot be treated as unrelated matters.

The debts in the prayer represent sins, as the related prayer in Luke 11:4 makes explicit. Here Jesus speaks of people's trespasses. The concern here is wrong done against another person.

The warning addresses the person asking for forgiveness. Jesus does not describe mercy as something one may receive while deliberately withholding it from everyone else. He presents both sides of the relationship plainly, including the consequence of refusing to forgive.

A later illustration develops the same concern. In Matthew 18:21–35, a servant is forgiven a huge debt but refuses mercy to a fellow servant. Jesus ends by requiring forgiveness from the heart. Christians agree on the seriousness of that call, while differing over how these warnings relate to a believer's standing before God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand forgiveness of others as essential to a life that receives God's mercy. They differ in how they describe the consequences of a believer's refusal to forgive.

In the Reformed tradition, the Westminster Confession, 11.5, teaches that those God has declared righteous through Christ do not lose that standing, yet can fall under His disapproval as their Father until they repent, confess, and seek pardon. The Westminster Larger Catechism, question 194, connects asking for pardon with forgiveness of others as a reason for confidence, while grounding pardon in Christ's work and God's grace.

Catholic teaching says God's mercy cannot enter a heart that refuses to forgive; it also directs the person who finds forgiveness difficult to the Holy Spirit's help. See the Compendium, questions 594–595. The call concerns a changed heart, including mercy toward enemies.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, article XII, allow for departing from grace after receiving it and returning through repentance and God's help. This differs from Westminster's teaching that God continues to accept those He has declared righteous through Christ. Wesley's note on Matthew 6:12 asks for forgiveness of sins through Christ's blood while God enables the person praying to forgive others. These are descriptions of the cited teachings, rather than a claim that every member explains the warning in the same way.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Forgiving others is connected with the forgiveness God gives through Christ. Ephesians 4:31–32 moves from putting away bitterness and malice to kindness and forgiveness, with God's forgiveness in Christ as the pattern.

Colossians 3:12–15 likewise calls people loved by God to bear with one another and forgive as the Lord forgave them. It places that conduct within the peace and unity of one body. God's mercy restores people to Himself and is meant to reshape their life together. This gives the warning a place within His purpose of forming a people who live by the mercy they have received.
