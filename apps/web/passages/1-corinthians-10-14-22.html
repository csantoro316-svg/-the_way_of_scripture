Passage

1 Corinthians 10:14-22

Why can’t believers share in both kinds of worship?

Berean Standard Bible

14 Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry. 15 I speak to reasonable people; judge for yourselves what I say. 16 Is not the cup of blessing that we bless a participation in the blood of Christ? And is not the bread that we break a participation in the body of Christ? 17 Because there is one loaf, we who are many are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf. 18 Consider the people of Israel: Are not those who eat the sacrifices fellow partakers in the altar?

19 Am I suggesting, then, that food sacrificed to an idol is anything, or that an idol is anything? 20 No, but the sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons, not to God. And I do not want you to be participants with demons. 21 You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons too; you cannot partake in the table of the Lord and the table of demons too. 22 Are we trying to provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than He?

What is the context?

Paul’s instruction is direct: flee idolatry. Knowing that an idol is not a real god does not make joining its worship harmless. He asks the Corinthians to consider what sharing a religious meal means.

The cup and bread of the Lord’s Supper express participation in Christ’s blood and body. The one loaf also expresses the unity of the many people who share it. Paul then points to Israel’s sacrifices: those who eat share in the altar. His concern reaches beyond what food is made of to the relationship in which people participate.

He is not taking back his teaching that an idol has no divine power. He is saying that pagan sacrifices involve demons rather than God, and he does not want believers to participate with them. Deuteronomy 32:16–21 already connects sacrifices to demons with provoking God’s jealousy.

This gives a clear boundary to Christian freedom. A meal that joins people in idol worship is different from buying food at a market or accepting an ordinary invitation, which Paul addresses next. Belonging to Christ cannot be treated as compatible with sharing the worship of His rivals.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as forbidding participation in idol worship. They differ over how believers participate in Christ’s body and blood in the Lord’s Supper.

The Catholic Church teaches that Christ is truly present and that the bread and wine become His body and blood; see Catechism 1374–1377. Lutheran teaching likewise affirms receiving Christ’s actual body and blood under the bread and wine; see Luther’s Small Catechism. That statement does not make the two churches’ explanations identical.

The Reformed Westminster Confession 29.5–7 and the 1689 Baptist Confession 30.5–7 teach that the elements remain bread and wine while believers truly receive Christ and the benefits of His death spiritually through faith. The Methodist Articles, Article XVIII, also describe receiving Christ spiritually through faith. These teachings go beyond remembering an absent person; they should not be reduced to the claim that nothing is received.

These are the stated positions of those documents. Individual Christians and congregations may explain the meal differently. The disagreement does not remove Paul’s shared demand for undivided loyalty to Christ.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Luke 22:19–20, Jesus gives the bread and cup in connection with His coming death and the new covenant in His blood. Paul’s description of one body formed from many people shows the communal reach of that covenant: the gift binds people to Christ and to one another.

The Lord’s table therefore belongs within God’s work of gathering a people through His Son’s death. Its meaning is not exhausted by a private moment between an individual and God. Sharing that table calls the gathered people to live as those who belong to the same Lord.
