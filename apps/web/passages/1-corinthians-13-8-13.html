Passage

1 Corinthians 13:8-13

Why is love greater than gifts that will pass away?

Berean Standard Bible

8 Love never fails. But where there are prophecies, they will cease; where there are tongues, they will be restrained; where there is knowledge, it will be dismissed. 9 For we know in part and we prophesy in part, 10 but when the perfect comes, the partial passes away.

11 When I was a child, I talked like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child. When I became a man, I set aside childish ways. 12 Now we see but a dim reflection as in a mirror; then we shall see face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I am fully known. 13 And now these three remain: faith, hope, and love; but the greatest of these is love.

What is the context?

Paul adds a further reason to make love central: love lasts. Prophecy, tongues and the knowledge he has been discussing do not have the same lasting role. They serve people whose present understanding is incomplete.

The word translated perfect is teleios (tell-AY-oss), τέλειος, which can mean complete or fully mature; see Thayer’s entry. Paul contrasts the partial with what brings that partial state to an end. The word’s meaning helps identify the contrast, but a dictionary definition alone does not settle when that happens.

He illustrates the change with growing from childhood to adulthood, then with seeing an indirect reflection rather than face to face. The Greek word for mirror really does mean a mirror (Thayer). The picture emphasizes the limits of present understanding and the greater clarity to come; it does not promise that a human being will become all-knowing as God is.

Faith, hope and love remain central, with love called the greatest. This completes Paul’s correction of a church tempted to make gifts its chief measure of importance. Whatever their usefulness now, they cannot replace the love that gives their use its proper character.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Many Christians understand the perfect as the fullness of life with God, rather than the completion of a book. Calvin’s comment on verse 10 looks toward perfection fully manifested at the day of judgment; Wesley’s notes on verses 8–12 likewise read the contrast in the light of life beyond the present age.

A different reading identifies the perfect with completed New Testament revelation. In an article published by La Vista Church of Christ, Dylan Steward reasons that the partial revelations give way to the complete revelation, and understands the mirror as God’s word making people known to themselves. This is that author’s reading, not a position that can be assigned to every Church of Christ congregation.

Belief that some gifts have ceased does not always depend on that second reading. The Grace Community Church statement on tongues understands the perfect as the eternal state, while arguing separately that tongues ceased earlier. The timing of the perfect and the timing of every individual gift are therefore distinct questions within the discussion. The shared point is that gifts do not have love’s lasting place.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

1 John 3:1–3 connects the Father’s love with the future of His children: when Christ appears, they will be like Him and see Him as He is. That hope is meant to lead them toward purity now.

Romans 8:18–30 also places present weakness and waiting within God’s purpose to bring His children to glory and conform them to His Son. The lasting love Paul describes belongs within that larger goal. Learning to love faithfully now is consistent with the life God is preparing for His people, beyond the limits and needs of the present.
