Passage

Ezekiel 4:1-8

What does the miniature siege say about Jerusalem?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Now you, son of man, take a brick, place it before you, and draw on it the city of Jerusalem. 2 Then lay siege against it: Construct a siege wall, build a ramp to it, set up camps against it, and place battering rams around it on all sides. 3 Then take an iron plate and set it up as an iron wall between yourself and the city. Turn your face toward it so that it is under siege, and besiege it. This will be a sign to the house of Israel.

4 Then lie down on your left side and place the iniquity of the house of Israel upon yourself. You are to bear their iniquity for the number of days you lie on your side. 5 For I have assigned to you 390 days, according to the number of years of their iniquity. So you shall bear the iniquity of the house of Israel. 6 When you have completed these days, lie down again, but on your right side, and bear the iniquity of the house of Judah. I have assigned to you 40 days, a day for each year. 7 You must turn your face toward the siege of Jerusalem with your arm bared, and prophesy against it. 8 Now behold, I will tie you up with ropes so you cannot turn from side to side until you have finished the days of your siege.

What is the context?

Ezekiel’s first sign uses a brick with Jerusalem drawn on it. Around the pictured city he is to arrange siege works, camps, a ramp and battering rams. An iron plate stands between him and the city as he turns his face against it.

The scene gives a visible warning of a siege still to come. The earlier removal of Jehoiachin and the captives is recorded in Second Kings 24:10–17; the later siege, famine and destruction of the temple appear in 25:1–12. The exiles’ displacement has not ended the crisis facing Jerusalem.

Ezekiel must also lie on his left side for 390 days for Israel and on his right for 40 days for Judah. Each day represents a year. His bared arm, face turned toward the city and inability to turn aside make the warning persistent and unmistakable.

The Hebrew noun עָוֹן, avon (ah-VONE), can refer to wrongdoing, its guilt or its punishment. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes Ezekiel 4 among its uses concerning the burden of punishment. In this enacted sign, the prophet bears the weight of what the people’s iniquity has brought.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley understand the sign as a warning of Jerusalem’s siege and the consequences of the people’s sin. They connect the numbered days with years of wrongdoing and God’s long patience before judgment.

Wesley proposes connections with the period beginning in Solomon’s reign and with the renewal of the covenant under Josiah. He also understands the action as represented in a vision.

The USCCB notes instead emphasize the periods of exile for Israel and Judah. These explanations differ over the precise time spans and how the actions occurred. The passage clearly gives the day-for-year correspondence and connects the sign with the people’s iniquity; it does not supply a complete dated timeline.
