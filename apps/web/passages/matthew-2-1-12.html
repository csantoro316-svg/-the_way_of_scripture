Passage

Matthew 2:1-12

The Magi Seek the King

Berean Standard Bible

1 After Jesus was born in Bethlehem in Judea, during the time of King Herod, Magi from the east arrived in Jerusalem, 2 asking, “Where is the One who has been born King of the Jews? We saw His star in the east and have come to worship Him.”

3 When King Herod heard this, he was disturbed, and all Jerusalem with him. 4 And when he had assembled all the chief priests and scribes of the people, he asked them where the Christ was to be born. 5 “In Bethlehem in Judea,” they replied, “for this is what the prophet has written: 6 ‘But you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah, are by no means least among the rulers of Judah, for out of you will come a ruler who will be the shepherd of My people Israel.’”

7 Then Herod called the Magi secretly and learned from them the exact time the star had appeared. 8 And sending them to Bethlehem, he said: “Go and search carefully for the Child, and when you find Him, report to me, so that I too may go and worship Him.”

9 After they had heard the king, they went on their way, and the star they had seen in the east went ahead of them until it stood over the place where the Child was. 10 When they saw the star, they rejoiced with great delight. 11 On coming to the house, they saw the Child with His mother Mary, and they fell down and worshiped Him. Then they opened their treasures and presented Him with gifts of gold and frankincense and myrrh. 12 And having been warned in a dream not to return to Herod, they withdrew to their country by another route.

What is the context?

After the birth account, Matthew introduces visitors from the east looking for the newborn King of the Jews. Their question unsettles Herod and Jerusalem. Herod consults the chief priests and scribes, who identify Bethlehem from the prophetic Scriptures. The king who already holds power and the visitors seeking the child respond very differently to the news.

Magi is the plural of μάγος, magos (MAH-goss). Thayer's Greek Lexicon explains the term's use for eastern wise men and astrologers. Matthew tells us their direction of origin, but gives neither their names nor their number. The three kinds of gifts do not establish that there were three men, and Matthew does not call them kings.

The answer about Bethlehem draws on Micah 5:2–4, where a ruler will come from the small town and shepherd God's people. Matthew's wording also recalls the shepherding language addressed to David in 2 Samuel 5:2. The expected king belongs to the story of God's care for Israel.

Herod secretly asks when the star appeared and requests a report about the child's location, saying he too wants to worship. The following passage exposes his intention to kill Him. The visitors instead rejoice, enter the house, bow before Jesus, and give their treasures. Warned in a dream, they go home without returning to Herod.

The Greek verb translated worshiped is προσκυνέω, proskyneō (pros-koo-NEH-oh). It describes bowing or falling down in reverence. Thayer's Greek Lexicon includes both homage to someone of high rank and worship directed to God. The word's range makes the surrounding account important: these visitors have come seeking the King of the Jews.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see the visitors as an early sign that Jesus' coming matters beyond Israel. The Catholic Catechism, paragraph 528, describes their adoration of Jesus and the welcome of the nations; Wesley's notes on Matthew 2:1–2 emphasize Gentile visitors paying homage to Israel's king. The Catholic teaching presents the visit within the revelation of Jesus as Son of God and Savior; Wesley's comment on the gesture stresses the honor shown to a monarch.

The gifts have also been read in more than one way. Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.9.2, connects gold with kingship, frankincense with His being God, and myrrh with Jesus' death and burial. Wesley's note on 2:11 instead emphasizes valuable gifts suitable for a person of importance and suggests they provided help for the journey to Egypt. These are interpretations of the gifts' significance; Matthew names the gifts without explaining the visitors' intended meaning.

Early Christian teachers also differed about the star. Chrysostom, Homily 6 on Matthew, section 3, understood it as an invisible power appearing as a star; Origen, Against Celsus 1.58–59, proposed a new heavenly object comparable to a comet. These are teachers' explanations, not a single church definition of the star's physical nature. Matthew focuses on its role in leading the visitors to the child.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The visitors from outside Israel anticipate a wider welcome that becomes explicit later in the Gospel. In Matthew 8:10–11, Jesus praises a Gentile centurion's faith and speaks of people coming from east and west to share a banquet with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. At the end He sends His disciples to all nations in Matthew 28:18–20. The newborn Jewish king belongs to God's blessing reaching the nations; the story does not make one people's ancestry the measure of a faithful response.

The gifts also recall older pictures of nations honoring God's king. Psalm 72:10–17 describes distant rulers bringing tribute and nations receiving blessing. Isaiah 60:3–6 describes nations coming to the light and bringing gold and frankincense. These are Scripture-supported parallels to the visit, not proof that the magi themselves ruled kingdoms or understood every later part of Jesus' mission.

The traditional connection between myrrh and Jesus' death also has a later point of comparison: John 19:39–40 records myrrh among the spices used for His burial. Reading the two scenes together can draw our attention from the honored child to the crucified Savior. Matthew does not say that the visitors explained His future death when they gave their gifts.

What is our position?

Our study Worship as the Early Church Knew It understands the magi's action as royal homage: they bow before the child they have come to honor as King of the Jews. It does not read this scene as a statement that they already recognized Him as Savior. The study emphasizes an act of personal submission before the king, rather than a description of a gathered church service.
