Passage

Daniel 1:1-7

How do Daniel and his friends come to serve in Babylon?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim king of Judah, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came to Jerusalem and besieged it. 2 And the Lord delivered into his hand Jehoiakim king of Judah, along with some of the articles from the house of God. He carried these off to the land of Shinar, to the house of his god, where he put them in the treasury of his god.

3 Then the king ordered Ashpenaz, the chief of his court officials, to bring in some Israelites from the royal family and the nobility— 4 young men without blemish, handsome, gifted in all wisdom, knowledgeable, quick to understand, and qualified to serve in the king’s palace—and to teach them the language and literature of the Chaldeans. 5 The king assigned them daily provisions of the royal food and wine. They were to be trained for three years, after which they were to enter the king’s service. 6 Among these young men were some from Judah: Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. 7 The chief official gave them new names: To Daniel he gave the name Belteshazzar; to Hananiah, Shadrach; to Mishael, Meshach; and to Azariah, Abednego.

What is the context?

Daniel’s story begins with Jerusalem under siege and the king of Judah delivered into Nebuchadnezzar’s hand. Some articles from the temple are carried to the treasury of the Babylonian king’s god. The opening attributes the defeat to the Lord’s action, rather than presenting it as proof that Babylon’s god has overcome Him.

Young men from Israel’s royal and noble families are selected for training in the language and literature of the Chaldeans. They are to receive the king’s food and wine and prepare for three years to enter his service.

Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah are among them. They receive new names—Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego—and a new setting in which to live faithfully. Exile changes their surroundings and their responsibilities, but does not remove them from the Lord’s care.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley understand the education and new names as bringing the young men into the king’s service and Babylon’s religious world. Their reading emphasizes the challenge of remaining faithful to the Lord within that setting.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A helpful parallel is the account of Joseph and Moses in Acts 7:9–22. Joseph is sold into Egypt, yet God gives him favor and wisdom before Pharaoh and uses his position when famine threatens his family. Moses is raised by Pharaoh’s daughter and educated in Egyptian wisdom.

These earlier stories do not make captivity good in itself or explain every person’s suffering. They show God’s work reaching into foreign courts and difficult circumstances. Daniel’s training belongs to a similar pattern: displacement does not prevent God from preparing someone to serve His purposes.
