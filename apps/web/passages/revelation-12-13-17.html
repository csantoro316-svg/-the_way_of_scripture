Passage

Revelation 12:13-17

How does God protect the woman when the dragon pursues her?

Berean Standard Bible

13 And when the dragon saw that he had been thrown to the earth, he pursued the woman who had given birth to the male child. 14 But the woman was given two wings of a great eagle to fly from the presence of the serpent to her place in the wilderness, where she was nourished for a time, and times, and half a time.

15 Then from his mouth the serpent spewed water like a river to overtake the woman and sweep her away in the torrent. 16 But the earth helped the woman and opened its mouth to swallow up the river that the dragon had poured from his mouth. 17 And the dragon was enraged at the woman and went to make war with the rest of her children, who keep the commandments of God and hold to the testimony of Jesus. And the dragon stood on the shore of the sea.

What is the context?

Thrown down to the earth, the dragon turns against the woman who bore the child. She receives the wings of a great eagle and escapes into the wilderness, where she is nourished away from the serpent for a time, times and half a time. This develops the earlier account of her flight and care in verse 6.

The serpent sends water like a river after her, seeking to sweep her away. The earth helps her by opening its mouth and swallowing the river. The attack is powerful, but it fails to remove the protection given to her.

The dragon then makes war on the rest of her children. John describes them as people who keep God’s commandments and hold to the testimony of Jesus. Both descriptions belong to the group named in the verse. Protection of the woman does not bring an end to all suffering among those associated with her.

The BSB includes the dragon’s position on the shore of the sea at the end of verse 17. That closing detail prepares for the next chapter’s beast rising from the sea. Some Bibles number the shoreline sentence separately as verse 18.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The woman’s identity continues to shape the explanation. Walvoord understands God preserving Israel during future persecution, with the remaining children especially identified as Jewish believers in Jesus. Wesley understands the protection and attacks through the history of the church, including Christians living under hostile rulers.

The details of the escape are also read differently. Wesley connects the eagle’s wings with the help of imperial powers. Walvoord favors a symbolic understanding of the flood as the enemy’s attempt to destroy Israel. The USCCB notes understand the eagle as a picture of swift divine help and distinguish God’s preservation of the church from the suffering individual Christians may endure.

The text names no modern aircraft, country or desert refuge. Proposed locations and political identifications should therefore be presented as interpretations. What is clear in the scene is the failure of the dragon’s attempt to sweep away the woman, followed by his continuing hostility toward her children.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The eagle’s wings have a strong parallel in Exodus 19:3–6. God describes bringing Israel out of Egypt as carrying His people on eagles’ wings and bringing them to Himself. Revelation reuses that recognizable picture of rescue as a woman escapes a hostile pursuer and is sustained in the wilderness.

The parallel connects the woman’s preservation with God’s earlier rescue and care for His people. Yet the final verse also keeps persecution in view. God’s larger purpose is not defeated by the enemy, even while His people face danger. The vision holds protection and costly faithfulness together as the story moves toward the dragon’s further attempts to oppose God.
