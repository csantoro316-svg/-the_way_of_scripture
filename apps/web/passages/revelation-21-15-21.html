Passage

Revelation 21:15-21

What do the city’s measurements and precious stones show?

Berean Standard Bible

15 The angel who spoke with me had a golden measuring rod to measure the city and its gates and walls. 16 The city lies foursquare, with its width the same as its length. And he measured the city with the rod, and all its dimensions were equal—12,000 stadia in length and width and height. 17 And he measured its wall to be 144 cubits, by the human measure the angel was using.

18 The wall was made of jasper, and the city itself of pure gold, as pure as glass. 19 The foundations of the city walls were adorned with every kind of precious stone: The first foundation was jasper, the second sapphire, the third chalcedony, the fourth emerald, 20 the fifth sardonyx, the sixth carnelian, the seventh chrysolite, the eighth beryl, the ninth topaz, the tenth chrysoprase, the eleventh jacinth, and the twelfth amethyst. 21 And the twelve gates were twelve pearls, with each gate consisting of a single pearl. The main street of the city was pure gold, as clear as glass.

What is the context?

The angel measures the city, its gates and its wall with a golden rod. Its length, width and height are equal: twelve thousand stadia. The wall measures one hundred and forty-four cubits, using a human measure. The repeated twelves continue the pattern of the tribes, apostles, gates and foundations.

The city is pure gold, clear like glass, and its wall is jasper. Twelve precious stones adorn its foundations. Each gate is a single pearl, and the main street is pure gold, again described as clear as glass.

John presents an abundance of beauty and order. The measurements and materials belong to a city shining with God’s glory. The description gives more attention to that glory than to the practical details of how a city of this kind would function.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ over how directly the dimensions and materials describe physical construction. Walvoord understands a real city of immense dimensions, while allowing more than one possible shape consistent with equal length, width and height. The Catholic USCCB notes emphasize completeness, the repeated twelve and the picture of the church’s beauty.

Wesley treats the gold, pearls and precious stones as figures expressing glory, while affirming a real dwelling for people with real resurrection bodies. His reading illustrates that an emphasis on the imagery’s meaning can still include a lasting place to live.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The equal length, width and height recall the inner sanctuary of Solomon’s temple in 1 Kings 6:19–22. That room was also a cube and overlaid with gold. The comparison is a scriptural parallel: the city as a whole now carries features associated with the place of God’s presence.

The jewels also recall Isaiah 54:11–14, where God promises an afflicted city foundations and walls of precious stones, established in righteousness and free from terror. Revelation’s beauty belongs with that hope of restoration.

The twelve named stones on the priest’s breastpiece in Exodus 28:17–21 provide another parallel linking precious stones and Israel’s tribes. The comparison does not require assigning a hidden meaning to every stone or matching each foundation to a named apostle.
