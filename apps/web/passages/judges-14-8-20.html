Passage

Judges 14:8-20

How does a wedding riddle become a conflict?

Berean Standard Bible

8 When Samson returned later to take her, he left the road to see the lion’s carcass, and in it was a swarm of bees, along with their honey. 9 So he scooped some honey into his hands and ate it as he went along. And when he returned to his father and mother, he gave some to them and they ate it. But he did not tell them that he had taken the honey from the lion’s carcass.

10 Then his father went to visit the woman, and Samson prepared a feast there, as was customary for the bridegroom. 11 And when the Philistines saw him, they selected thirty men to accompany him. 12 “Let me tell you a riddle,” Samson said to them. “If you can solve it for me within the seven days of the feast, I will give you thirty linen garments and thirty sets of clothes. 13 But if you cannot solve it, you must give me thirty linen garments and thirty sets of clothes.” “Tell us your riddle,” they replied. “Let us hear it.” 14 So he said to them: “Out of the eater came something to eat, and out of the strong came something sweet.” For three days they were unable to explain the riddle.

15 So on the fourth day they said to Samson’s wife, “Entice your husband to explain the riddle to us, or we will burn you and your father’s household to death. Did you invite us here to rob us?” 16 Then Samson’s wife came to him, weeping, and said, “You hate me! You do not really love me! You have posed to my people a riddle, but have not explained it to me.” “Look,” he said, “I have not even explained it to my father or mother, so why should I explain it to you?” 17 She wept the whole seven days of the feast, and finally on the seventh day, because she had pressed him so much, he told her the answer. And in turn she explained the riddle to her people. 18 Before sunset on the seventh day, the men of the city said to Samson: “What is sweeter than honey? And what is stronger than a lion?” So he said to them: “If you had not plowed with my heifer, you would not have solved my riddle!”

19 Then the Spirit of the LORD came mightily upon him, and he went down to Ashkelon, killed thirty of their men, took their apparel, and gave their clothes to those who had solved the riddle. And burning with anger, Samson returned to his father’s house, 20 and his wife was given to one of the men who had accompanied him.

What is the context?

When Samson returns, he finds bees and honey in the lion’s carcass. He eats some and shares it with his parents without telling them its source. This private experience becomes the basis of his riddle during the wedding feast: something sweet to eat has come from a strong creature that would normally be the eater.

The feast is called a מִשְׁתֶּה, mishteh (mish-TEH), a banquet associated with drinking. Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically lists this wedding feast. The word describes the occasion; the account does not explicitly report Samson drinking wine.

The wager concerns thirty linen garments and thirty sets of clothes. Unable to solve the riddle, the companions threaten to burn Samson’s wife and her father’s household to death. Under that threat, she presses Samson for the answer and passes it to her people. The danger to her family is part of the account and matters when considering her actions.

When the men give the answer, Samson recognizes that they obtained it through his wife. The Spirit of the LORD comes upon him, and he kills thirty men in Ashkelon, taking their apparel to pay the wager. He returns angrily to his father’s house, and his wife is given to one of his companions. The celebration has become a source of violence, anger, and broken relationships.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads Samson’s reply about plowing with his heifer as an image of the men using his wife to uncover what was hidden. He connects the comparison with the shared yoke of marriage. The expression identifies the means by which they obtained the answer; it need not become a general statement about women’s character.

Gill understands the attack at Ashkelon as action by a God-empowered judge against Israel’s oppressors. He takes the Spirit’s coming upon Samson as important to that reading. The text records that empowering alongside Samson’s anger, without giving a detailed description of the thirty men’s occupations or the encounter.

Henry draws attention to the riddle’s simple basis in the lion and honey, then makes a devotional comparison with Christ’s victory bringing benefit to His people. That comparison belongs to his wider reading of Scripture rather than to an explanation of the riddle supplied by Samson.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Henry’s comparison can be considered alongside the New Testament’s own teaching. First Peter 5:6–10 uses a roaring lion as an image of the devil’s danger. Hebrews 2:14–18 explains that Jesus shares our humanity and, through His death, breaks the devil’s power and frees people from slavery to the fear of death.

The honey illustration brings out a shared theme: an encounter with danger becomes an occasion of benefit. The fulfillment of the Christian hope rests in Jesus’ death and resurrection. First Corinthians 15:54–58 gives thanks for victory over death through Him and calls believers to steadfast service.
