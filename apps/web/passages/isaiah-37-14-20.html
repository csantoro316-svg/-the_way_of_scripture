Passage

Isaiah 37:14-20

What is Hezekiah asking for when he spreads out the letter?

Berean Standard Bible

14 So Hezekiah received the letter from the messengers, read it, and went up to the house of the LORD and spread it out before the LORD. 15 And Hezekiah prayed to the LORD: 16 “O LORD of Hosts, God of Israel, enthroned above the cherubim, You alone are God over all the kingdoms of the earth. You made the heavens and the earth.

17 Incline Your ear, O LORD, and hear; open Your eyes, O LORD, and see. Listen to all the words that Sennacherib has sent to defy the living God. 18 Truly, O LORD, the kings of Assyria have laid waste all these countries and their lands. 19 They have cast their gods into the fire and destroyed them, for they were not gods, but only wood and stone—the work of human hands. 20 And now, O LORD our God, save us from his hand, so that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that You alone, O LORD, are God.”

What is the context?

Hezekiah reads the letter, takes it to the house of the Lord and spreads it before Him. His prayer begins by naming God as the Lord of Hosts, the God of Israel and the Creator of heaven and earth. He alone is God over every kingdom.

The prayer does not pretend the Assyrian victories are imaginary. Hezekiah acknowledges the destruction of countries and their gods, then identifies the crucial difference: those gods were human workmanship, wood and stone.

He asks the living God to hear the defiance and save His people. The stated purpose reaches beyond Jerusalem’s relief: all the kingdoms of the earth are to know that the Lord alone is God. The letter is brought into a prayer centered on who God is and the honor of His name.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Gill explains the spread-out letter as a way of presenting the insult and the need before God, rather than supplying information God lacks. He connects the final request with the Lord making Himself known in a way the defeated idols could not.

Henry’s explanation of the parallel prayer emphasizes an appeal grounded in God’s honor. The USCCB note likewise contrasts Assyrian boasting with Hezekiah’s confession that God rules over every kingdom.

These readings make the substance of the prayer central. Spreading out a document expresses the appeal in this scene; the passage does not give the physical action an independent power.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The believers’ prayer after Peter and John are threatened in Acts 4:23–31 provides a close parallel. They begin with God as Creator, bring the rulers’ threats before Him, and ask for boldness to speak His word while He acts through Jesus.

The prayers arise in different circumstances: Hezekiah asks for the city’s rescue, while the believers ask to continue their witness. Both bring the threat under the larger truth that human rulers do not stand above God’s purpose.

In Acts, that purpose is made known through Jesus and the Spirit-empowered witness to Him. The connection broadens the meaning of deliverance: prayer seeks more than escape from fear; it seeks the work through which God makes Himself known.
