Passage

2 Samuel 21:1-9

Why does David seek to make amends to the Gibeonites?

Berean Standard Bible

1 During the reign of David there was a famine for three successive years, and David sought the face of the LORD. And the LORD said, “It is because of the blood shed by Saul and his family, because he killed the Gibeonites.” 2 At this, David summoned the Gibeonites and spoke to them. (Now the Gibeonites were not Israelites, but a remnant of the Amorites. The Israelites had taken an oath concerning them, but in his zeal for Israel and Judah, Saul had sought to kill them.) 3 So David asked the Gibeonites, “What shall I do for you? How can I make amends so that you may bless the inheritance of the LORD?”

4 The Gibeonites said to him, “We need no silver or gold from Saul or his house, nor should you put to death anyone in Israel for us.” “Whatever you ask, I will do for you,” he replied. 5 And they answered the king, “As for the man who consumed us and plotted against us to exterminate us from existing within any border of Israel, 6 let seven of his male descendants be delivered to us so that we may hang them before the LORD at Gibeah of Saul, the chosen of the LORD.” “I will give them to you,” said the king.

7 Now the king spared Mephibosheth son of Jonathan, the son of Saul, because of the oath before the LORD between David and Jonathan son of Saul. 8 But the king took Armoni and Mephibosheth, the two sons whom Rizpah daughter of Aiah had borne to Saul, as well as the five sons whom Merab daughter of Saul had borne to Adriel son of Barzillai the Meholathite. 9 And he delivered them into the hands of the Gibeonites, and they hanged them on the hill before the LORD. So all seven of them fell together; they were put to death in the first days of the harvest, at the beginning of the barley harvest.

What is the context?

A three-year famine leads David to seek the LORD. The answer identifies bloodshed by Saul and his family against the Gibeonites, a people Israel had sworn to protect. The episode is placed during David’s reign without an exact date.

The background is Joshua 9:14–21. Although the Gibeonites deceived Israel about where they came from, Israel’s leaders made a treaty to let them live. After discovering the deception, the leaders still recognized their obligation to keep the oath.

David asks the Gibeonites how he can make amends. The Hebrew verb is kaphar, pronounced kah-FAR. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes this verse under dealing with guilt without a sacrificial rite. David’s question concerns the wrong done to this people and how it can be addressed.

The Gibeonites reject payment and ask for seven male descendants of Saul. David agrees, while sparing Jonathan’s son Mephibosheth because of his oath to Jonathan. The seven handed over include two sons of Rizpah and five sons of Merab. Rizpah’s son Mephibosheth is a different man from Jonathan’s son.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry understands Saul’s family as sharing his violent conduct. On that reading, the deaths address wrongdoing in the household as well as Saul’s own offense.

Wesley and Gill, however, describe the seven as dying for Saul’s sin rather than for personal crimes of their own. They understand the episode as an exceptional judgment authorized by God. Both infer that authorization from God’s response to the land; a separate command naming these seven men is not recorded.

That difference matters because Deuteronomy 24:16 says that children are not to be executed for their fathers’ sins. The chapter identifies the bloodguilt of Saul’s house but gives no individual account of what each of the seven did. Readers should distinguish the facts the narrator supplies from the explanations commentators offer.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

This episode belongs to the Bible’s wider concern for faithfulness to promises. Saul’s zeal for Israel does not cancel Israel’s obligation toward outsiders. David also recognizes his promise to Jonathan while dealing with the wrong done to the Gibeonites.

Matthew 5:33–37 takes truthfulness into everyday speech: Jesus teaches His followers to give a dependable yes or no. The connection is the seriousness of keeping one’s word, even when circumstances change.

That shared theme does not make this execution a pattern for settling broken promises today. It directs attention to the faithfulness owed both to God and to people who depend on what we have said.
