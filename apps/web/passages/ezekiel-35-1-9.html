Passage

Ezekiel 35:1-9

Why does Edom’s old hatred return upon it?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Moreover, the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 2 “Son of man, set your face against Mount Seir and prophesy against it, 3 and declare that this is what the Lord GOD says: Behold, I am against you, O Mount Seir. I will stretch out My hand against you and make you a desolate waste. 4 I will turn your cities into ruins, and you will become a desolation. Then you will know that I am the LORD.

5 Because you harbored an ancient hatred and delivered the Israelites over to the sword in the time of their disaster at the final stage of their punishment, 6 therefore as surely as I live, declares the Lord GOD, I will give you over to bloodshed and it will pursue you. Since you did not hate bloodshed, it will pursue you. 7 I will make Mount Seir a desolate waste and will cut off from it those who come and go. 8 I will fill its mountains with the slain; those killed by the sword will fall on your hills, in your valleys, and in all your ravines. 9 I will make you a perpetual desolation, and your cities will not be inhabited. Then you will know that I am the LORD.

What is the context?

The Lord directs Ezekiel against Mount Seir, identified at the end of the chapter with Edom. The family background reaches back to Esau, whose dwelling in Seir is recorded in Genesis 36:8–9.

The charge is specific: Edom harbored an ancient hatred and handed Israelites over to the sword during their disaster. It chose to deepen the suffering of a people already under judgment.

The Lord announces bloodshed in return for bloodshed. Seir’s cities, hills, valleys and ravines will become places of desolation and death. The repeated outcome is that Edom will know He is the Lord.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands the accusation as delight in bloodshed rather than hatred of it. Cambridge connects the enduring hostility with Edom’s participation in Israel’s calamity and its attempt to seize the land.

Henry emphasizes the seriousness of maintaining hostility toward God and His people. The judgment answers the conduct named in the passage, rather than treating descent from an ancestor as a substitute for examining what these people did.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 12:14–17 brings Esau’s story into a warning to pursue peace and holiness, guard against a root of bitterness, and refuse the godlessness that trades away a birthright for a meal.

Hebrews is addressing its own readers, rather than interpreting every detail of Edom’s judgment. The family background gives the passages a meaningful connection: what is received from God must not be treated lightly, and bitterness must not be allowed to grow into harm that spreads to others.
