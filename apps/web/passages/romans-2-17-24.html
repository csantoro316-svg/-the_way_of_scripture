Passage

Romans 2:17-24

What happens when someone teaches God’s law but does not live it?

Berean Standard Bible

17 Now you, if you call yourself a Jew; if you rely on the law and boast in God; 18 if you know His will and approve of what is superior because you are instructed by the law; 19 if you are convinced that you are a guide for the blind, a light for those in darkness, 20 an instructor of the foolish, a teacher of infants, because you have in the law the embodiment of knowledge and truth—

21 you, then, who teach others, do you not teach yourself? You who preach against stealing, do you steal? 22 You who forbid adultery, do you commit adultery? You who abhor idols, do you rob temples? 23 You who boast in the law, do you dishonor God by breaking the law? 24 As it is written: “God’s name is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you.”

What is the context?

Paul now explicitly addresses the person who calls himself a Jew, relies on the law and boasts in God. He describes the confidence of someone instructed in God’s will who sees himself as a guide and teacher for others.

The questions then turn that teaching back on the teacher. Does the person who preaches against stealing steal? Does the person who forbids adultery commit it? Does the person who rejects idols rob temples? Claiming to teach what is right cannot excuse violating it.

Paul turns the teacher’s own teaching into a test of his life. Breaking the law dishonors the God in whom he boasts. The questions address hypocrisy in the person making these claims; they are not a description of every Jewish person.

He concludes with Scripture about God’s name being blasphemed among the Gentiles. Here blasphemed means spoken against or dishonored. The failure has a consequence beyond the teacher’s reputation: it affects how others speak of God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as a warning that religious knowledge and the work of teaching increase responsibility rather than cancel it. The questions confront hypocrisy in the person making the claims; they should not become a stereotype about an entire people. Christian teachers must also hear the challenge they apply to others.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Paul explicitly uses Isaiah 52:5. In that setting, God’s name is blasphemed amid His people’s humiliation and their rulers’ taunts. Paul applies the language of dishonor to people who claim God’s law while breaking it. The connection is real, though the two settings are not identical.

Ezekiel 36:20–27 joins concern for God’s name among the nations with His promise to cleanse His people, give them a new heart and put His Spirit within them. The larger answer to dishonor is more than protecting a religious reputation: it includes the inward renewal God promises to bring.
