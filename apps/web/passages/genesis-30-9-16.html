Passage

Genesis 30:9-16

Gad and Asher

Berean Standard Bible

9 When Leah saw that she had stopped having children, she gave her servant Zilpah to Jacob as a wife. 10 And Leah’s servant Zilpah bore Jacob a son. 11 Then Leah said, “How fortunate!” So she named him Gad.

12 When Leah’s servant Zilpah bore Jacob a second son, 13 Leah said, “How happy I am! For the women call me happy.” So she named him Asher.

14 Now during the wheat harvest, Reuben went out and found some mandrakes in the field. When he brought them to his mother, Rachel begged Leah, “Please give me some of your son’s mandrakes.”

15 But Leah replied, “Is it not enough that you have taken away my husband? Now you want to take my son’s mandrakes as well?”

“Very well,” said Rachel, “he may sleep with you tonight in exchange for your son’s mandrakes.”

16 When Jacob came in from the field that evening, Leah went out to meet him and said, “You must come with me, for I have hired you with my son’s mandrakes.” So he slept with her that night.

What is the context?

When Leah stops having children, she gives her maidservant Zilpah to Jacob as a wife. Zilpah bears Gad and Asher. Leah's words at their births express good fortune and happiness, while the growing family remains divided by the sisters' competing hopes.

The scene then moves to the wheat harvest. Reuben finds mandrakes and brings them to Leah, and Rachel asks for some. Leah answers with her deeper grievance: Rachel already has the husband whose affection she wants. Their dispute is about much more than plants.

Rachel offers Leah a night with Jacob in exchange for the mandrakes. Leah meets him when he returns from the field and tells him of the arrangement, and he spends the night with her. The next passage expressly credits God with hearing Leah and enabling her to conceive. This exchange does not establish that the plants themselves cause a pregnancy.

How is this passage commonly understood?

A common Christian reading sees rivalry spreading as Leah follows Rachel's example and the sisters bargain over Jacob's company. Wesley's notes on Genesis 30:9, 14 emphasize the damage of that competition, without treating the bargain as a healthy pattern for marriage.
