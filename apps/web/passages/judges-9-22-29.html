Passage

Judges 9:22-29

Why does the alliance begin to break apart?

Berean Standard Bible

22 After Abimelech had reigned over Israel for three years, 23 God sent a spirit of animosity between Abimelech and the leaders of Shechem and caused them to treat Abimelech deceitfully, 24 in order that the crime against the seventy sons of Jerubbaal might come to justice and their blood be avenged on their brother Abimelech and on the leaders of Shechem, who had helped him murder his brothers. 25 The leaders of Shechem set up an ambush against Abimelech on the hilltops, and they robbed all who passed by them on the road. So this was reported to Abimelech.

26 Meanwhile, Gaal son of Ebed came with his brothers and crossed into Shechem, and the leaders of Shechem put their confidence in him. 27 And after they had gone out into the fields, gathered grapes from their vineyards, and trodden them, they held a festival and went into the house of their god; and as they ate and drank, they cursed Abimelech. 28 Then Gaal son of Ebed said, “Who is Abimelech, and who is Shechem, that we should serve him? Is he not the son of Jerubbaal, and is not Zebul his officer? You are to serve the men of Hamor, the father of Shechem. Why should we serve Abimelech? 29 If only this people were under my authority, I would remove Abimelech; I would say to him, ‘Muster your army and come out!’”

What is the context?

After three years, the alliance between Abimelech and Shechem’s leaders begins to collapse. The account says that God sends a spirit of animosity between them. It also gives the purpose: the murder of Gideon’s sons is to come to justice, with responsibility falling on both the killer and those who helped him.

The leaders arrange ambushes and rob travelers. Gaal arrives with his brothers and gains their confidence. During a harvest festival in the house of their god, the people curse Abimelech. Gaal challenges his right to rule, invokes Shechem’s connection with Hamor, and says he would remove Abimelech if the people were under his authority.

The motives and competing claims belong to the human conflict, while the narrator identifies a larger judgment at work. The partnership that enabled the murders is becoming a source of danger to its own members.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers explain the spirit’s action in different ways. Wesley understands God as allowing the devil to work on their minds. Gill discusses that reading and also the possibility of God giving them over to their own jealousy, hatred, and desire for revenge. The BSB’s wording, a spirit of animosity, highlights the hostility that develops.

Henry emphasizes that the people who helped Abimelech seize power are among the first to suffer under it. The central agreement is that the breakdown serves judgment on their shared guilt. The passage does not give a detailed account of the unseen means by which every suspicion or decision arises.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God’s ability to carry out His purposes amid human wrongdoing becomes especially important in the account of Jesus’ death and resurrection. Acts 2:22–24 describes the cross as within God’s plan while still holding the people who killed Jesus responsible. God raises Him from the dead.

The purpose there includes a greater rescue: Peter announces Jesus as Lord and Christ and calls his hearers to repentance and forgiveness in His name (Acts 2:36–39). Human evil is not the final word. God can accomplish judgment and salvation without making the wrongdoing itself good. James 1:13–17 also insists that God does not tempt anyone to evil.
