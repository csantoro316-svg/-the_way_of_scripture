Passage

Luke 9:18-22

Why does Peter’s confession lead into Jesus’ coming suffering?

Berean Standard Bible

18 One day as Jesus was praying in private and the disciples were with Him, He questioned them: “Who do the crowds say I am?” 19 They replied, “Some say John the Baptist; others say Elijah; and still others, that a prophet of old has arisen.” 20 “But what about you?” Jesus asked. “Who do you say I am?” Peter answered, “The Christ of God.”

21 Jesus strictly warned them not to tell this to anyone. 22 “The Son of Man must suffer many things,” He said. “He must be rejected by the elders, chief priests, and scribes, and He must be killed and on the third day be raised to life.”

What is the context?

While Jesus is praying in private with His disciples present, He asks who the crowds say He is. They repeat the reports about John, Elijah and a prophet from long ago. He then asks what they themselves say.

Peter answers that Jesus is the Christ of God. Jesus strictly warns them not to spread this and immediately explains the path ahead: the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected by the elders, chief priests and scribes, be killed and be raised on the third day.

The explanation gives the disciples something essential to hear alongside the confession. Recognizing Him as God’s Christ includes learning that His mission will pass through rejection and death into resurrection. The next teaching will address what following Him will mean for them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly understand the confession and the announcement together: Jesus is God’s Messiah, and His mission includes suffering, death and resurrection. The Catholic Catechism places this announcement at the turning point after Peter’s confession, while the Gospel itself makes the coming suffering explicit. (Catechism 554–555; Luke 9:20–22)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah’s servant suffers despite His innocence, bears the sins of others and brings them peace. The announcement here prepares the disciples to recognize that God’s saving work includes such suffering. This is a Scriptural connection with the servant’s mission, not an assumption that rejection means God’s purpose has failed. (Isaiah 53:4–12)

After the resurrection, Peter proclaims that Jesus was handed over according to God’s purpose, put to death through human actions and raised by God. Acts holds those truths together. The death and resurrection Jesus announces here stand within God’s saving purpose while those involved in killing Him remain responsible for their acts. (Acts 2:22–24)

What is our position?

What Does Prayer Look Like? draws on the parallel announcement in Matthew 16:21 when discussing Jesus’ prayer in Gethsemane. He already knows the path ahead, yet its cost remains real. The study emphasizes honest prayer governed by surrender to the Father’s will: knowing God’s purpose does not make obedience painless. This supplies our position on His awareness of the coming suffering, while a fuller position on the whole passage remains open.
