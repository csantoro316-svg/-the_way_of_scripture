Passage

John 6:48-51

How is the living bread different from manna?

Berean Standard Bible

48 I am the bread of life. 49 Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, yet they died. 50 This is the bread that comes down from heaven, so that anyone may eat of it and not die. 51 I am the living bread that came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever. And this bread, which I will give for the life of the world, is My flesh.”

What is the context?

Jesus has spoken of the Father drawing and teaching people, and has promised eternal life to the believer. He now contrasts Himself as living bread with the manna eaten in the wilderness.

Those ancestors died despite the food they received. Jesus promises lasting life through Himself and names His flesh as the bread given for the life of the world. The following verses develop the language of receiving His flesh and blood. Read John 6:41–51 in context.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readers understand these words as a call to receive life from Christ through His self-giving. Where traditions differ is how the language of eating and drinking relates to Communion, also called the Lord’s Supper or Eucharist.

The Catholic Church reads the discourse as an announcement of the Eucharist and teaches that the bread and wine become Christ’s body and blood, with the whole Christ truly present. (Catechism §1336; Catechism §§1374–1376)

The Lutheran Formula of Concord explains John 6 as receiving Christ through faith, which happens through hearing the gospel and also in Communion. Lutheran teaching separately affirms that Christ’s true body and blood are received in and under the bread and wine. (Formula of Concord VII.61–65; Large Catechism, Altar §§8–14)

Calvin reads John 6 as receiving Christ continually through faith, rather than as referring directly to the Communion rite. The Westminster and Baptist confessions nevertheless teach that believers truly receive spiritual nourishment from Christ in the meal; their account is more than simply recalling a distant event. (Calvin on John 6:53; Westminster Confession 29.5–7; Baptist Confession 30.1, 7)

Wesley connects the words directly with Christ’s suffering and receiving Him through faith, and only remotely, if at all, with the Lord’s Supper. This is his interpretation of the passage, rather than a denial that Christians should share the meal. (Wesley on John 6:51–53)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews describes Christ entering God’s presence by His own blood, securing redemption and cleansing the conscience so that people may serve the living God. His flesh and blood given for life belong alongside that wider account of His self-offering. (Hebrews 9:11–14)

Paul connects the shared cup and bread with participation in Christ and with many believers becoming one body. He also says that the meal proclaims the Lord’s death until He comes. The gift of life through Christ therefore reaches into a people joined to Him and to one another, remembering His death while awaiting His return. (1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 1 Corinthians 11:23–26)

What is our position?

Our study Prayer and Faith reads Jesus as the living bread to whom the manna pointed. The manna sustained life for a time; Christ Himself gives lasting life. The study connects receiving that life with faith and continued dependence on Him.
