Passage

Nehemiah 7:1-5

Why does a finished wall still leave work to do?

Berean Standard Bible

1 When the wall had been rebuilt and I had set the doors in place, the gatekeepers, singers, and Levites were appointed. 2 Then I put my brother Hanani in charge of Jerusalem, along with Hananiah the commander of the fortress, because he was a faithful man who feared God more than most. 3 And I told them, “Do not open the gates of Jerusalem until the sun is hot. While the guards are on duty, keep the doors shut and securely fastened. And appoint the residents of Jerusalem as guards, some at their posts and some at their own homes.”

4 Now the city was large and spacious, but there were few people in it, and the houses had not yet been rebuilt. 5 Then my God put it into my heart to assemble the nobles, the officials, and the people to be enrolled by genealogy. I found the genealogical register of those who had first returned, and I found the following written in it:

What is the context?

With the wall rebuilt and its doors installed, Nehemiah appoints gatekeepers, singers and Levites. He entrusts responsibility for Jerusalem to Hanani and Hananiah, describing Hananiah as faithful and especially reverent toward God.

The instructions for opening, closing and guarding the gates make the city’s protection an ongoing responsibility. Residents are to serve as guards, including near their own homes.

The city is spacious, but its population is small and houses remain unbuilt. Nehemiah says God puts it into his heart to assemble the people for registration by family. He finds the register of those who returned at the beginning; the long list that follows comes from that earlier return.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes the choice of trustworthy people and the continuing need for care after construction. Gill relates the small population to the many returnees who lived in other towns. Both read the register as a record of the first return, which Nehemiah finds useful for the needs of the community in his own time.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jerusalem’s restored defenses meet an immediate need, but the wider biblical hope reaches beyond a city that must keep watch against attack. Revelation 21:1–4, 22–27 describes the new Jerusalem where God dwells with His people, death and mourning are gone, and the gates never close because there is no night.

The connection is the larger hope of God living with His people in lasting safety. Nehemiah’s guards and gate instructions show what protection requires in a threatened city; Revelation places that hope’s fulfillment in God’s presence and the Lamb.
