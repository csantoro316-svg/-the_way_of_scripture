Passage

Genesis 49:1-28

Jacob Blesses His Sons

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then Jacob called for his sons and said, “Gather around so that I can tell you what will happen to you in the days to come:

2 Come together and listen, O sons of Jacob;

listen to your father Israel.

3 Reuben, you are my firstborn, my might,

and the beginning of my strength,

excelling in honor,

excelling in power.

4 Uncontrolled as the waters,

you will no longer excel,

because you went up to your father’s bed,

onto my couch, and defiled it.

5 Simeon and Levi are brothers;

their swords are weapons of violence.

6 May I never enter their council;

may I never join their assembly.

For they kill men in their anger,

and hamstring oxen on a whim.

7 Cursed be their anger, for it is strong,

and their wrath, for it is cruel!

I will disperse them in Jacob

and scatter them in Israel.

8 Judah, your brothers shall praise you.

Your hand shall be on the necks of your enemies;

your father’s sons shall bow down to you.

9 Judah is a young lion—

my son, you return from the prey.

Like a lion he crouches and lies down;

like a lioness, who dares to rouse him?

10 The scepter will not depart from Judah,

nor the staff from between his feet,

until Shiloh comes

and the allegiance of the nations is his.

11 He ties his donkey to the vine,

his colt to the choicest branch.

He washes his garments in wine,

his robes in the blood of grapes.

12 His eyes are darker than wine,

and his teeth are whiter than milk.

13 Zebulun shall dwell by the seashore

and become a harbor for ships;

his border shall extend to Sidon.

14 Issachar is a strong donkey,

lying down between the sheepfolds.

15 He saw that his resting place was good

and that his land was pleasant,

so he bent his shoulder to the burden

and submitted to labor as a servant.

16 Dan shall provide justice for his people

as one of the tribes of Israel.

17 He will be a snake by the road,

a viper in the path

that bites the horse’s heels

so that its rider tumbles backward.

18 I await Your salvation, O LORD.

19 Gad will be attacked by raiders,

but he will attack their heels.

20 Asher’s food will be rich;

he shall provide royal delicacies.

21 Naphtali is a doe set free

that bears beautiful fawns.

22 Joseph is a fruitful vine—

a fruitful vine by a spring,

whose branches scale the wall.

23 The archers attacked him with bitterness;

they aimed at him in hostility.

24 Yet he steadied his bow,

and his strong arms were tempered

by the hands of the Mighty One of Jacob,

in the name of the Shepherd, the Rock of Israel,

25 by the God of your father who helps you,

and by the Almighty who blesses you,

with blessings of the heavens above,

with blessings of the depths below,

with blessings of the breasts and womb.

26 The blessings of your father have surpassed

the blessings of the ancient mountains

and the bounty of the everlasting hills.

May they rest on the head of Joseph,

on the brow of the prince of his brothers.

27 Benjamin is a ravenous wolf;

in the morning he devours the prey,

in the evening he divides the plunder.”

28 These are the tribes of Israel, twelve in all, and this was what their father said to them. He blessed them, and he blessed each one with a suitable blessing.

What is the context?

Jacob gathers his sons to speak about their future. The closing verse identifies them as the twelve tribes of Israel, so these sayings look beyond the men in the room to the peoples bearing their names. The words include rebuke, warning, promise, and blessing; they are not simply compliments offered at a farewell.

Reuben's loss of the leading place among his brothers is tied directly to his violation of his father's bed. Simeon and Levi are addressed together because of their violent anger, recalling the attack on Shechem. Jacob rejects their cruelty and speaks of their scattering. Later, Joshua 19:1 places Simeon's inheritance within Judah, while Deuteronomy 10:8–9 describes Levi's distinct calling to service rather than an ordinary territorial share.

Judah receives the promise of leadership. His brothers will praise and bow before him; the lion, scepter, and staff express strength and rule. The allegiance of nations extends the picture beyond the immediate household. Images of abundant wine and milk describe rich provision alongside that authority.

The brief sayings for Zebulun and Issachar speak of the sea, trade, productive land, and labor. Dan's saying combines justice with the image of a snake overturning a rider. In the middle of these words, Jacob voices his dependence on the LORD's salvation. Gad faces raids and counters them; Asher supplies rich food; Naphtali is pictured as a free doe bearing beautiful fawns.

Joseph's blessing recalls hostility overcome through the help of the Mighty One of Jacob. The fruitful vine, strong arms, shepherd, and rock bring together his hard experience and the care that has sustained him. The blessing asks for abundant life and fruitfulness. Benjamin's wolf image describes a forceful tribe taking and dividing spoil.

The poem links remembered actions with future tribal life, using vivid images that are sometimes difficult to interpret in detail. Its main movements remain clear: wrongdoing has consequences, leadership is associated with Judah, Joseph is richly blessed, and the family must depend on the God whose help Jacob names.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read these sayings as looking forward to the tribes' future, with Judah's royal promise reaching toward Christ. Wesley's notes on Genesis 49:1, 8–10 connect the rule of Judah, David's line, and the Messiah. His explanations of individual images are commentary, not a requirement that every detail have only one possible historical application.

The BSB retains שִׁילֹה, šîlōh (shee-LOH), in verse 10. Its exact meaning is debated. Brown–Driver–Briggs discusses several readings and favors a connection with the one to whom something belongs, while acknowledging uncertainty in the exact translation. The US Catholic bishops' note on Genesis 49:10 likewise discusses different readings while emphasizing Judah and the Davidic dynasty.

The messianic reading also has a Jewish history. Rashi's own commentary on Genesis 49:10 understands Shiloh as the King Messiah to whom the kingdom belongs. Christians identify the promised ruler with Jesus; Rashi's Jewish reading should be recognized on its own terms rather than presented as a Christian identification.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 5:5–10 directly takes up Judah's lion imagery in its description of Jesus, the Lion of the tribe of Judah and Root of David. The vision then shows Him as the slain Lamb whose blood purchases people for God from every tribe, language, people, and nation. The royal promise reaches its Christian fulfillment through the One whose victory includes His self-giving death.

The chapter also keeps different family roles in view. 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 distinguishes Joseph's birthright from the ruler coming through Judah. God's purpose moves through the whole family, with particular responsibilities within it. The warnings against uncontrolled desire and violent anger remain part of that story; receiving a place in the promise does not make the family's wrongdoing harmless.
