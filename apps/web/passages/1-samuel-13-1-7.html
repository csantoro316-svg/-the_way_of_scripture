Passage

1 Samuel 13:1-7

What happens when the Philistines answer Jonathan’s attack?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Saul was thirty years old when he became king, and he reigned over Israel forty-two years. 2 He chose for himself three thousand men of Israel: Two thousand were with Saul at Michmash and in the hill country of Bethel, and a thousand were with Jonathan in Gibeah of Benjamin. And the rest of the troops he sent away, each to his own home. 3 Then Jonathan attacked the Philistine outpost at Geba, and the Philistines heard about it. So Saul blew the ram’s horn throughout the land, saying, “Let the Hebrews hear!” 4 And all Israel heard the news: “Saul has attacked an outpost of the Philistines, and now Israel has become a stench to the Philistines!” Then the people were summoned to join Saul at Gilgal.

5 Now the Philistines assembled to fight against Israel with three thousand chariots, six thousand horsemen, and troops as numerous as the sand on the seashore. They went up and camped at Michmash, east of Beth-aven. 6 Seeing that they were in danger because their troops were hard-pressed, the men of Israel hid in caves and thickets, among the rocks, and in cellars and cisterns. 7 Some Hebrews even crossed the Jordan into the land of Gad and Gilead. Saul, however, remained at Gilgal, and all his troops were quaking in fear.

What is the context?

Saul retains three thousand men, dividing them between himself and Jonathan, and sends the rest home. Jonathan attacks a Philistine outpost at Geba. The news spreads as Saul summons Israel to Gilgal, with the attack being reported under the king’s name.

The Philistines assemble at Michmash with chariots, horsemen and an immense body of troops. The BSB gives three thousand chariots and six thousand horsemen. Faced with that force, Israelites hide in caves, thickets and other shelters; some cross the Jordan. Saul stays at Gilgal with people who are trembling.

The opening age and reign figures require care. The BSB footnotes identify differences in the underlying textual witnesses, while Acts 13:21 gives forty years for Saul’s reign. The BSB wording is reproduced above, but this paragraph does not establish an exact date for every event in Saul’s early rule.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads the crisis as exposing the limits of confidence in a human king: the people still need God’s help. Henry similarly emphasizes the fear and desertion surrounding Saul. Their readings keep the military danger connected with the larger question of Israel’s trust.

Translations and older commentaries differ over the opening chronology and the number of chariots. The USCCB study notes identify the textual difficulty in verse 1 and the three-thousand reading in some Greek manuscripts. These are questions about the wording transmitted, rather than a necessary division between church traditions. The quotations here follow the checked BSB, without silently replacing its numbers with those of an older commentary.
