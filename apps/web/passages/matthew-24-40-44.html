Passage

Matthew 24:40-44

One Taken, One Left: Be Ready for Your Lord

Berean Standard Bible

40 Two men will be in the field: one will be taken and the other left. 41 Two women will be grinding at the mill: one will be taken and the other left. 42 Therefore keep watch, because you do not know the day on which your Lord will come.

43 But understand this: If the homeowner had known in which watch of the night the thief was coming, he would have kept watch and would not have let his house be broken into. 44 For this reason, you also must be ready, because the Son of Man will come at an hour you do not expect.

What is the context?

Jesus continues with two scenes of ordinary work: two men in a field and two women grinding at a mill. In each pair, one is taken and the other left. He immediately draws the practical conclusion that His followers must keep watch because they do not know the day their Lord will come.

Taken comes from παραλαμβάνω, paralambanō (pah-rah-lahm-BAH-noh), to take someone with oneself or receive someone. Thayer's entry includes both companions and people taken as prisoners. The word alone does not tell us whether being taken here means rescue or judgment; that question depends on how the surrounding comparison is read.

Keep watch is γρηγορέω, grēgoreō (gray-gor-EH-oh). Thayer's entry explains its use here as staying attentive and active. Jesus illustrates the need through a homeowner who would have guarded his house if he had known when the thief was coming. The comparison concerns an unexpected arrival and the need to be ready.

The coming of the Son of Man will be at an hour they do not expect. Jesus then moves directly to a servant carrying out his master's work. That next picture makes the kind of readiness He seeks concrete: faithful conduct while waiting.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ over which person is taken. Both main readings recognize a decisive separation and the call to be ready.

Wesley on 24:40–41 understands the taken person to enter God's protection, while the other remains exposed to calamity. The US Catholic bishops' note on 24:40–41 similarly proposes being taken into the kingdom and being left for destruction. In this reading, being taken is the hopeful side of the contrast.

Walvoord on 24:40–41 reads it the other way: as the flood swept away the unprepared, the person taken is taken in judgment, while the one left enters the kingdom. He distinguishes this scene from the earlier gathering of the church that he expects before the tribulation. The different outcomes attached to the same words show why the context must be considered alongside the Greek verb.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The wider promise to Christ's people is that they will be with Him. In John 14:1–6, Jesus speaks of preparing a place and welcoming His followers into His presence. 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 joins His coming with resurrection, His people gathered to Him, and encouragement rooted in being with Him always. These promises supply hope without making the disputed meaning of taken here certain.

1 Thessalonians 5:1–11 likewise uses the thief comparison, then calls believers to remain awake, live in faith and love, hold the hope of salvation, and build one another up. God's purpose gives readiness a positive shape: a community living faithfully together as it waits for its Lord.
