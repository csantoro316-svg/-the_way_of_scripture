Passage

Exodus 16:22-30

How did the double portion make rest possible?

Berean Standard Bible

22 On the sixth day, they gathered twice as much food—two omers per person —and all the leaders of the congregation came and reported this to Moses. 23 He told them, “This is what the LORD has said: ‘Tomorrow is to be a day of complete rest, a holy Sabbath to the LORD. So bake what you want to bake, and boil what you want to boil. Then set aside whatever remains and keep it until morning.’” 24 So they set it aside until morning as Moses had commanded, and it did not smell or contain any maggots. 25 “Eat it today,” Moses said, “because today is a Sabbath to the LORD. Today you will not find anything in the field. 26 For six days you may gather, but on the seventh day, the Sabbath, it will not be there.”

27 Yet on the seventh day some of the people went out to gather, but they did not find anything. 28 Then the LORD said to Moses, “How long will you refuse to keep My commandments and instructions? 29 Understand that the LORD has given you the Sabbath; that is why on the sixth day He will give you bread for two days. On the seventh day, everyone must stay where he is; no one may leave his place.” 30 So the people rested on the seventh day.

What is the context?

On the sixth day the people gather twice the usual amount. Moses explains that the next day is a holy Sabbath to the LORD. They are to prepare their food and keep the remainder overnight. This time it stays fresh: the supply God has given for the day of rest does not spoil.

Some still go out on the seventh day and find nothing. The LORD challenges their refusal to keep His instructions and reminds them that He has given the Sabbath and the two-day supply. The account ends with the people resting.

The instruction and the provision belong together. God makes it possible for Israel to stop gathering, and they are to trust what He has already supplied. Keeping the extra food is obedience on the sixth day, even though keeping it against His instruction on other days was disobedience. The test is whether they listen to Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian traditions agree that God commands Israel to rest here; they differ on how that command applies to Christians. Wesley’s note on verse 23 understands the weekly rest as already rooted in creation, rather than a completely new practice invented at this point.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 21.7–8, and the 1689 Baptist Confession, 22.7–8, teach that God requires one day in seven for worship and rest. They understand the day to have changed from the seventh day to the first after Christ’s resurrection. Both allow duties of necessity and mercy. These are the teachings of these confessions, not a claim that every Presbyterian or Baptist practices alike.

The Catholic Church’s Catechism, 2174–2176, distinguishes Sunday from the Jewish Sabbath and describes Sunday observance as fulfilling its spiritual meaning through Christ’s resurrection. The continuing duty is regular public worship of God, now centered on the Lord’s Day.

The Lutheran Large Catechism, third commandment, distinguishes Israel’s required outward observance from Christian practice. It emphasizes rest, hearing God’s word and gathering for worship. It retains Sunday for an orderly common gathering while saying that no day is inherently better than another.

The Seventh-day Adventist Church’s belief on the Sabbath teaches that the seventh day remains the appointed day of rest and worship for all people. It understands the day as a continuing memorial of creation and a sign of the relationship between God and His people.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 4:1–11 draws together God’s seventh-day rest, Israel’s wilderness disobedience and the promise of entering God’s rest. It says that Joshua’s bringing the people into the land did not exhaust that promise, and urges its hearers to respond to God in faith rather than repeat the wilderness refusal.

That larger purpose adds depth to the day of rest in this account. God is teaching His rescued people to trust Him, and Scripture carries that theme forward into the call to enter His rest. The explanation in Hebrews reaches beyond deciding which day a congregation meets.
