Passage

Isaiah 51:1-16

What can a frightened people learn from Abraham and Sarah?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Listen to Me, you who pursue righteousness, you who seek the LORD: Look to the rock from which you were cut, and to the quarry from which you were hewn. 2 Look to Abraham your father, and to Sarah who gave you birth. When I called him, he was but one; then I blessed him and multiplied him. 3 For the LORD will comfort Zion and will look with compassion on all her ruins; He will make her wilderness like Eden and her desert like the garden of the LORD. Joy and gladness will be found in her, thanksgiving and melodious song.

4 Pay attention to Me, My people, and listen to Me, My nation; for a law will go out from Me, and My justice will become a light to the nations; I will bring it about quickly. 5 My righteousness draws near, My salvation is on the way, and My arms will bring justice to the nations. The islands will look for Me and wait in hope for My arm. 6 Lift up your eyes to the heavens, and look at the earth below; for the heavens will vanish like smoke, the earth will wear out like a garment, and its people will die like gnats. But My salvation will last forever, and My righteousness will never fail. 7 Listen to Me, you who know what is right, you people with My law in your hearts: Do not fear the scorn of men; do not be broken by their insults. 8 For the moth will devour them like a garment, and the worm will eat them like wool. But My righteousness will last forever, My salvation through all generations.”

9 Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD. Wake up as in days past, as in generations of old. Was it not You who cut Rahab to pieces, who pierced through the dragon? 10 Was it not You who dried up the sea, the waters of the great deep, who made a road in the depths of the sea for the redeemed to cross over? 11 So the redeemed of the LORD will return and enter Zion with singing, crowned with everlasting joy. Gladness and joy will overtake them, and sorrow and sighing will flee.

12 “I, even I, am He who comforts you. Why should you be afraid of mortal man, of a son of man who withers like grass? 13 But you have forgotten the LORD, your Maker, who stretched out the heavens and laid the foundations of the earth. You live in terror all day long because of the fury of the oppressor who is bent on destruction. But where is the fury of the oppressor? 14 The captive will soon be freed; he will not die in the dungeon, and his bread will not be lacking. 15 For I am the LORD your God who stirs up the sea so that its waves roar— the LORD of Hosts is His name. 16 I have put My words in your mouth, and covered you with the shadow of My hand, to establish the heavens, to found the earth, and to say to Zion, ‘You are My people.’”

What is the context?

The Lord addresses people who seek Him and pursue righteousness. He tells them to look back to Abraham and Sarah: the family began small, yet He blessed and multiplied it. Their present weakness does not put restoration beyond His reach.

Comfort for Zion is pictured as a wilderness becoming like Eden, filled with joy and thanksgiving. The promise then reaches beyond the city. God’s justice will become a light to the nations, and distant islands will wait for His saving power.

He contrasts what lasts with what wears out. The heavens, earth and human opponents are passing; His righteousness and salvation endure. The people who carry His law in their hearts are therefore told not to let human insults break them.

A plea follows for the Lord to act as He did in the past. The sea made into a road recalls the deliverance from Egypt. The promised return to Zion is marked by singing and an end to sorrow.

The Lord answers as their Maker and comforter. Their oppressors are mortal; He is the One who made heaven and earth and rules the sea. The closing words join His protection and His word with His declaration that Zion belongs to Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads Abraham and Sarah as encouragement for people who cannot see how recovery is possible: the God who multiplied that small beginning can raise His people again. Henry likewise draws attention to dependence on God’s grace.

Both connect the promised light to the nations with the gospel. They read the prayer about the sea as an appeal to the God who has already rescued His people, and the answer as reassurance that human threats cannot overturn His saving purpose.

The chapter does not ask the frightened people to pretend their enemies are harmless. It calls them to remember whose power and promise will outlast those enemies.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The appeal to Abraham and Sarah belongs to a larger biblical pattern: God brings about what His promise requires when human strength cannot. Romans 4:16–25 also recalls Abraham’s aged body and Sarah’s inability to bear a child, then explains his confidence that God could do what He had promised.

Paul carries that lesson forward to faith in the God who raised Jesus from the dead. This is a shared theme, rather than a direct quotation of Isaiah 51: hope rests on God’s ability to keep His word, and His saving purpose reaches beyond the limits of the people He calls.
