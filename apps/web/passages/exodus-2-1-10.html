Passage

Exodus 2:1-10

How was Moses preserved under Pharaoh’s decree?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now a man of the house of Levi married a Levite woman, 2 and she conceived and gave birth to a son. When she saw that he was a beautiful child, she hid him for three months. 3 But when she could no longer hide him, she got him a papyrus basket and coated it with tar and pitch. Then she placed the child in the basket and set it among the reeds along the bank of the Nile. 4 And his sister stood at a distance to see what would happen to him.

5 Soon the daughter of Pharaoh went down to bathe in the Nile, and her attendants were walking along the riverbank. And when she saw the basket among the reeds, she sent her maidservant to retrieve it. 6 When she opened it, she saw the child, and behold, the little boy was crying. So she had compassion on him and said, “This is one of the Hebrew children.” 7 Then his sister said to Pharaoh’s daughter, “Shall I go and call one of the Hebrew women to nurse the child for you?” 8 “Go ahead,” Pharaoh’s daughter told her. And the girl went and called the boy’s mother. 9 Pharaoh’s daughter said to her, “Take this child and nurse him for me, and I will pay your wages.” So the woman took the boy and nursed him. 10 When the child had grown older, she brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became her son. She named him Moses and explained, “I drew him out of the water.”

What is the context?

The order to kill Hebrew boys is still the setting when a Levite couple has a son. His mother hides him for three months. When that is no longer possible, she waterproofs a papyrus basket and places it among the reeds beside the Nile, with his sister watching from a distance.

The Hebrew word for the basket, tebah, is also the word used for Noah’s ark. Brown–Driver–Briggs lists both vessels under that word. The connection draws attention to a protected life in water that otherwise threatens it.

Pharaoh’s daughter finds the crying child, recognizes him as Hebrew and has compassion on him. His sister offers to find a nurse and brings his own mother. The mother who could no longer hide him is now paid to care for him under the princess’s protection.

When he is older, the child becomes Pharaoh’s daughter’s son. She names him Moses, explaining it through his rescue from the water. The household of the king who ordered the boys’ deaths has become the place where this boy will grow up.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The shared reading emphasizes God preserving the future deliverer through his family’s faith and the princess’s compassion. Wesley highlights God’s care in bringing the child to Pharaoh’s daughter. The Catholic USCCB notes emphasize the reversals in the story: the king’s daughter adopts a Hebrew boy and pays his mother to nurse him. Their explanations recognize purpose working through the people’s actions.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 11:23 explicitly remembers the parents hiding Moses as an act of faith despite the king’s command. Acts 7:20–22 carries the story from his birth to his upbringing and education. The preserved child is being prepared for the deliverance Stephen will go on to describe.

The basket also provides a parallel with Genesis 6:14–18, where Noah’s ark preserves life through threatening waters. The vessels and circumstances differ, but the shared word and pattern connect this rescue with an earlier act of preservation in God’s larger story.
