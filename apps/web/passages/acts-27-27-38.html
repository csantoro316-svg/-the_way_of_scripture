Passage

Acts 27:27-38

Why must the sailors stay, and why does Paul urge everyone to eat?

Berean Standard Bible

27 On the fourteenth night we were still being driven across the Adriatic Sea. About midnight the sailors sensed they were approaching land. 28 They took soundings and found that the water was twenty fathoms deep. Going a little farther, they took another set of soundings that read fifteen fathoms. 29 Fearing that we would run aground on the rocks, they dropped four anchors from the stern and prayed for daybreak. 30 Meanwhile, the sailors attempted to escape from the ship. Pretending to lower anchors from the bow, they let the lifeboat down into the sea. 31 But Paul said to the centurion and the soldiers, “Unless these men remain with the ship, you cannot be saved.” 32 So the soldiers cut the ropes to the lifeboat and set it adrift.

33 Right up to daybreak, Paul kept urging them all to eat: “Today is your fourteenth day in constant suspense, without taking any food. 34 So for your own preservation, I urge you to eat something, because not a single hair of your head will be lost.” 35 After he had said this, Paul took bread and gave thanks to God in front of them all. Then he broke it and began to eat. 36 They were all encouraged and took some food themselves. 37 In all, there were 276 of us on board. 38 After the men had eaten their fill, they lightened the ship by throwing the grain into the sea.

What is the context?

On the fourteenth night, the sailors sense land is near. Their depth measurements show shallower water, and they drop anchors, fearing rocks. Some then try to escape in the lifeboat, pretending to put out more anchors. Paul tells the centurion and soldiers that the men must remain with the ship for the company to be saved. The soldiers cut the lifeboat loose.

Paul then urges everyone to eat after the prolonged strain and lack of food. He reassures them that their lives will be preserved, takes bread, thanks God publicly, breaks it and begins eating. The others take courage and eat as well.

Luke gives the number aboard as 276. After eating, they lighten the ship further by throwing grain into the sea. The promise of preservation is accompanied by concrete action: keeping the crew aboard, taking food and preparing for the landing.

Here being saved means surviving the danger and reaching land alive. Luke records the preservation of the whole company; he does not describe a response of faith by every person aboard.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read the promise and the warning together: trust in God includes responding to the instruction given and taking practical steps. Paul’s encouragement strengthens people to act rather than leaving them passive.

The Catholic USCCB note on verse 35 notices language associated with the Eucharist, or Lord’s Supper, in taking bread, giving thanks and breaking it. Calvin on verse 35 explains Paul’s example and thanksgiving in eating a meal that encourages the frightened company. These readings differ in the emphasis given to the meal’s language; neither requires us to invent a conversion account for all 276 people.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God’s promise in verses 23–24 grants Paul not only his own continuing life but the lives of the people with him. The concern reaches the entire company. Paul helps preserve their strength through food and hope, and the crew’s remaining aboard is part of the rescue that follows.

The scene shows God’s purpose being served through words, decisions and care for bodies under strain. Public thanksgiving also acknowledges the Giver of life before the danger is over. The mission to Rome proceeds with people being cared for along the way.
