Passage

Luke 15:11-32

Will both sons share their father’s joy?

Berean Standard Bible

11 Then Jesus said, “There was a man who had two sons. 12 The younger son said to him, ‘Father, give me my share of the estate.’ So he divided his property between them. 13 After a few days, the younger son got everything together and journeyed to a distant country, where he squandered his wealth in wild living. 14 After he had spent all he had, a severe famine swept through that country, and he began to be in need. 15 So he went and hired himself out to a citizen of that country, who sent him into his fields to feed the pigs. 16 He longed to fill his belly with the pods the pigs were eating, but no one would give him a thing.

17 Finally he came to his senses and said, ‘How many of my father’s hired servants have plenty of food, but here I am, starving to death! 18 I will get up and go back to my father and say to him, “Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you. 19 I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Make me like one of your hired servants.”’ 20 So he got up and went to his father. But while he was still in the distance, his father saw him and was filled with compassion. He ran to his son, embraced him, and kissed him. 21 The son declared, ‘Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son.’ 22 But the father said to his servants, ‘Quick! Bring the best robe and put it on him. Put a ring on his finger and sandals on his feet. 23 Bring the fattened calf and kill it. Let us feast and celebrate. 24 For this son of mine was dead and is alive again! He was lost and is found!’ So they began to celebrate.

25 Meanwhile the older son was in the field, and as he approached the house, he heard music and dancing. 26 So he called one of the servants and asked what was going on. 27 ‘Your brother has returned,’ he said, ‘and your father has killed the fattened calf, because he has him back safe and sound.’ 28 The older son became angry and refused to go in. So his father came out and pleaded with him. 29 But he answered his father, ‘Look, all these years I have served you and never disobeyed a commandment of yours. Yet you never gave me even a young goat so I could celebrate with my friends. 30 But when this son of yours returns from squandering your wealth with prostitutes, you kill the fattened calf for him!’ 31 ‘Son, you are always with me,’ the father said, ‘and all that is mine is yours. 32 But it was fitting to celebrate and be glad, because this brother of yours was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found.’”

What is the context?

Jesus’ third story begins with a father and two sons. The younger asks for his share of the estate, and the father divides his property between them. The son leaves for a distant country and wastes what he has received.

When his money is gone, a famine leaves him in need. He takes work feeding pigs and becomes desperately hungry. Remembering that his father’s hired servants have food, he decides to return, confess his sin and ask to be received as a hired servant.

While he is still far away, his father sees him, runs to meet him and embraces him. The son confesses. The father calls for a robe, a ring, sandals and a feast, describing the return as a son dead and now alive, lost and now found.

The older son comes from the field, hears the celebration and learns what has happened. He becomes angry and refuses to enter. The father comes out and pleads with him too.

The older son appeals to his years of service and complains about the generosity shown to his brother. The father assures him of their continuing relationship and what is his, then explains why they must rejoice: this is his brother, restored to them.

The story ends without telling us whether the older son goes inside. Both sons matter in the setting of the chapter. The welcome of the returning son answers the complaint about Jesus receiving sinners, while the older son’s resistance brings the question of shared joy back to those who grumble.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly recognize a picture of repentance, the Father’s merciful welcome and the challenge of receiving a restored brother. The Catholic Catechism makes the merciful father central to its account of conversion and describes the welcome and feast as signs of renewed life in God’s family. (Catechism §1439)

Calvin emphasizes God’s generous readiness to receive the returning sinner and the older brother’s resistance to that mercy. Wesley likewise connects the elder son with the Pharisees and scribes of the opening scene. These readings keep the story’s ending connected to the complaint that prompted it. (Calvin on Luke 15:20, 25–32; Wesley on Luke 15:25–32)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Romans places the human story within the contrast between Adam’s disobedience, which brings sin and death, and Christ’s obedience, which brings righteousness and life. The son’s movement from estrangement to restoration can be read alongside that larger movement of God’s saving purpose. This is a parallel between the stories, rather than an identification of the younger son as Adam. (Romans 5:12–19)

Paul also explains that God reconciles people to Himself through Christ and entrusts them with a message of reconciliation. In Romans, that love is shown while people are still sinners, through the death and life of the Son. The father’s welcome belongs with this wider account of mercy that restores a broken relationship. (2 Corinthians 5:17–21; Romans 5:6–11)

Ephesians uses the language of being dead and made alive to describe God’s mercy in Christ. It identifies salvation as His gift and the new life as a life directed toward good works. That is a related New Testament picture of the life and welcome God gives, rather than a reward someone can demand by comparing himself with a brother. (Ephesians 2:1–10)
