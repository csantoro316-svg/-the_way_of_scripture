Passage

Isaiah 6:1-13

How is Isaiah cleansed and sent into a ministry people will resist?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord seated on a throne, high and exalted; and the train of His robe filled the temple. 2 Above Him stood seraphim, each having six wings: With two wings they covered their faces, with two they covered their feet, and with two they were flying. 3 And they were calling out to one another: “Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Hosts; all the earth is full of His glory.” 4 At the sound of their voices the doorposts and thresholds shook, and the temple was filled with smoke.

5 Then I said: “Woe is me, for I am ruined, because I am a man of unclean lips dwelling among a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of Hosts.” 6 Then one of the seraphim flew to me, and in his hand was a glowing coal that he had taken with tongs from the altar. 7 And with it he touched my mouth and said: “Now that this has touched your lips, your iniquity is removed and your sin is atoned for.”

8 Then I heard the voice of the Lord saying: “Whom shall I send? Who will go for Us?” And I said: “Here am I. Send me!” 9 And He replied: “Go and tell this people, ‘Be ever hearing, but never understanding; be ever seeing, but never perceiving.’ 10 Make the hearts of this people calloused; deafen their ears and close their eyes. Otherwise they might see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their hearts, and turn and be healed.”

11 Then I asked: “How long, O Lord?” And He replied: “Until the cities lie ruined and without inhabitant, until the houses are left unoccupied and the land is desolate and ravaged, 12 until the LORD has driven men far away and the land is utterly forsaken. 13 And though a tenth remains in the land, it will be burned again. As the terebinth and oak leave stumps when felled, so the holy seed will be a stump in the land.”

What is the context?

The account now describes Isaiah’s vision in the year King Uzziah dies. The earthly king’s death stands beside the sight of the Lord enthroned, with His robe filling the temple. Heavenly attendants called seraphim cover their faces and feet as they proclaim His holiness; their voices shake the thresholds, and smoke fills the temple.

Isaiah responds by confessing his own unclean lips and his place among a people with the same uncleanness. He has seen the King and recognizes his unfitness to stand before Him. One of the seraphim touches his mouth with a coal from the altar and announces that his guilt is removed and his sin atoned for.

Only then does Isaiah hear the call for a messenger and offer himself. The commission is sobering: his hearers will keep hearing and seeing without understanding. His message will be bound up with the hardening of their hearts, rather than producing the widespread turning he might have hoped for.

When Isaiah asks how long, the answer is devastation, empty houses, a ravaged land and people driven far away. Even a remaining tenth faces further burning. Yet the final image is a stump that remains after a tree is felled: the holy seed is still there.

The chapter joins God’s holiness, Isaiah’s cleansing, his willingness to serve and the painful outcome of speaking to a resistant people. The surviving stump keeps the final word from being complete destruction.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the vision in light of John 12:41, which connects it with Christ’s glory, and understands the coal as assurance of pardon through Christ’s work. Wesley likewise sees the touch as a sign of pardon and cleansing. Their readings emphasize that Isaiah is made ready by God’s mercy before he is sent.

On the hardening, Henry and Wesley both connect the judgment with people who have refused to listen. Wesley describes God continuing to send His word while withdrawing the help they have resisted. The Reformed Westminster Confession 5.6 and the 1689 Baptist Confession 5.6 similarly describe God judging earlier sin by withholding or withdrawing help, while people harden themselves through what softens others.

These explanations take both the divine judgment and the people’s refusal seriously. They do not turn the promise of a surviving holy seed into an announcement that everyone responds in exactly the same way. The closing image preserves hope within a severe commission.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament directly connects this chapter with Jesus. John 12:37–43 quotes the hardening words and says Isaiah spoke because he saw Jesus’ glory. The vision therefore belongs to the New Testament’s account of who Jesus is, as well as its explanation of unbelief in the face of His signs.

Jesus quotes the passage when explaining His use of parables in Matthew 13:10–17; the connection also appears in Mark 4:10–12 and Luke 8:9–10. Hearing the message and understanding it are not treated as the same response.

Paul quotes the passage in Acts 28:23–28, after some hearers are persuaded about Jesus and others refuse to believe. He then announces that God’s salvation has been sent to the Gentiles. Isaiah’s commission becomes part of the explanation of the gospel’s reception and its movement among the nations.

Romans 11:1–12, 17–32 adds an essential part of that larger picture. Paul rejects the claim that God has rejected His people, speaks of a remnant by grace and a hardening in part, and warns Gentile believers against boasting. God’s purpose includes mercy toward people who have been disobedient; the account calls for humility and faith, rather than superiority toward others.
