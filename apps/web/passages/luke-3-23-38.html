Luke 3:23-38

Why trace Jesus’ family back to Adam?

Berean Standard Bible

What is the context?

Luke places the family list after the Father’s declaration about Jesus and before the wilderness testing. Jesus is about thirty when He begins His ministry. The account identifies Him as regarded as Joseph’s son while preserving what it has already said about His conception through the Holy Spirit. (Luke 1:26–35)

The list moves backward through David and Abraham, then beyond Israel’s founding family to Adam and God. It roots Jesus within Israel’s story and also within the wider human family. The scope fits the salvation for Israel and the nations already announced in Luke.

Matthew’s list begins with Abraham and moves toward Jesus. The names between Joseph and David differ: Matthew follows David’s son Solomon, while Luke follows Nathan. The difference is visible in the texts themselves and should be acknowledged when the lists are read together. (Matthew 1:1–17)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the family lists as connecting Jesus with God’s promises to David and Abraham and affirming His place in real human history. They differ over how the two lists fit together.

Wesley explains Luke as tracing Mary’s family, with Heli understood as Joseph’s father-in-law, while Matthew traces Joseph’s line. An early Christian writer, Julius Africanus, offered another explanation: both lists trace Joseph, one through birth and the other through a family’s legal line after a childless relative’s death. These are proposed explanations; Luke does not spell out either reconstruction. (Wesley on Luke 3:23; Africanus preserved in Eusebius, Church History 1.7)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Tracing the line to Adam has a supported connection with Paul’s explicit comparison of Adam and Christ. Romans describes sin and death entering through Adam, then contrasts that with the grace and life that come through Christ’s obedience. Jesus’ place in the human story matters to His work of bringing life to people caught under sin and death. (Romans 5:12–21)

Paul also calls Christ the last Adam and connects His resurrection with the future resurrection of His people. The one who shares our humanity brings a new future beyond the death inherited in Adam. Luke’s family list does not explain every part of that teaching, but its reach back to Adam gives a fitting setting for the Son’s obedient life and saving work. (1 Corinthians 15:20–28, 42–49)
