Passage

John 16:7-15

Why is Jesus’ departure for the disciples’ good?

Berean Standard Bible

7 But I tell you the truth, it is for your benefit that I am going away. Unless I go away, the Advocate will not come to you; but if I go, I will send Him to you. 8 And when He comes, He will convict the world in regard to sin and righteousness and judgment: 9 in regard to sin, because they do not believe in Me; 10 in regard to righteousness, because I am going to the Father and you will no longer see Me; 11 and in regard to judgment, because the prince of this world has been condemned.

12 I still have much to tell you, but you cannot yet bear to hear it. 13 However, when the Spirit of truth comes, He will guide you into all truth. For He will not speak on His own, but He will speak what He hears, and He will declare to you what is to come. 14 He will glorify Me by taking from what is Mine and disclosing it to you. 15 Everything that belongs to the Father is Mine. That is why I said that the Spirit will take from what is Mine and disclose it to you.

What is the context?

Jesus tells the grieving disciples that His departure will bring a benefit: He will send the Advocate to them. The promised Spirit will continue the work of making Him known and will expose the world’s error about sin, righteousness and judgment.

The word translated convict is ἐλέγχω, elenchō (eh-LENG-khoh). Thayer’s lexicon explains it as bringing fault or error to light and identifies this use in verse 8. The point is more than creating a bad feeling: the Spirit shows what is wrong and what is true.

Jesus gives a reason for each part of that work. Sin is connected with unbelief in Him; righteousness with His going to the Father; judgment with the condemnation of the prince of this world. These statements direct attention to who Jesus is and what His departure accomplishes.

The Spirit will also guide the disciples into truth they cannot yet bear. His work will glorify Jesus, taking what belongs to Him and making it known. The Father, Son and Spirit work together in this revelation. The disciples are being promised the help they need to understand and bear witness.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the Spirit’s work here as exposing error and making the truth about Jesus known. The details of righteousness and judgment receive different emphases. Wesley on verses 10–11 emphasizes the vindication of Jesus’ righteousness in His return to the Father and the defeat of the world’s ruler. Calvin on verses 10–11 emphasizes the righteousness received through Christ and the order restored by His victory over Satan. These are the named teachers’ explanations.

Christians also differ over how the Spirit’s promised guidance relates to the church’s teaching after the apostles. The Catholic Church’s Catechism, paragraphs 889–892, teaches that the Spirit preserves the church in the apostolic faith through its teaching office. It identifies particular definitive teaching on faith and morals by the pope or bishops united with him as protected from error. This does not mean that every statement by a church leader is infallible.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 1.6 and 1.10, recognizes the Spirit’s help in understanding Scripture and identifies the Spirit speaking in Scripture as the final judge of doctrine. It rejects adding new revelations or human traditions to what God has given there.

The Catholic account also distinguishes growth in understanding from new public revelation: Catechism paragraphs 65–67 teach that Christ’s revelation is complete. The disagreement concerns the authority by which that faith is preserved and explained, rather than whether the Spirit should lead people away from Jesus.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promised benefit becomes visible in Acts 2:32–41: the exalted Jesus pours out the Spirit, the apostles announce His resurrection and hearers are moved to repentance. His departure advances the witness instead of ending it.

The judgment of the world’s ruler also fits the victory accomplished through the cross. Colossians 2:13–15 joins forgiveness and the cancellation of sin’s debt with Christ’s triumph over hostile powers. The Spirit makes known a victory grounded in Jesus’ work.
