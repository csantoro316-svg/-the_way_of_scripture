Passage

Matthew 6:19-24

Treasure and an Undivided Heart

Berean Standard Bible

19 Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves break in and steal. 20 But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where moth and rust do not destroy, and where thieves do not break in and steal. 21 For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.

22 The eye is the lamp of the body. If your eyes are good, your whole body will be full of light. 23 But if your eyes are bad, your whole body will be full of darkness. If then the light within you is darkness, how great is that darkness! 24 No one can serve two masters: Either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money.

What is the context?

The earlier examples of giving, prayer, and fasting asked whose approval a person seeks. Jesus now addresses what a person treasures. Earthly possessions can be damaged, consumed, or stolen. Treasure kept with God is beyond those losses, and the direction of the heart follows what a person values.

The eye and light picture continues the concern for the whole person. A good eye fills the body with light; a bad eye leaves it in darkness. Placed between the sayings about treasure and two masters, the illustration asks what is guiding a person's life. Something meant to provide light can instead leave the person deeply misdirected.

The final saying presents two masters whose claims cannot both govern the same life. The word translated money is μαμωνᾶς, mamōnas (mah-moh-NAHS). Thayer's Greek Lexicon explains it as wealth or riches and notes its use here as a rival master. Jesus is addressing love, devotion, and service: wealth can claim the loyalty that belongs to God.

The teaching that follows about food, clothing, and worry belongs to this same question of allegiance. If God is the One His followers serve, His knowledge and care are to shape how they face their needs. Their security and purpose are to be directed toward Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read the treasure and two-masters sayings as a call to give God first loyalty over wealth. Wesley on Matthew 6:19–24 explains the eye picture as an undivided purpose directed toward God, and the US Catholic bishops' notes on Matthew 6:22–24 connect it with being guided by Jesus' teaching about the passing nature of earthly riches.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Faith has often required choosing a future with God over present advantage. Hebrews 11:24–26 describes Moses choosing hardship with God's people over Egypt's treasures, looking ahead to his reward. Hebrews describes that hardship as bearing disgrace for Christ. That provides a concrete example of value being measured by God's future rather than immediate gain.

For those joined to the risen Christ, the future is tied to Him. Colossians 3:1–4 calls believers to seek the things above because their life is hidden with Christ and will be revealed with Him. The reason for a changed focus is the new life He has given.

1 Timothy 6:17–19 makes that hope practical for people who possess wealth: trust God, do good, and share generously. It describes such a life as storing up a foundation for the future. Wealth can be used in service of God's purposes when the heart's hope rests in Him.
