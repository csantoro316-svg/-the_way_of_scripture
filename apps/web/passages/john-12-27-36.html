Passage

John 12:27-36

How does the cross reveal God’s glory?

Berean Standard Bible

27 Now My soul is troubled, and what shall I say? ‘Father, save Me from this hour’? No, it is for this purpose that I have come to this hour. 28 Father, glorify Your name!” Then a voice came from heaven: “I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again.” 29 The crowd standing there heard it and said that it had thundered. Others said that an angel had spoken to Him. 30 In response, Jesus said, “This voice was not for My benefit, but yours.

31 Now judgment is upon this world; now the prince of this world will be cast out. 32 And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw everyone to Myself.” 33 He said this to indicate the kind of death He was going to die.

34 The crowd replied, “We have heard from the Law that the Christ will remain forever. So how can You say that the Son of Man must be lifted up? Who is this Son of Man?” 35 Then Jesus told them, “For a little while longer, the Light will be among you. Walk while you have the Light, so that darkness will not overtake you. The one who walks in the darkness does not know where he is going. 36 While you have the Light, believe in the Light, so that you may become sons of light.” After Jesus had spoken these things, He went away and was hidden from them.

What is the context?

Jesus is troubled as the hour approaches, yet He asks the Father to glorify His name. His distress and His obedience appear together. John does not portray the suffering as easy for Him, or as a reason for abandoning the purpose for which He came.

The voice from heaven answers that the Father has glorified His name and will do so again. The people offer different explanations of what they heard: thunder or an angel’s voice. Jesus says the voice came for their benefit. The witness is meant to direct them toward the Father’s work through Him.

Jesus then speaks of judgment, the defeat of the ruler of this world and His own being lifted up. John explicitly explains that lifting up refers to the kind of death He would die. The cross is therefore presented as the place of victory and of drawing people to Himself, even though it will appear to His opponents as their success.

The crowd asks how this can fit with a Christ who remains forever. Jesus calls them to walk in the light and believe while the opportunity is before them. He then withdraws. The passage records both the explanation of His coming death and the urgent invitation to receive Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly connect Jesus’ being lifted up with the cross and understand His death as bringing victory over sin and evil. The main difference concerns how to understand the promise of drawing everyone to Himself.

Calvin’s commentary on verse 32 understands everyone as God’s people gathered from both Jews and Gentiles. In the Reformed Westminster Confession, 8.8, Christ certainly brings those for whom He has purchased redemption into its benefits. This reading emphasizes a saving work that brings His people to Him.

Wesley’s note on verse 32 also includes Jews and Gentiles, while distinguishing those who follow His drawing. The Catholic Church teaches that Christ died for all without exception (Catechism 605) and that God’s grace calls for a free response (Catechism 2002). These teachings describe the wider reach of His saving work without treating the verse as a promise that every person is automatically saved.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The obedience of Jesus answers a much older story. Romans 5:12–19 contrasts Adam’s disobedience, through which sin and death entered, with Christ’s obedience, through which righteousness and life come. His resolve here belongs to that reversal: the Son carries out the Father’s saving purpose in a world marked by Adam’s sin.

The cross also confronts the powers that hold people in bondage. Colossians 2:13–15 joins forgiveness and canceled debt with God’s triumph over the powers through the cross. This helps explain why Jesus speaks of judgment and victory while moving toward His death. What appears to be defeat becomes the means of rescue.
