Passage

1 Samuel 18:17-30

How does Saul turn a marriage offer into a trap?

Berean Standard Bible

17 Then Saul said to David, “Here is my older daughter Merab. I will give her to you in marriage. Only be valiant for me and fight the LORD’s battles.” But Saul was thinking, “I need not raise my hand against him; let the hand of the Philistines be against him.” 18 And David said to Saul, “Who am I, and what is my family or my father’s clan in Israel, that I should become the son-in-law of the king?” 19 So when it was time to give Saul’s daughter Merab to David, she was given in marriage to Adriel of Meholah.

20 Now Saul’s daughter Michal loved David, and when this was reported to Saul, it pleased him. 21 “I will give her to David,” Saul thought, “so that she may be a snare to him, and the hand of the Philistines may be against him.” So Saul said to David, “For a second time now you can be my son-in-law.” 22 Then Saul ordered his servants, “Speak to David privately and tell him, ‘Behold, the king is pleased with you, and all his servants love you. Now therefore, become his son-in-law.’” 23 But when Saul’s servants relayed these words to David, he replied, “Does it seem trivial in your sight to be the son-in-law of the king? I am a poor man and lightly esteemed.” 24 And the servants told Saul what David had said. 25 Saul replied, “Say to David, ‘The king desires no other dowry but a hundred Philistine foreskins as revenge on his enemies.’” But Saul intended to cause David’s death at the hands of the Philistines.

26 When the servants reported these terms to David, he was pleased to become the king’s son-in-law. Before the wedding day arrived, 27 David and his men went out and killed two hundred Philistines. He brought their foreskins and presented them as payment in full to become the king’s son-in-law. Then Saul gave his daughter Michal to David in marriage. 28 When Saul realized that the LORD was with David and that his daughter Michal loved David, 29 he grew even more afraid of David. So from then on Saul was David’s enemy. 30 Every time the Philistine commanders came out for battle, David was more successful than all of Saul’s officers, so that his name was highly esteemed.

What is the context?

Saul offers David his older daughter Merab if David will fight his battles. The account tells us Saul’s hidden intention: he wants the Philistines to kill David for him. David responds with humility about his family’s standing, and Merab is eventually married to someone else.

When Saul learns that Michal loves David, he sees another opportunity. He has his servants encourage the marriage privately. David objects that he is poor and of little standing, so Saul asks for a hundred Philistine foreskins instead of an ordinary marriage payment. The gruesome demand turns enemy deaths into the price of joining the royal family.

Saul hopes David will die trying to meet the terms. Instead, David and his men kill two hundred Philistines, and David presents the full payment. Saul gives him Michal. The attempt to remove David has brought him into Saul’s own family.

The chapter closes with Saul recognizing both the LORD’s presence with David and Michal’s love for him. He becomes still more afraid and remains David’s enemy. David’s continued military success deepens the conflict rather than ending it.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Henry and Gill read the marriage offers in light of the intention the passage states openly: Saul is trying to arrange David’s death. His religious language about fighting the LORD’s battles does not make that intention faithful to God.

Henry emphasizes David’s careful conduct under provocation. Wesley also highlights his humility about entering the royal family. Their reading recognizes David’s restraint without turning Saul’s cruelty into an acceptable way for a ruler or parent to behave.

These commentators also see God preserving David through Saul’s schemes. The result reverses the king’s intention: David survives, gains a place in the royal family and grows in public esteem. The account explains this particular turn in David’s calling; it does not promise that every faithful person will gain status through danger.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Saul’s plans cannot stop the royal purpose God has begun with David. A related theme comes into view in Acts 4:24–30, where the believers pray about the rulers who opposed Jesus. They recognize that even the conspiracy against Him did not defeat what God had determined to accomplish.

This is a wider pattern, rather than a claim that Acts identifies Saul’s marriage plot as a direct prediction of the cross. God works through opposition without making the opponents’ cruelty good. For Jesus, that purpose includes suffering and death, followed by resurrection; for the praying believers, it becomes a reason to ask for courage to keep speaking His word.
