Passage

1 Samuel 25:39-44

What follows Nabal’s death?

Berean Standard Bible

39 On hearing that Nabal was dead, David said, “Blessed be the LORD, who has upheld my cause against the reproach of Nabal and has restrained His servant from evil. For the LORD has brought the wickedness of Nabal down upon his own head.” Then David sent word to Abigail, asking her to become his wife. 40 When his servants came to Abigail at Carmel, they said, “David has sent us to take you as his wife.” 41 She arose, bowed facedown, and said, “Here is your servant, ready to serve and to wash the feet of my lord’s servants.” 42 So Abigail hurried and got on a donkey, and attended by five of her maidens, she followed David’s messengers and became his wife. 43 David had also married Ahinoam of Jezreel. So she and Abigail were both his wives. 44 But Saul had given his daughter Michal, David’s wife, to Palti son of Laish, who was from Gallim.

What is the context?

David responds to Nabal’s death by thanking the LORD for upholding his cause and keeping him from evil. He then sends a marriage proposal to Abigail. She accepts with an expression of humble service and follows his messengers, accompanied by five maidens.

The account adds that David has also married Ahinoam of Jezreel. Meanwhile Saul has given Michal, still identified here as David’s wife, to Palti son of Laish. David’s family life is becoming more complicated while his conflict with Saul continues.

The report of multiple wives belongs alongside the warning to Israel’s kings in Deuteronomy 17:16–20, including the command not to take many wives lest the heart go astray. Being chosen to rule does not place a king above the instruction given to him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Gill distinguishes David’s gratitude for being preserved from bloodshed from delight in death simply as revenge. Wesley is more critical of David’s response, calling it another instance of human weakness. The report gives David’s words without requiring readers to treat every part of his reaction as a universal model.

Henry emphasizes Abigail’s willingness to share David’s present troubles because she believes in his future. Gill separately criticizes the practice of multiple wives, even when found among otherwise faithful people. Admiration for Abigail’s wisdom need not become approval of every feature of David’s household.
