Passage

Ruth 3:1-13

What is Ruth asking Boaz to do?

Berean Standard Bible

1 One day Ruth’s mother-in-law Naomi said to her, “My daughter, should I not seek a resting place for you, that it may be well with you? 2 Now is not Boaz, with whose servant girls you have been working, a relative of ours? In fact, tonight he is winnowing barley on the threshing floor. 3 Therefore wash yourself, put on perfume, and wear your best clothes. Go down to the threshing floor, but do not let the man know you are there until he has finished eating and drinking. 4 When he lies down, note the place where he lies. Then go in and uncover his feet, and lie down, and he will explain to you what you should do.” 5 “I will do everything you say,” Ruth answered.

6 So she went down to the threshing floor and did everything her mother-in-law had instructed her to do. 7 After Boaz had finished eating and drinking and was in good spirits, he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain. Then Ruth went in secretly, uncovered his feet, and lay down. 8 At midnight, Boaz was startled, turned over, and there lying at his feet was a woman! 9 “Who are you?” he asked. “I am your servant Ruth,” she replied. “Spread the corner of your garment over me, for you are a kinsman-redeemer.”

10 Then Boaz said, “May the LORD bless you, my daughter. You have shown more kindness now than before, because you have not run after the younger men, whether rich or poor. 11 And now do not be afraid, my daughter. I will do for you whatever you request, since all my fellow townspeople know that you are a woman of noble character. 12 Yes, it is true that I am a kinsman-redeemer, but there is a redeemer nearer than I. 13 Stay here tonight, and in the morning, if he wants to redeem you, good. Let him redeem you. But if he does not want to redeem you, as surely as the LORD lives, I will. Now lie here until morning.”

What is the context?

Naomi now seeks a lasting home for Ruth. The word מָנוֹחַ, manoach (mah-NOH-akh), means a resting place or condition of rest. Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically explains its use here as the rest and security sought in marriage. Naomi’s concern extends beyond the food Ruth has obtained during harvest.

She tells Ruth to wash, put on perfume and her best clothes, and approach Boaz after he has finished eating and drinking at the threshing floor. Ruth is to note where he lies, uncover his feet, and lie down. She follows the instructions, and at midnight he wakes in surprise.

Ruth identifies herself and asks him to spread the corner of his garment over her because he is a kinsman-redeemer. כָּנָף, kanaph (kah-NAHF), can mean a wing or the corner of a garment; Brown–Driver–Briggs gives both uses. The request recalls Boaz’s prayer in 2:12 that she find refuge beneath God’s wings. She is asking him to extend care through the family responsibility he can assume.

Boaz blesses her, praises her kindness in not pursuing younger men, and acknowledges that the town knows her noble character. He is willing to act, but another relative has priority. He promises to address that man’s claim in the morning and, if the man declines, to redeem her himself. The response is warm, while the outcome remains subject to a right he will not ignore.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Henry, and the USCCB Bible study notes understand Ruth’s request as seeking marriage and read Boaz as treating her honorably. The covering language has a close biblical comparison in Ezekiel 16:8, where the LORD describes spreading His cloak and entering a covenant with Jerusalem.

Gill also reads the action as a marriage request, but acknowledges the risks in Naomi’s unusual plan. He asks how she could be sure the situation would not lead to wrongdoing or misunderstanding. His discussion preserves the honorable purpose without pretending that the approach carried no danger.

These readers also recognize that Boaz’s willingness does not remove the nearer relative’s claim. The scene establishes Ruth’s request and Boaz’s promise to act properly; it does not yet complete the public arrangement described in the next chapter.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The sheltering garment belongs beside the Bible’s wider use of marriage to describe God’s covenant care. Ezekiel 16:6–14 portrays the LORD giving life, covering, and dignity to Jerusalem, then pledging Himself to her. The image emphasizes His initiative and care for someone who could not supply those things for herself.

The New Testament brings marriage language to Christ and the church. Ephesians 5:25–33 explains His love through giving Himself to make His people holy and care for them. Revelation 19:6–9 looks toward the joy of the Lamb’s marriage. Ruth’s appeal for a faithful protector and husband fits within that larger theme of belonging secured through faithful love.
