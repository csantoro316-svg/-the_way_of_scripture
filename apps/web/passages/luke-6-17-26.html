Passage

Luke 6:17-26

Why does Jesus call the poor and the rejected blessed?

Berean Standard Bible

17 Then Jesus came down with them and stood on a level place. A large crowd of His disciples was there, along with a great number of people from all over Judea, Jerusalem, and the seacoast of Tyre and Sidon. 18 They had come to hear Him and to be healed of their diseases, and those troubled by unclean spirits were healed. 19 The entire crowd was trying to touch Him, because power was coming from Him and healing them all.

20 Looking up at His disciples, Jesus said: “Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God. 21 Blessed are you who hunger now, for you will be filled. Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh. 22 Blessed are you when people hate you, and when they exclude you and insult you and reject your name as evil because of the Son of Man. 23 Rejoice in that day and leap for joy, because great is your reward in heaven. For their fathers treated the prophets in the same way.

24 But woe to you who are rich, for you have already received your comfort. 25 Woe to you who are well fed now, for you will hunger. Woe to you who laugh now, for you will mourn and weep. 26 Woe to you when all men speak well of you, for their fathers treated the false prophets in the same way.

What is the context?

Jesus comes down with the twelve and stands on a level place. Disciples and people from a wide region gather to hear Him and seek healing. He then looks toward His disciples and begins teaching them about the kingdom.

Four blessings are followed by four warnings. Poverty is set beside riches, hunger beside being well fed, weeping beside laughter, and rejection because of the Son of Man beside universal praise. The repeated contrast between now and what is to come directs attention beyond present circumstances.

The word translated blessed is μακάριος, makarios (mah-KAH-ree-oss), meaning blessed or happy. Here Jesus explains the reason for that blessing through God’s kingdom and promised future, even when a person is suffering now. (Thayer, G3107)

The rejection He describes has a stated cause: allegiance to the Son of Man. Jesus places those who suffer this way alongside the prophets, while warning that praise can also be given to false prophets. His teaching asks the disciples to consider whose approval and whose promised future they are living for.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian interpreters place different emphasis on the relationship between outward hardship and inward dependence on God. The Catholic bishops’ notes stress Luke’s direct concern with actual poverty, hunger and social rejection; Wesley also takes the poor here literally, particularly disciples who have left things behind. (USCCB note on Luke 6:20–26; Wesley on Luke 6:20–26)

Calvin reads Luke alongside Matthew’s poverty of spirit: hardship is joined with humility and reliance on God. On the warnings, he emphasizes the danger of making present wealth and pleasure one’s whole comfort. These emphases can overlap. The Catholic Catechism likewise joins detachment from wealth with humility and trust in the Father; the bishops’ notes should not be taken to mean Catholic teaching ignores the heart. (Calvin on Matthew 5:3 and Luke 6:24–26; Catechism 2544–2547)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Mary’s earlier song describes God bringing down the mighty, lifting the humble, filling the hungry and sending the rich away empty. Jesus’ blessings and warnings continue that theme of God overturning the security people find in present status. The link is a shared theme within Luke, not a claim that every poor or wealthy person has the same relationship with God. (Luke 1:51–55)

The New Testament also develops the promised comfort. Revelation describes people shepherded by the Lamb, no longer hungry or thirsty, with their tears wiped away. Peter connects suffering for Christ’s name with blessing and future joy at His appearing. These passages carry the teaching toward God’s final restoration and the hope that sustains His people before it arrives. (Revelation 7:15–17; 1 Peter 4:12–16)
