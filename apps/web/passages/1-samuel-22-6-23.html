Passage

1 Samuel 22:6-23

What happens when Saul treats help for David as treason?

Berean Standard Bible

6 Soon Saul learned that David and his men had been discovered. At that time Saul was sitting under the tamarisk tree on the hill at Gibeah, with his spear in hand and all his servants standing around him. 7 Then Saul said to his servants, “Listen, men of Benjamin! Is the son of Jesse giving all of you fields and vineyards and making you commanders of thousands or hundreds? 8 Is that why all of you have conspired against me? Not one of you told me that my own son had made a covenant with the son of Jesse. Not one of you has shown concern for me or revealed to me that my son has stirred up my own servant to lie in wait against me, as is the case today.” 9 But Doeg the Edomite, who had stationed himself with Saul’s servants, answered: “I saw the son of Jesse come to Ahimelech son of Ahitub at Nob. 10 Ahimelech inquired of the LORD for him and gave him provisions. He also gave him the sword of Goliath the Philistine.”

11 Then the king sent messengers to summon Ahimelech the priest, the son of Ahitub, and his father’s whole family, who were priests at Nob. And all of them came to the king. 12 “Listen now, son of Ahitub,” said Saul. “Here I am, my lord,” he replied. 13 And Saul asked him, “Why have you and the son of Jesse conspired against me? You gave him bread and a sword and inquired of God for him so that he could rise up against me to lie in wait, as he is doing today.” 14 Ahimelech answered the king, “Who among all your servants is as faithful as David, the king’s son-in-law, the captain of your bodyguard and honored in your house? 15 Was that day the first time I inquired of God for him? Far be it from me! Let not the king accuse your servant or any of my father’s household, for your servant knew nothing of this whole affair—not in part or in whole.”

16 But the king replied, “You will surely die, Ahimelech, you and all your father’s house!” 17 Then the king ordered the guards at his side, “Turn and kill the priests of the LORD, because they too sided with David. For they knew he was fleeing, but they did not tell me.” But the king’s servants would not lift a hand to strike the priests of the LORD. 18 So the king ordered Doeg, “You turn and strike down the priests!” And Doeg the Edomite turned and struck down the priests himself. On that day he killed eighty-five men who wore the linen ephod. 19 He also put to the sword Nob, the city of the priests, with its men and women, children and infants, oxen, donkeys, and sheep.

20 But one of the sons of Ahimelech son of Ahitub escaped. His name was Abiathar, and he fled to David. 21 And Abiathar told David that Saul had killed the priests of the LORD. 22 Then David said to Abiathar, “I knew that Doeg the Edomite was there that day, and that he was sure to tell Saul. I myself am responsible for the lives of everyone in your father’s house. 23 Stay with me; do not be afraid, for he who seeks your life is seeking mine as well. You will be safe with me.”

What is the context?

Saul hears that David and his men have been found. He appeals to his servants as men of Benjamin, asking whether David will give them the land and commands Saul can offer. He accuses them of conspiring against him and claims Jonathan has stirred David to ambush him. These are Saul’s accusations, rather than evidence that David is preparing such an attack.

Doeg reports David’s visit to Ahimelech at Nob: the priest inquired of the LORD for him and gave him food and Goliath’s sword. Saul summons Ahimelech and his family and accuses them of conspiracy. Ahimelech answers that David is a trusted servant and the king’s own son-in-law. He denies knowing anything about the alleged plot.

Saul orders the priests killed. His guards refuse to strike the priests of the LORD, so he turns to Doeg. Doeg kills eighty-five men who wore the linen ephod. He also kills the people of Nob, including women, children and infants, along with their animals. The accusation ends in a massacre of priests and their town.

Abiathar, one of Ahimelech’s sons, escapes and tells David. David accepts responsibility, saying he had known Doeg would tell Saul. He asks Abiathar to stay with him and promises protection because they face the same enemy. The man Saul pursues becomes a refuge for the surviving priest.

Psalm 52 names Doeg’s report in its heading. It condemns destructive speech and confidence in wrongdoing, then turns to trust in God’s enduring loving devotion. It gives the report a moral setting beyond its value as information to a king.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads Saul’s charges as the product of jealous hostility rather than a fair judgment. Gill emphasizes Ahimelech’s innocence of the alleged conspiracy and the guards’ refusal to commit the killing. Royal authority does not make the order right.

Wesley and Gill note the deceit in reporting the assistance without the explanation David had given Ahimelech. Gill allows the inquiry of God to have happened even though chapter21 did not describe it; Ahimelech’s answer in verse15 supports that reading. The priest’s ordinary service to someone he believed loyal is turned into evidence of treason.

Henry and Gill take David’s admission seriously: his concealment and his failure to act on the danger he recognized helped bring others into harm. That does not transfer Saul’s order or Doeg’s killings to David. The account gives space both to David accepting responsibility and to naming those who committed the violence.

Henry sees the shelter offered to Abiathar as a picture of refuge with the Son of David. David promises to care for someone who has suffered because their lives are threatened by the same king. His response moves beyond regret to practical protection.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

This tragedy belongs within the longer account of Eli’s household. 1 Samuel 2:27–36 had announced judgment on that priestly house. Later, 1 Kings 2:26–27 explicitly identifies Solomon’s removal of Abiathar from the priesthood as fulfilling the LORD’s word against Eli’s house. Abiathar’s escape preserves a life whose story continues into that later judgment.

That larger connection does not turn Saul’s cruelty into obedience. God had not commanded him to manufacture a conspiracy charge and slaughter the people of Nob. His purpose can continue through a history in which human beings remain responsible for doing wrong.

The same distinction appears at the center of the gospel in Acts 2:22–24. Peter speaks of Jesus being delivered up according to God’s plan while holding those who crucified Him accountable, and then announces His resurrection. This is a shared theme of God’s purpose continuing through human wrongdoing, rather than a claim that the massacre itself was good or a direct prediction of the cross.
