Passage

Genesis 38:1-26

Judah and Tamar

Berean Standard Bible

1 About that time, Judah left his brothers and settled near a man named Hirah, an Adullamite. 2 There Judah saw the daughter of a Canaanite man named Shua, and he took her as a wife and slept with her. 3 So she conceived and gave birth to a son, and Judah named him Er. 4 Again she conceived and gave birth to a son, and she named him Onan. 5 Then she gave birth to another son and named him Shelah; it was at Chezib that she gave birth to him.

6 Now Judah acquired a wife for Er, his firstborn, and her name was Tamar. 7 But Er, Judah’s firstborn, was wicked in the sight of the LORD; so the LORD put him to death. 8 Then Judah said to Onan, “Sleep with your brother’s wife. Perform your duty as her brother-in-law and raise up offspring for your brother.”

9 But Onan knew that the offspring would not belong to him; so whenever he would sleep with his brother’s wife, he would spill his seed on the ground so that he would not produce offspring for his brother. 10 What he did was wicked in the sight of the LORD, so He put Onan to death as well.

11 Then Judah said to his daughter-in-law Tamar, “Live as a widow in your father’s house until my son Shelah grows up.” For he thought, “He may die too, like his brothers.” So Tamar went to live in her father’s house.

12 After a long time Judah’s wife, the daughter of Shua, died. When Judah had finished mourning, he and his friend Hirah the Adullamite went up to his sheepshearers at Timnah. 13 When Tamar was told, “Your father-in-law is going up to Timnah to shear his sheep,” 14 she removed her widow’s garments, covered her face with a veil to disguise herself, and sat at the entrance to Enaim, which is on the way to Timnah. For she saw that although Shelah had grown up, she had not been given to him as a wife.

15 When Judah saw her, he thought she was a prostitute because she had covered her face. 16 Not realizing that she was his daughter-in-law, he went over to her and said, “Come now, let me sleep with you.” “What will you give me for sleeping with you?” she inquired.

17 “I will send you a young goat from my flock,” Judah answered. But she replied, “Only if you leave me something as a pledge until you send it.”

18 “What pledge should I give you?” he asked. She answered, “Your seal and your cord, and the staff in your hand.” So he gave them to her and slept with her, and she became pregnant by him. 19 Then Tamar got up and departed. And she removed her veil and put on her widow’s garments again.

20 Now when Judah sent his friend Hirah the Adullamite with the young goat to collect the items he had left with the woman, he could not find her. 21 He asked the men of that place, “Where is the shrine prostitute who was beside the road at Enaim?” “No shrine prostitute has been here,” they answered.

22 So Hirah returned to Judah and said, “I could not find her, and furthermore, the men of that place said, ‘No shrine prostitute has been here.’”

23 “Let her keep the items,” Judah replied. “Otherwise we will become a laughingstock. After all, I did send her this young goat, but you could not find her.”

24 About three months later, Judah was told, “Your daughter-in-law Tamar has prostituted herself, and now she is pregnant.” “Bring her out!” Judah replied. “Let her be burned to death!”

25 As she was being brought out, Tamar sent a message to her father-in-law: “I am pregnant by the man to whom these items belong.” And she added, “Please examine them. Whose seal and cord and staff are these?”

26 Judah recognized the items and said, “She is more righteous than I, since I did not give her to my son Shelah.” And he did not have relations with her again.

What is the context?

The narrative pauses Joseph's story to follow Judah. He settles near Hirah, marries a Canaanite woman, and has three sons: Er, Onan, and Shelah. Er marries Tamar but dies under God's judgment for wickedness. The account does not identify his specific offense.

Judah tells Onan to provide offspring for his deceased brother through Tamar. Onan has sexual relations with her but deliberately prevents conception because the offspring would not count as his own. God judges his conduct as wicked and puts him to death. A later law in Deuteronomy 25:5–10 also addresses a brother's responsibility to preserve a deceased brother's family name, though Genesis is describing events before that law is given.

Judah sends Tamar back to her father's house to wait for Shelah to grow up, fearing he might also die. Time passes, but Tamar is not given to Shelah even after he is grown. After Judah's wife dies, Tamar disguises herself beside the road to Timnah. Judah mistakes her for a prostitute and has relations with her, leaving his seal, cord, and staff as security for a promised payment.

When Tamar's pregnancy becomes known, Judah demands her death. She sends him the personal items, identifying him as the father. He recognizes them and acknowledges that she is more righteous than he is because he withheld Shelah. The man ready to condemn her is forced to face his own responsibility. The account ends by saying he does not have relations with her again.

This episode belongs beside Joseph's story in more than one way. Judah, who proposed selling Joseph, must now recognize his own wrongdoing. A goat and personal belongings again play a part in a deception, but this time the belongings expose Judah rather than sustaining the lie sent to Jacob.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly recognize Onan's wrongdoing toward his brother and Tamar, Judah's failure to provide what he promised, and the exposure of his double standard. Wesley's notes on Genesis 38:7–23 discuss the family duty, sexual wrongdoing, and Judah's concern for his reputation.

The Catholic Church also draws on Onan's story in its teaching against contraception. Pius XI's Casti connubii, paragraphs 54–56, applies the passage to deliberately preventing conception during sexual intercourse. This extends the application beyond Onan's refusal to provide a child for his brother.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Matthew 1:3 explicitly includes Judah, Tamar, and their sons in Jesus' ancestry. This troubling episode therefore belongs to the family history through which the promised Messiah comes. Matthew preserves Tamar's name rather than presenting an ancestry free from wrongdoing and painful circumstances.

The connection shows God's purpose continuing through a family that needs correction and mercy. It does not make Judah's conduct right. His acknowledgment of responsibility matters within the story, while the continuing family line shows that the future promise does not rest on a spotless record of human behavior.
