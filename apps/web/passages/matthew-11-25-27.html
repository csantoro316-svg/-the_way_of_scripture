Passage

Matthew 11:25-27

The Son Makes the Father Known

Berean Standard Bible

25 At that time Jesus declared, “I praise You, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because You have hidden these things from the wise and learned, and revealed them to little children. 26 Yes, Father, for this was well-pleasing in Your sight.

27 All things have been entrusted to Me by My Father. No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him.

What is the context?

After warning the unrepentant towns, Jesus praises the Father. He describes a striking reversal: truths hidden from the wise and learned are revealed to little children. People with little standing can receive what those confident in their own understanding miss.

The child picture points to dependence and receptiveness. In the same prayer, Jesus calls the Father Lord of heaven and earth and gives Him the credit for revealing these things. Knowing God begins with His gift; social importance and learning cannot put Him under anyone's control.

Jesus then speaks of His unique relationship with the Father. Each knows the other, all things have been entrusted to the Son, and the Son makes the Father known. He presents Himself as more than another teacher offering information about God. Access to the Father is bound to Him.

The next words are His invitation for the weary to come to Him. The statements about revelation and the invitation to receive rest belong next to each other. Traditions agree that God's grace is necessary, while explaining differently how that grace brings people to faith.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand these verses as revealing the unique relationship of the Father and Son and our need for God to make Himself known. Traditions differ over how to explain God's choice and the human response.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 10.1–2, and the 1689 Baptist Confession, 10.1–2, teach that God brings those He has chosen to life through His word and Spirit, renewing their wills so that they freely come to Christ. Their response follows God's work of making them willing.

Wesley on Matthew 11:25–28 reads the hiding as God allowing these things to remain hidden from the self-confident, while revealing them to those with little standing; he connects the following invitation with those who receive revelation. The Methodist Articles of Religion, VIII, affirm that no one can turn to faith through natural strength: God's grace must first enable the response and continue working with the person.

The Catholic Catechism, 1996–2002, likewise says that knowing God depends on His undeserved initiative and that even readiness to receive grace is His work. It joins this with a free human response. Paragraph 1993 explicitly says that a person can reject the Spirit's prompting. This differs from the Reformed account of God's call that brings the chosen person to faith.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The reversal between the wise and the little children appears again in 1 Corinthians 1:18–31. Paul describes the message of Christ crucified as God's power and wisdom, though others dismiss it. God brings people to Himself in a way that leaves no room for boasting in status, strength, or intelligence.

That gives a wider setting for Jesus' prayer. God's purpose is to give salvation through His Son, with the glory belonging to Him. Paul includes both Jews and Greeks among those who recognize Christ as God's wisdom. The Father's gift reaches people across the human divisions that so often decide whose voice seems important.
