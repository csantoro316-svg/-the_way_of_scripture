Passage

Mark 8:31-33

The Messiah Must Suffer and Rise

Berean Standard Bible

31 Then He began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected by the elders, chief priests, and scribes, and that He must be killed and after three days rise again. 32 He spoke this message quite frankly, and Peter took Him aside and began to rebuke Him. 33 But Jesus, turning and looking at His disciples, rebuked Peter and said, “Get behind Me, Satan! For you do not have in mind the things of God, but the things of men.”

What is the context?

Immediately after Peter's confession, Jesus begins to explain His coming suffering. He names rejection by elders, chief priests, and scribes, His death, and His rising after three days. Mark says He speaks frankly. The announcement includes both the cost and what follows it.

Peter takes Jesus aside and rebukes Him. Jesus then turns, looks at His disciples, and rebukes Peter in return. The person who has just named Him as the Christ now opposes the path He says He must take.

Jesus addresses Peter as Satan and gives the reason: Peter has human concerns in mind rather than God's. The rebuke exposes how Peter's response stands against Jesus' mission. The passage does not diagnose Peter as possessed or say that he has literally become Satan.

This is the first of several plain announcements of suffering and resurrection in Mark. It also prepares for Jesus' next words to the crowd about denying oneself, taking up a cross, and following Him. The disciple's path is being explained in relation to the Messiah's own.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see Peter's rebuke as resistance to a suffering Messiah and Jesus' answer as a forceful rejection of that resistance. Calvin on Mark 8:31–33 explains that even zeal for Jesus can oppose His calling when it refuses the cross; Wesley's note on verse 32 emphasizes how directly Jesus now states what He will undergo.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The suffering has a saving purpose. Isaiah 53:10–12 describes the Servant pouring out His life and bearing the sins of many. 1 Peter 2:21–25 applies the language of that passage to Christ, who bears sins in His body and brings people back to their Shepherd. Jesus later states His purpose in Mark as giving His life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45). His death is part of His mission, not its defeat.

Acts 2:22–24 holds together God's set purpose, the responsibility of those who kill Jesus, and God's raising Him from death. That wider account helps explain the necessity announced here without excusing the rejection or leaving the story at the cross.

What is our position?

In What Does Prayer Look Like?, we draw on the parallel announcement in Matthew 16:21 when considering Jesus' later prayer in Gethsemane. He already knows the path before Him, yet its cost remains real. The study emphasizes honest prayer governed by surrender to the Father's will. Knowing God's purpose does not make obedience painless.
