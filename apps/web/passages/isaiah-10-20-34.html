Passage

Isaiah 10:20-34

Where will the survivors place their trust?

Berean Standard Bible

20 On that day the remnant of Israel and the survivors of the house of Jacob will no longer depend on him who struck them, but they will truly rely on the LORD, the Holy One of Israel. 21 A remnant will return —a remnant of Jacob— to the Mighty God. 22 Though your people, O Israel, be like the sand of the sea, only a remnant will return. Destruction has been decreed, overflowing with righteousness. 23 For the Lord GOD of Hosts will carry out the destruction decreed upon the whole land.

24 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD of Hosts says: “O My people who dwell in Zion, do not fear Assyria, who strikes you with a rod and lifts his staff against you as the Egyptians did. 25 For in just a little while My fury against you will subside, and My anger will turn to their destruction.” 26 And the LORD of Hosts will brandish a whip against them, as when He struck Midian at the rock of Oreb. He will raise His staff over the sea, as He did in Egypt. 27 On that day the burden will be lifted from your shoulders, and the yoke from your neck. The yoke will be broken because your neck will be too large.

28 Assyria has entered Aiath and passed through Migron, storing their supplies at Michmash. 29 They have crossed at the ford: “We will spend the night at Geba.” Ramah trembles; Gibeah of Saul flees. 30 Cry aloud, O Daughter of Gallim! Listen, O Laishah! O wretched Anathoth! 31 Madmenah flees; the people of Gebim take refuge. 32 Yet today they will halt at Nob, shaking a fist at the mount of Daughter Zion, at the hill of Jerusalem.

33 Behold, the Lord GOD of Hosts will lop off the branches with terrifying power. The tall trees will be cut down, the lofty ones will be felled. 34 He will clear the forest thickets with an axe, and Lebanon will fall before the Mighty One.

What is the context?

The survivors of Israel will no longer depend on the power that struck them. They will truly rely on the Lord, the Holy One of Israel. A remnant—a surviving part of the people—will return to the Mighty God, even though the nation had been as numerous as the sand of the sea.

The Lord tells His people in Zion not to fear Assyria. He recalls His earlier victories over Midian and Egypt and promises to remove the burden and yoke from His people. The same God who judged them also sets the limit on their oppression.

The final scene makes the threat feel close. An army passes through a series of towns until it shakes its fist at Jerusalem. Then the image changes to a forest being cut down: the Lord fells the lofty trees and clears the thickets.

The movement of the passage is from threatened destruction to a changed dependence. The survivors are not merely people who escaped a stronger army; they are people who turn back to the Lord.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley emphasize genuine reliance on God in place of reliance on the power that had oppressed the people. They connect the closing forest image with God bringing down the army threatening Jerusalem.

There are differences over how closely the named route describes a particular campaign. Henry and Wesley describe it as Sennacherib’s march. The NABRE notes read it as a poetic advance toward Jerusalem and distinguish it from the route of Sennacherib’s troops. Either way, the scene brings the threat to the city’s doorstep before the Lord intervenes.

Verse 27 also has a long history of different explanations. Henry and Wesley develop an anointing reading that points toward the Messiah. The BSB instead pictures a neck grown too large for its yoke. The Hebrew word שֶׁמֶן, shemen, pronounced SHEH-men, can mean fat or oil; Brown–Driver–Briggs places this verse under fatness and notes that the expression is difficult. These explanations differ over the picture, while the promise that the burden will be broken remains clear.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Romans 9:27–28 directly quotes Isaiah’s words about the remnant and God carrying out His sentence. Paul uses them while discussing God’s mercy, the inclusion of Gentiles and the response of Israel. His argument goes on to contrast seeking righteousness by works with receiving it through faith.

Romans 11:1–12 makes clear that this does not mean God has simply rejected His people: Paul points to himself and to a remnant chosen by grace. He later warns Gentile believers against boasting over the branches and speaks of God’s power to graft them in again (Romans 11:17–32).

Isaiah’s surviving people therefore belong to a larger account of judgment and mercy that the New Testament itself develops. The response called for is faith and humility before God, rather than confidence in numbers, ancestry or superiority over others.
