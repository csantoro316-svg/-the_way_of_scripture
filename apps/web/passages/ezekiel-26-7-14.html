Passage

Ezekiel 26:7-14

How should we understand the destruction and the promise that Tyre will not be rebuilt?

Berean Standard Bible

7 For this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Behold, I will bring against Tyre from the north Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, king of kings, with horses and chariots, with cavalry and a great company of troops. 8 He will slaughter the villages of your mainland with the sword; he will set up siege works against you, build a ramp to your walls, and raise his shields against you. 9 He will direct the blows of his battering rams against your walls and tear down your towers with his axes. 10 His multitude of horses will cover you in their dust. When he enters your gates as an army entering a breached city, your walls will shake from the noise of cavalry, wagons, and chariots. 11 The hooves of his horses will trample all your streets. He will slaughter your people with the sword, and your mighty pillars will fall to the ground.

12 They will plunder your wealth and pillage your merchandise. They will demolish your walls, tear down your beautiful homes, and throw your stones and timber and soil into the water. 13 So I will silence the sound of your songs, and the music of your lyres will no longer be heard. 14 I will make you a bare rock, and you will become a place to spread the fishing nets. You will never be rebuilt, for I, the LORD, have spoken, declares the Lord GOD.’

What is the context?

The Lord names Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon as the approaching ruler. The siege picture moves through ramps, battering rams, falling towers, cavalry and slaughter, until the city’s wealth and merchandise are plundered.

The announcement reaches beyond damaged defenses. Homes are demolished, building materials are thrown into the water, and the music of the city is silenced. The place of wealth and celebration becomes bare rock for fishing nets.

The strong statement about never being rebuilt has to be read alongside later biblical references. Ezekiel 29:17–21 says that Nebuchadnezzar’s army labored against Tyre without receiving wages from it. Acts 21:1–6 later describes a ship unloading cargo there and disciples living in the city.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Gill explains that the rebuilding language needs qualification, offering the loss of former grandeur or royal standing among the possible readings. He also reads the materials thrown into the water in connection with Alexander’s later attack. The earlier announcement of many nations helps explain why readers consider more than one campaign.

That later attack is independently documented. Arrian’s account describes Alexander building a causeway with stone and wood toward the island. UNESCO records the causeway and subsequent Greek and Roman construction on the site.

The question is therefore how the announced end of Tyre relates to its later settlement and changed standing. A reading centered on the destruction of its former power is an interpretation of the prophecy, not a claim that the Bible or historical records contain no later city called Tyre.
