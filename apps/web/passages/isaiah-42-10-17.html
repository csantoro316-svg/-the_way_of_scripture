Passage

Isaiah 42:10-17

Why are distant peoples invited to sing a new song?

Berean Standard Bible

10 Sing to the LORD a new song— His praise from the ends of the earth— you who go down to the sea, and all that is in it, you islands, and all who dwell in them. 11 Let the desert and its cities raise their voices; let the villages of Kedar cry aloud. Let the people of Sela sing for joy; let them cry out from the mountaintops. 12 Let them give glory to the LORD and declare His praise in the islands. 13 The LORD goes forth like a mighty one; He stirs up His zeal like a warrior. He shouts; yes, He roars in triumph over His enemies:

14 “I have kept silent from ages past; I have remained quiet and restrained. But now I will groan like a woman in labor; I will at once gasp and pant. 15 I will lay waste the mountains and hills and dry up all their vegetation. I will turn the rivers into dry land and drain the marshes. 16 I will lead the blind by a way they did not know; I will guide them on unfamiliar paths. I will turn darkness into light before them and rough places into level ground. These things I will do for them, and I will not forsake them. 17 But those who trust in idols and say to molten images, ‘You are our gods!’ will be turned back in utter shame.

What is the context?

The Servant’s introduction is followed by a call for praise from the ends of the earth. Sea travelers, islands, desert settlements, Kedar and Sela are included. The Lord’s glory is to be declared far beyond a single city.

The song celebrates His action against His enemies. A warrior’s shout is followed by the image of a woman in labor after a long silence. Mountains, hills, rivers and marshes all appear in the picture of decisive change.

Within that upheaval, the Lord promises to guide the blind along unfamiliar ways, turn darkness into light and make rough ground level. He will not forsake them. The contrast is with those who trust manufactured images and are finally put to shame.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley connect the song with the good news spreading among the nations. They read the Lord’s victory as removing opposition to His work, and the guidance of the blind as the giving of truth and light.

Gill particularly applies the leading of the blind to Gentiles coming to know God through Christ. He reads the long silence and decisive action through the overthrow of pagan opposition and the spread of the gospel.

These explanations join praise with the Lord’s power to guide people who could not find their own way. The judgment on idols stands beside His commitment not to abandon those He leads.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A new song is heard in Revelation 5:9–14, praising the Lamb because His blood has purchased people for God from every tribe, language, people and nation. The song widens into the praise of every creature.

This is a well-supported parallel with Isaiah’s worldwide invitation, rather than a claim that Revelation directly quotes Isaiah 42. The New Testament makes the saving work at the center of that praise explicit: the Lamb was slain and has made a people to serve God.

God’s purpose therefore reaches beyond the defeat of an enemy. People from the world’s many communities are gathered into worship, giving glory to the One who has rescued them.
