Passage

2 Samuel 23:1-7

What hope does David express in his last words?

Berean Standard Bible

1 These are the last words of David: “The oracle of David son of Jesse, the oracle of the man raised on high, the one anointed by the God of Jacob, and the sweet psalmist of Israel: 2 The Spirit of the LORD spoke through me; His word was on my tongue. 3 The God of Israel spoke; the Rock of Israel said to me, ‘He who rules the people with justice, who rules in the fear of God, 4 is like the light of the morning at sunrise of a cloudless dawn, the glistening after the rain on the sprouting grass of the earth.’

5 Is not my house right with God? For He has established with me an everlasting covenant, ordered and secured in every part. Will He not bring about my full salvation and my every desire? 6 But the worthless are all like thorns raked aside, for they can never be gathered by hand. 7 The man who touches them must be armed with iron or with the shaft of a spear. The fire burns them to ashes in the place where they lie.”

What is the context?

David’s last words are introduced as an oracle. The Hebrew word is neum, pronounced neh-OOM. Brown–Driver–Briggs describes it as an utterance or declaration and explicitly includes this verse. David says that the Spirit of the LORD spoke through him.

The message describes a ruler who acts with justice and in the fear of God. Such rule is compared to a clear sunrise and the brightness after rain on growing grass. Right government is a blessing to the people under it.

David then speaks of God’s everlasting covenant with his house. His confidence rests on that ordered and secure promise. The closing picture contrasts this hope with the wicked, who are like harmful thorns gathered for burning.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands last words as David’s final inspired testimony, rather than the last sounds he ever spoke. He takes the description of just rule as instruction about a king’s duties toward God and people.

Gill also sees instruction for rulers, including Solomon, and reads its fullest expression in the Messiah. The duties of a present king and the hope of a greater King need not be separated into competing readings.

Verse 5 has been worded differently in English translations. The older wording followed by Wesley and Gill begins by acknowledging the shortcomings of David’s house; the BSB presents a question expressing confidence in its standing with God. In their comments, both writers place David’s hope in God’s covenant despite the failures within his family.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The hope of righteous rule and an enduring house reaches beyond David’s own achievements. Jeremiah 23:5–6 promises a righteous Branch raised up for David, a King who will act wisely and administer justice.

Luke 1:32–33 identifies Jesus as the Son who receives David’s throne and whose kingdom has no end. These passages help place David’s confidence within the larger promise of a lasting, righteous King.

This connection rests on the promises Scripture gives. It does not require treating David’s own reign as flawless or making every detail of the morning picture a separate prediction.
