Passage

Matthew 27:57-61

Joseph Places Jesus in His Own Tomb

Berean Standard Bible

57 When it was evening, there came a rich man from Arimathea named Joseph, who himself was a disciple of Jesus. 58 He went to Pilate to ask for the body of Jesus, and Pilate ordered that it be given to him.

59 So Joseph took the body, wrapped it in a clean linen cloth, 60 and placed it in his own new tomb that he had cut into the rock. Then he rolled a great stone across the entrance to the tomb and went away. 61 Mary Magdalene and the other Mary were sitting there opposite the tomb.

What is the context?

As evening comes, Joseph of Arimathea, a wealthy disciple, asks Pilate for Jesus' body. Pilate grants the request. Joseph wraps the body in clean linen, places it in his own new tomb cut into the rock, and rolls a large stone across its entrance. Mary Magdalene and the other Mary sit opposite the tomb.

The other Gospels add details without making Joseph's private thoughts known. Luke 23:50–56 calls him a good and righteous Council member who had not agreed with its decision and action. John 19:38–42 says he had followed Jesus secretly because of fear, and that Nicodemus helped with the burial, bringing myrrh and aloes. John explicitly says they wrapped the body with spices; Matthew's shorter account does not mean none were used.

John also explains that the tomb was nearby and the day was Preparation Day. Luke says the Sabbath was beginning. These details explain the urgency. The women see the burial place rather than hearing about it later, which matters when Matthew returns to them at the start of chapter 28.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the burial as the burial of someone who truly died, leading to the proclamation that this same Jesus rose. The Catholic Catechism, 624–627, and the Reformed Westminster Confession, 8.4, both affirm His real death, burial, and resurrection; Joseph's care gives concrete detail to that shared confession.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

1 Corinthians 15:3–5 includes Jesus' burial among the central events of the gospel, between His death for sins and His resurrection. Burial is part of the story of His saving work, not an interruption of it. Acts 13:27–39 likewise moves from His condemnation and burial to God's raising Him and the announcement of forgiveness.

Joseph's wealth and his gift of a tomb also recall Isaiah 53:9, where the suffering servant is associated with a rich man in death. Matthew does not introduce this detail with a quotation, so the connection should be recognized as a scriptural parallel. The New Testament explicitly connects Isaiah's servant with Jesus in Acts 8:30–35.

There is also a moving picture here: Jesus is placed in a disciple's own tomb. That can bring to mind His taking the place of sinners, as Romans 5:6–11 explains. It is a parallel for reflection, rather than a claim that Matthew assigns a special meaning to Joseph's ownership or tells us what Joseph thought about it.
