Passage

Colossians 2:6-15

What has God already given us in Christ?

Berean Standard Bible

6 Therefore, just as you have received Christ Jesus as Lord, continue to walk in Him, 7 rooted and built up in Him, established in the faith as you were taught, and overflowing with thankfulness. 8 See to it that no one takes you captive through philosophy and empty deception, which are based on human tradition and the spiritual forces of the world rather than on Christ.

9 For in Christ all the fullness of the Deity dwells in bodily form. 10 And you have been made complete in Christ, who is the head over every ruler and authority. 11 In Him you were also circumcised, in the putting off of your sinful nature, with the circumcision performed by Christ and not by human hands. 12 And having been buried with Him in baptism, you were raised with Him through your faith in the power of God, who raised Him from the dead.

13 When you were dead in your trespasses and in the uncircumcision of your sinful nature, God made you alive with Christ. He forgave us all our trespasses, 14 having canceled the debt ascribed to us in the decrees that stood against us. He took it away, nailing it to the cross! 15 And having disarmed the powers and authorities, He made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross.

What is the context?

Paul asks the believers to continue living in the Christ they received as Lord: rooted in Him, built up in faith and overflowing with thanks. He warns against being carried away by deceptive teaching grounded elsewhere.

His reason is what they have in Christ. The fullness of deity dwells in Him bodily; He is above every ruler and authority. Their completeness rests in Him.

Paul describes a circumcision performed by Christ rather than human hands, then burial and resurrection with Him in baptism through faith in God’s power. God made them alive with Christ and forgave their trespasses. These descriptions all direct attention to what God has done.

The canceled record is called a cheirographon (khay-ROH-grah-fon). Thayer’s lexicon explains its use for a handwritten acknowledgment of debt. Paul uses that image for the debt that stood against them: God has taken it away at the cross.

The paragraph ends with the powers disarmed and publicly defeated. The cross that dealt with their debt also displays Christ’s triumph. This leads directly into Paul’s warning against accepting the judgments and religious demands of the following verses.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see this as a declaration of Christ’s sufficiency, forgiveness and victory. They differ in how they describe the baptismal participation in His death and resurrection.

The Catholic Catechism, paragraph 1213, teaches that through baptism people are freed from sin, reborn as God’s children and incorporated into Christ and His church.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 28, calls baptism a sign and seal of grace, union with Christ and forgiveness. It also teaches that baptism’s effectiveness is not bound to the moment it is administered and that not everyone baptized is necessarily reborn.

The Baptist 1689 Confession, chapter 29, explicitly cites Colossians 2:12 when describing baptism as a sign of fellowship in Christ’s death and resurrection. It limits baptism to those professing repentance and faith.

The Methodist Articles of Religion, articles XVI–XVII, describe sacraments as means by which God strengthens faith and baptism as a sign of new birth, retaining infant baptism. Wesley’s note on verse 12 stresses the inward new life God works. These descriptions distinguish the traditions’ teachings about baptism while keeping God’s action and Christ at the center.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Romans 6:3–11 gives a close parallel: burial with Christ, new life with Him and the hope of resurrection lead into living for God. The pattern of death and life reaches into both present conduct and the future.

Hebrews 2:10–18 places Christ’s death and defeat of the devil within God’s purpose of bringing many children to glory and freeing them from slavery through fear of death. The victory named here serves that larger rescue, rather than being a reason to seek protection from rival powers.
