Passage

1 Samuel 30:1-6

Where does David find strength when his own men turn against him?

Berean Standard Bible

1 On the third day David and his men arrived in Ziklag, and the Amalekites had raided the Negev, attacked Ziklag, and burned it down. 2 They had taken captive the women and all who were there, both young and old. They had not killed anyone, but had carried them off as they went on their way. 3 When David and his men came to the city, they found it burned down and their wives and sons and daughters taken captive. 4 So David and the troops with him lifted up their voices and wept until they had no strength left to weep. 5 David’s two wives, Ahinoam of Jezreel and Abigail the widow of Nabal of Carmel, had been taken captive. 6 And David was greatly distressed because the people spoke of stoning him, because the soul of every man grieved for his sons and daughters. But David found strength in the LORD his God.

What is the context?

David and his men reach Ziklag on the third day and find it burned. Amalekite raiders have taken the women and everyone else captive without killing anyone. The returning men see the ruin before they can know the outcome for their families.

They weep until they have no strength left. David’s own wives have been taken, and the men speak of stoning him because each is grieving for sons and daughters. The danger now comes from the people who have shared his life in the wilderness.

At this point David finds strength in the LORD his God. The loss is not denied or immediately undone. The statement marks where he turns while grief, fear and responsibility press on him together.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley notes that grief does not disgrace even brave people. David and his men genuinely lament their families. Finding strength in God is not presented as having no tears.

Wesley, Henry and Gill understand David’s strength in terms of God’s power, promises and faithfulness. Gill points to remembered mercy and the relationship on which David can rely. The answer to despair begins with turning toward God before the rescue is visible.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The threatened king’s strength comes from God at a point when his own resources are exhausted. The wider biblical theme is that weakness need not prevent God’s life-giving work.

2 Corinthians 4:7–18 describes God’s power carried in fragile human lives. Paul connects suffering with the death and life of Jesus, and grounds endurance in the promise that God will raise His people with Him. The hope reaches beyond having every present loss repaired.

This is a shared theme, rather than a promise that each crisis will end like Ziklag. David’s tears and his trust can stand together, just as Christian hope can remain alive in people who are still grieving.
