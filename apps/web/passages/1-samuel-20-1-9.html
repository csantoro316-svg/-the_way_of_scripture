Passage

1 Samuel 20:1-9

How can David find out whether he is safe?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then David fled from Naioth in Ramah. He came to Jonathan and asked, “What have I done? What is my iniquity? How have I sinned against your father, that he wants to take my life?” 2 “Far from it!” Jonathan replied. “You will not die. Indeed, my father does nothing, great or small, without telling me. So why would he hide this matter from me? This cannot be true!” 3 But David again vowed, “Your father knows very well that I have found favor in your eyes, and he has said, ‘Jonathan must not know of this, or he will be grieved.’ As surely as the LORD lives and as you yourself live, there is but a step between me and death.” 4 Then Jonathan said to David, “Whatever you desire, I will do for you.”

5 So David told him, “Look, tomorrow is the New Moon, and I am supposed to dine with the king. Instead, let me go and hide in the field until the third evening from now. 6 If your father misses me at all, tell him, ‘David urgently requested my permission to hurry to Bethlehem, his hometown, because there is an annual sacrifice for his whole clan.’ 7 If he says, ‘Good,’ then your servant is safe, but if he is enraged, you will know he has evil intentions. 8 Therefore show kindness to your servant, for you have brought me into a covenant with you before the LORD. If there is iniquity in me, then kill me yourself; why should you bring me to your father?” 9 “Never!” Jonathan replied. “If I ever found out that my father had evil intentions against you, would I not tell you?”

What is the context?

David leaves Naioth and goes to Jonathan with a painful question: what has he done to deserve Saul’s pursuit? Jonathan cannot believe his father intends to kill him without first telling him. David answers that Saul knows about their friendship and could be keeping Jonathan in the dark.

They agree to test Saul’s response to David’s absence from the coming New Moon meal. Jonathan is to say that David requested leave for a family sacrifice at Bethlehem. A calm response would point toward safety; anger would reveal a settled intention to harm him.

David appeals to the covenant they made before the LORD. He asks Jonathan to deal kindly and honestly with him. Even his stark offer that Jonathan should kill him if he is guilty presses the same point: he is asking for justice rather than being handed over to someone determined to destroy him.

Jonathan promises to tell him if he discovers danger. Their friendship must now do more than offer reassurance. It must face the evidence about Saul and provide a way for David to act on what they learn.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley understands Jonathan’s confidence in light of Saul’s oath in chapter19. David’s warning challenges that confidence: a father who once spoke openly may now conceal his intentions. Jonathan’s willingness to investigate matters more than simply repeating that everything will be well.

There are different readings of the Bethlehem explanation. Wesley and Gill allow that David really went there before returning to hide in the field. A reading that takes his hiding as covering the whole interval understands the explanation as a cover story. The passage records the plan and his hiding, without separately narrating a journey to Bethlehem.

Henry sees David’s trials as preparation for the responsibilities ahead and Jonathan’s practical help as true friendship. That reading recognizes a purpose beyond the immediate danger without making Saul’s treatment of an innocent man just.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

David has been chosen for kingship, but the way ahead includes fear, uncertainty and dependence on help. His calling does not make every difficult question disappear. The larger biblical theme is that God can form faith through trials while bringing His people toward what He has promised.

1 Peter 1:3–9 places that theme in the living hope given through Jesus’ resurrection. Grief and tested faith sit alongside an inheritance God preserves. This does not make Saul’s violence good or promise believers a throne like David’s. It shows how present hardship need not cancel the future God has opened through Christ.
