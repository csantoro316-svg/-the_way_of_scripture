Passage

Matthew 12:9-14

Doing Good on the Sabbath

Berean Standard Bible

9 Moving on from there, Jesus entered their synagogue, 10 and a man with a withered hand was there. In order to accuse Jesus, they asked Him, “Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?” 11 He replied, “If one of you has a sheep and it falls into a pit on the Sabbath, will he not take hold of it and lift it out? 12 How much more valuable is a man than a sheep! Therefore it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath.”

13 Then Jesus said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” So he stretched it out, and it was restored to full use, just like the other. 14 But the Pharisees went out and plotted how they might kill Jesus.

What is the context?

The Sabbath dispute continues in a synagogue, where a man has a withered hand. Matthew states the purpose of the question about healing: the questioners want grounds to accuse Jesus. The man's need becomes the setting for another test of His conduct.

Jesus answers with an example of a sheep falling into a pit. Someone would take hold of the animal and lift it out, even on the Sabbath. He then turns attention to the greater value of a human being. The conclusion is direct: doing good on the Sabbath is lawful.

He tells the man to stretch out his hand. The man does so, and it is restored to full use. Matthew describes the action and healing without giving a history of the disability or the man's private thoughts. The focus is on Jesus making the man whole in the very setting where His right to help has been challenged.

The response is a plot to kill Him. That ending sharply contrasts the good He has just done with the harm His opponents intend. In the parallel account, Mark 3:1–6, Jesus' question contrasts doing good with doing evil and saving life with destroying it. The healing and the plot make that contrast concrete.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand this as showing the rightness of active compassion and the authority of Jesus to restore life. Wesley on Matthew 12:12 follows Jesus' comparison from helping an animal to helping a person. The Catholic Catechism, 582, likewise treats His Sabbath healings as service to God and neighbor. These readings do not make the Sabbath a reason to withhold the good that can be done.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The concern for refreshment belongs to the Sabbath command itself. Exodus 23:12 includes workers, foreign residents, and animals in the rest God provides. Jesus' concern for the man fits that wider picture of God's care reaching those who need relief.

The plot also looks ahead to a greater reversal. Acts 2:22–24 recalls Jesus' works of power, His death through human wrongdoing, and God's raising Him to life. Opposition will lead toward the cross, yet God brings salvation through the Savior whom His opponents try to destroy. The healing is part of a ministry whose end will be decided by God, rather than by the plot against Him.
