Passage

Genesis 9:18-25

Noah’s Shame and Canaan’s Curse

Berean Standard Bible

18 The sons of Noah who came out of the ark were Shem, Ham, and Japheth. And Ham was the father of Canaan. 19 These three were the sons of Noah, and from them the whole earth was populated.

20 Now Noah, a man of the soil, proceeded to plant a vineyard. 21 But when he drank some of its wine, he became drunk and uncovered himself inside his tent. 22 And Ham, the father of Canaan, saw his father’s nakedness and told his two brothers outside.

23 Then Shem and Japheth took a garment and placed it across their shoulders, and walking backward, they covered their father’s nakedness. Their faces were turned away so that they did not see their father’s nakedness.

24 When Noah awoke from his drunkenness and learned what his youngest son had done to him, 25 he said,

“Cursed be Canaan!

A servant of servants

shall he be to his brothers.”

What is the context?

The account moves from God's covenant to the life of the surviving family. Noah plants a vineyard, becomes drunk, and lies uncovered. Ham sees his father's nakedness and tells his brothers. Shem and Japheth respond differently: they walk backward with a garment and cover their father without looking at him. The contrast concerns how the sons handle another person's exposure and shame.

Genesis does not tell us all that Noah knew about wine or supply a fuller scene inside the tent. It does tell us that he later learns what his youngest son has done. His pronouncement names Canaan, Ham's son, rather than Ham himself. The repeated identification of Ham as Canaan's father prepares for that ending, but the reason for directing the curse to this son is not fully explained.

The curse speaks of Canaan's service to his brothers. Nothing in the words assigns a skin color to anyone, curses all Ham's descendants, or commands the enslavement of an entire modern population. The people named in the passage and the actual terms of the pronouncement must govern how it is read.

How is this passage commonly understood?

A common Christian reading focuses on the contrast between Ham's dishonor and his brothers' care, with the curse anticipating the later place of Canaan's descendants in the story of Israel. Wesley's notes on verses 22–25 develop those themes and relate the service language to Israel's later dealings with the Canaanites, rather than making the passage a decree about every descendant of Ham.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The failure after the flood shows a limit of the new beginning: being carried through a judgment has not removed humanity's need for inward renewal. That theme develops in Ezekiel 36:26–27, where God promises a new heart and His Spirit to enable a different way of life.

2 Corinthians 5:17–21 brings new creation and reconciliation together in Christ. The contrast helps explain why the Bible's hope must reach beyond surviving a disaster. God's purpose includes restoring people to Himself.
