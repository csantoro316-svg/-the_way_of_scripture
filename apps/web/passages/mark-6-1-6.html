Passage

Mark 6:1-6

A Prophet Without Honor at Home

Berean Standard Bible

1 Jesus went on from there and came to His hometown, accompanied by His disciples. 2 When the Sabbath came, He began to teach in the synagogue, and many who heard Him were astonished. “Where did this man get these ideas?” they asked. “What is this wisdom He has been given? And how can He perform such miracles? 3 Isn’t this the carpenter, the son of Mary and the brother of James, Joses, Judas, and Simon? Aren’t His sisters here with us as well?” And they took offense at Him. 4 Then Jesus said to them, “Only in his hometown, among his relatives, and in his own household is a prophet without honor.”

5 So He could not perform any miracles there, except to lay His hands on a few of the sick and heal them. 6 And He was amazed at their unbelief. And He went around from village to village, teaching the people.

What is the context?

Jesus comes to His hometown with His disciples and teaches in the synagogue on the Sabbath. The hometown is Nazareth, where He grew up (Luke 4:16). His listeners are astonished by His wisdom and mighty works, yet their questions move toward offense rather than trust.

They know His work and family: the carpenter, Mary's son, with brothers and sisters among them. What is familiar becomes a reason to doubt what they are hearing and seeing. Mark records their response without saying every relative has exactly the same thoughts.

Jesus answers with the saying about a prophet lacking honor in his hometown, among relatives, and in his own household. The people closest to a messenger can resist the message. This continues the earlier family and rejection themes, though the disciples still accompany Him.

Mark then says He could not perform miracles there, except that He heals a few sick people by laying His hands on them. The account keeps those healings alongside the strong statement about unbelief. Jesus is amazed at their unbelief and continues teaching in the surrounding villages. Their rejection does not end His mission.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see familiarity becoming an obstacle to receiving Jesus. On the statement that He could not perform miracles there, Wesley on Mark 6:5 understands the restraint in relation to Jesus' wisdom and goodness, while the US Catholic bishops' note on 6:5 emphasizes unbelief preventing His power from taking effect.

Traditions also differ over the brothers' relationship to Mary. The Catholic Church teaches in Catechism 499–500 that Mary remained a virgin and these are close relatives, rather than other children of hers. The Orthodox Church in America's account of Joseph presents the Orthodox tradition that they are his children from an earlier marriage.

An Orthodox Presbyterian Church answer reads them as children born to Mary and Joseph after Jesus. Protestant readings also vary: Calvin on Matthew 13:55 understands brothers as wider relatives. These differences about the family tree should be distinguished from the passage's central account of rejecting Jesus despite hearing His wisdom.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Luke 4:16–30 gives a related Nazareth rejection scene. There Jesus points to Elijah sent to the widow in Sidon and Elisha's cleansing of Naaman the Syrian. Familiarity and ancestry do not give people control over God's mercy. This is a connection between the accounts; Mark does not identify the Scripture read or repeat all of Luke's dialogue.

John 1:10–13 places rejection and reception within a larger purpose. The One through whom the world was made is not recognized, yet those who receive Him and believe in His name are given the right to become God's children. Nearness by hometown or family is different from receiving the Son God has sent.
