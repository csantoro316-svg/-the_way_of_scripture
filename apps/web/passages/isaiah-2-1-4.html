Passage

Isaiah 2:1-4

Why do the nations come to learn from the Lord?

Berean Standard Bible

1 This is the message that was revealed to Isaiah son of Amoz concerning Judah and Jerusalem:

2 In the last days the mountain of the house of the LORD will be established as the chief of the mountains; it will be raised above the hills, and all nations will stream to it. 3 And many peoples will come and say: “Come, let us go up to the mountain of the LORD, to the house of the God of Jacob. He will teach us His ways so that we may walk in His paths.” For the law will go forth from Zion, and the word of the LORD from Jerusalem. 4 Then He will judge between the nations and arbitrate for many peoples. They will beat their swords into plowshares and their spears into pruning hooks. Nation will no longer take up the sword against nation, nor train anymore for war.

What is the context?

A new heading introduces another message concerning Judah and Jerusalem. In contrast with the corruption described in chapter 1, Isaiah sees the Lord’s house established above the mountains and the nations streaming toward it.

The peoples come to be taught, so that they can walk in God’s ways. His instruction goes out from Zion, and His decisions settle disputes between nations. Weapons become tools for farming, and the nations stop learning warfare.

Micah 4:1–5 contains closely matching words and adds the picture of people sitting safely beneath their vines and fig trees. The shared vision joins recognition of God’s authority with teaching, justice and peace.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readings differ over when and how this vision is fulfilled. Calvin understands the last days as beginning with Christ’s coming and continuing toward His return. He reads Zion’s exaltation through the gospel going out from it. Wesley likewise understands the mountain as God’s church and the instruction as the gospel reaching the nations.

The Catholic Church’s Second Vatican Council, Gaudium et spes 78, applies the swords-to-farming-tools promise to peacemaking grounded in Christ’s reconciling work. It also says the threat of war remains until Christ returns. This connects the promise with the church’s present responsibility without declaring the full peace already achieved.

Teachers who expect a future thousand-year reign of Christ on earth, such as John Walvoord, read the passage as a promise that Jerusalem will be His earthly capital and warfare will cease under His rule. On this reading, the geographical setting belongs to the promised fulfillment.

The difference concerns the relationship between the gospel’s present spread and the future completion of God’s reign. The readings share the hope of nations learning God’s ways and peace established under His authority.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The theme of God’s word reaching the nations from Jerusalem continues in Luke 24:44–49. Jesus announces that repentance and forgiveness will be proclaimed to all nations in His name, beginning in Jerusalem.

Acts 2:1–11 then describes people from many nations hearing God’s wonders in their own languages there. These texts connect Jerusalem, God’s message and the nations with Christ’s work. They do not describe all warfare as already ended; Isaiah’s picture continues to direct attention toward the fullness of God’s peace.
