Passage

Matthew 11:7-15

John the Messenger Who Prepared the Way

Berean Standard Bible

7 As John’s disciples were leaving, Jesus began to speak to the crowds about John: “What did you go out into the wilderness to see? A reed swaying in the wind? 8 Otherwise, what did you go out to see? A man dressed in fine clothes? Look, those who wear fine clothing are found in kings’ palaces. 9 What then did you go out to see? A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet.

10 This is the one about whom it is written: ‘Behold, I will send My messenger ahead of You, who will prepare Your way before You.’ 11 Truly I tell you, among those born of women there has risen no one greater than John the Baptist. Yet even the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.

12 From the days of John the Baptist until now, the kingdom of heaven has been subject to violence, and the violent lay claim to it. 13 For all the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John. 14 And if you are willing to accept it, he is the Elijah who was to come. 15 He who has ears, let him hear.

What is the context?

Once John's disciples leave, Jesus asks the crowds what drew them into the wilderness. John was no swaying reed or court figure dressed for luxury. The questions lead to a stronger description: he was a prophet, and more than a prophet, because he prepared the way for the One who was coming.

Jesus applies the messenger promise of Malachi 3:1 to John. His greatness is tied to that place in God's work. The saying about the least in the kingdom being greater puts the gift of belonging to God's kingdom above even John's remarkable role. It does not say that John was an enemy of God or that his faithful service counted for nothing.

Verse 12 is difficult. The Greek verb is βιάζω, biazō (bee-AH-zoh), meaning to use force. Thayer's entry explains this saying as people eagerly laying hold of the kingdom, while other interpreters understand hostile force against it. The BSB main text uses the latter kind of wording, and its footnote offers forceful advance as another translation. The different readings are explained below.

The Law and the Prophets pointed forward to what is now unfolding. Jesus identifies John with the promised Elijah of Malachi 4:5–6, and calls the crowd to listen. Luke 1:16–17 describes John's calling in terms of Elijah's spirit and power: turning people back to God and preparing them for the Lord. In Matthew 17:10–13, the disciples again understand Jesus' words about Elijah as referring to John.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly recognize John as the promised messenger who prepared for Jesus. Wesley on Matthew 11:9–11 emphasizes John's closeness to the fulfillment and the fuller knowledge of Christ available after His saving work. The US Catholic bishops' note on 11:11 emphasizes the greater privilege of belonging to the kingdom. These explanations both place John's greatness within the greater gift Jesus brings.

The force language in verse 12 receives different readings. Wesley on 11:12 pictures eager people pressing into the kingdom, like people storming a city. The US Catholic bishops' note on 11:12 instead understands opponents trying to keep people from receiving it. That is a difference between these commentators' explanations; neither note establishes what every Methodist or Catholic believes about the verse. The saying is not a command for the disciples to spread their message through physical attack.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John's work is one part of a promise moving toward salvation through Jesus. In Acts 13:23–26, Paul retells that movement: God brings the promised Savior from David's descendants, John prepares the people through a baptism of repentance, and the message of salvation is announced through Jesus.

The same chapter carries the story beyond that first announcement. In Acts 13:46–49, Paul and Barnabas proclaim salvation to Gentiles as well. John prepares the way in Israel, and the Savior he announces becomes the center of good news reaching the nations. The servant's importance lies in helping people receive that Savior.
