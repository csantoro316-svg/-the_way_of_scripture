Passage

Ezekiel 36:33-38

Why will people compare the restored land to Eden?

Berean Standard Bible

33 This is what the Lord GOD says: On the day I cleanse you from all your iniquities, I will cause the cities to be resettled and the ruins to be rebuilt. 34 The desolate land will be cultivated instead of lying desolate in the sight of all who pass through. 35 Then they will say, ‘This land that was desolate has become like the garden of Eden. The cities that were once ruined, desolate, and destroyed are now fortified and inhabited.’ 36 Then the nations around you that remain will know that I, the LORD, have rebuilt what was destroyed, and I have replanted what was desolate. I, the LORD, have spoken, and I will do it.

37 This is what the Lord GOD says: Once again I will hear the plea of the house of Israel and do for them this: I will multiply their people like a flock. 38 Like the numerous flocks for sacrifices at Jerusalem during her appointed feasts, so the ruined cities will be filled with flocks of people. Then they will know that I am the LORD.”

What is the context?

The Lord joins cleansing from sin with the rebuilding of cities and cultivation of the land. Passersby will see a place once desolate becoming like the garden of Eden.

The nations that remain will recognize the One who has rebuilt and replanted it. The Lord declares that He has spoken and will do it.

He also promises to hear Israel’s plea and multiply its people like a flock. The ruined cities will be filled with people as Jerusalem’s appointed feasts were associated with great flocks for sacrifice. The picture ends with living fullness where there had been emptiness.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry treats the promises as grounds for believers to plead for God’s grace and live in the renewed obedience it gives. Wesley likewise understands the invitation to inquire of the Lord as a call to prayer, rather than a reason to stop asking because a promise has been made.

Cambridge connects the final flock picture with the restoration of the people’s numbers. The Lord’s promised action and His willingness to receive their prayer are presented together.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The Eden picture opens onto a larger biblical hope in Revelation 22:1–5: the water of life, the tree of life, healing for the nations and an end to the curse. The throne of God and the Lamb stands at the center, and His servants see His face.

These scenes belong to their own settings, but the connection is clear: God’s purpose answers the ruin brought by sin with life, blessing and His presence. Ezekiel’s rebuilt land is part of that larger movement from desolation toward the life God gives.
