Passage

Genesis 3:8-13

God Arraigns Adam and Eve

Berean Standard Bible

8 Then the man and his wife heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the breeze of the day, and they hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God among the trees of the garden.

9 But the LORD God called out to the man, “Where are you?”

10 “I heard Your voice in the garden,” he replied, “and I was afraid because I was naked; so I hid myself.”

11 “Who told you that you were naked?” asked the LORD God. “Have you eaten from the tree of which I commanded you not to eat?”

12 And the man answered, “The woman whom You gave me, she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate it.”

13 Then the LORD God said to the woman, “What is this you have done?”

“The serpent deceived me,” she replied, “and I ate.”

What is the context?

The promised new wisdom has produced fear. Even after making coverings, the man and woman hide among the trees when they hear God. The garden that had been a place of provision becomes somewhere to avoid the One who provided it. These verses show what their disobedience has done to their relationship with Him and with each other.

God's questions bring the act into the open. He calls the man, asks about his nakedness, and asks directly whether he ate from the forbidden tree. The man points to the woman and reminds God that He gave her to him. The woman points to the serpent. Both acknowledge eating, but their answers also direct attention away from their own choices.

The order matters: God calls and questions them before announcing the consequences. The passage gives us their fear and their answers; it does not supply a full account of their private thoughts or pronounce that blaming another person removes responsibility. The next verses address the serpent, the woman, and the man in turn.

How is this passage commonly understood?

These verses are commonly read as the exposure of sin and the failure of blame to restore a broken relationship with God. Wesley's notes on verses 9–13 understand God's questions as a gracious call to acknowledge wrongdoing, while the Catholic Catechism, 399, describes the fear of God that follows the loss of their original holiness. Both explanations keep responsibility with the people who disobeyed, even though a deceiver and another person were involved.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The hiding here introduces a pattern the rest of Scripture confronts: people try to conceal what God already sees, yet reconciliation requires coming into the light. Hebrews 4:13–16 puts those truths together. Nothing is hidden from God's sight, and through Jesus the high priest, His people are invited to approach Him for mercy and help.

This is a Bible-wide connection, not a claim that God's question in the garden already explained the whole gospel. 1 John 1:8–9 carries the lesson forward: denying sin deceives us; confessing it meets God's promise of forgiveness and cleansing. God's purpose is not served by a better hiding place, but by restored fellowship with Him.
