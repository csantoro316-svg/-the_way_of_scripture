Passage

Matthew 7:7-11

Ask the Father for What Is Good

Berean Standard Bible

7 Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and the door will be opened to you. 8 For everyone who asks receives; he who seeks finds; and to him who knocks, the door will be opened.

9 Which of you, if his son asks for bread, will give him a stone? 10 Or if he asks for a fish, will give him a snake? 11 So if you who are evil know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!

What is the context?

After teaching about judgment and discernment, Jesus encourages His hearers to ask, seek, and knock. Each action is followed by assurance of a response. The picture is of approaching the Father with confidence, and the comparison that follows explains the character of the One being approached.

A child asks for bread or fish. Jesus asks whether the parent would substitute a stone or snake. Even people who share human sinfulness know the difference between feeding a child and giving something harmful. The Father in heaven is more trustworthy still.

The assurance reaches its conclusion in verse 11: the Father gives good things. The promise is understood in relation to His goodness, alongside the earlier prayer for His will in Matthew 6:9–13. The person asking approaches a Father, not a device for making every preference come true.

The related teaching in Luke 11:5–13 places asking, seeking, and knocking after a story about persistence, then names the Holy Spirit as the Father's gift. Matthew's following verse calls for treating others as one wishes to be treated. Trust in the Father and good conduct toward others both belong within the Sermon's call to a life directed toward God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read these verses as encouragement to keep praying with confidence in the Father's goodness. Wesley on Matthew 7:7–11, the Reformed Westminster Larger Catechism, questions 184–185, and the Catholic Compendium, question 575, all direct asking toward God's will and the good He gives, rather than treating Him as a means of obtaining whatever the person wants.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The Father's generosity includes His own Spirit. In the closely related saying in Luke 11:9–13, the Father gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask. That parallel helps place the promise within God's work of bringing people near and helping them live in His ways.

Ephesians 1:13–14 describes the promised Spirit as the pledge of believers' inheritance. The word is ἀρραβών, arrabōn (ar-rah-BOHN). Thayer's Greek Lexicon explains the picture as a deposit assuring the payment to come, and applies it to the Spirit as a foretaste of future blessing. God's gift gives present help and assurance of the future He promises.

What is our position?

Our study Prayer and Faith reads this promise alongside Luke 11:13, understanding the Father's highest good gift as His Spirit and the life He forms within His people. It therefore directs asking toward God's will and spiritual growth, rather than treating the passage as a guarantee of every desired outcome.

Our companion study What Does Prayer Look Like? shares that emphasis on surrender and trust. Both studies recognize that asking is not itself sinful; the question is what governs the asking and where the person's confidence rests.
