Passage

Isaiah 47:1-15

What becomes of power that believes it will never answer to anyone?

Berean Standard Bible

1 “Go down and sit in the dust, O Virgin Daughter of Babylon. Sit on the ground without a throne, O Daughter of the Chaldeans! For you will no longer be called tender or delicate. 2 Take millstones and grind flour; remove your veil; strip off your skirt, bare your thigh, and wade through the streams. 3 Your nakedness will be uncovered and your shame will be exposed. I will take vengeance; I will spare no one.” 4 Our Redeemer—the LORD of Hosts is His name— is the Holy One of Israel.

5 “Sit in silence and go into darkness, O Daughter of the Chaldeans. For you will no longer be called the queen of kingdoms. 6 I was angry with My people; I profaned My heritage, and I placed them under your control. You showed them no mercy; even on the elderly you laid a most heavy yoke. 7 You said, ‘I will be queen forever.’ You did not take these things to heart or consider their outcome.

8 So now hear this, O lover of luxury who sits securely, who says to herself, ‘I am, and there is none besides me. I will never be a widow or know the loss of children.’ 9 These two things will overtake you in a moment, in a single day: loss of children, and widowhood. They will come upon you in full measure, in spite of your many sorceries and the potency of your spells. 10 You were secure in your wickedness; you said, ‘No one sees me.’ Your wisdom and knowledge led you astray; you told yourself, ‘I am, and there is none besides me.’ 11 But disaster will come upon you; you will not know how to charm it away. A calamity will befall you that you will be unable to ward off. Devastation will happen to you suddenly and unexpectedly.

12 So take your stand with your spells and with your many sorceries, with which you have wearied yourself from your youth. Perhaps you will succeed; perhaps you will inspire terror! 13 You are wearied by your many counselors; let them come forward now and save you— your astrologers who observe the stars, who monthly predict your fate. 14 Surely they are like stubble; the fire will burn them up. They cannot deliver themselves from the power of the flame. There will be no coals to warm them or fire to sit beside. 15 This is what they are to you— those with whom you have labored and traded from youth— each one strays in his own direction; not one of them can save you.

What is the context?

Babylon is addressed as a royal daughter brought down from her throne to the dust. The language of grinding flour, wading through streams and exposed shame reverses her former luxury and status.

The reason for judgment is stated clearly. The Lord placed His people under Babylon’s power, but she showed no mercy, laying a heavy yoke even on the elderly. Her authority did not give her freedom to act cruelly without answering for it.

Babylon expects to remain queen forever. She claims unmatched greatness, imagines that no one sees her wrongdoing and assumes she will never know widowhood or the loss of children. Disaster overturns that confidence in a moment.

The final challenge exposes the failure of her spells and counselors. Those who predict by the stars cannot save her or even themselves. The fire that consumes them is no comforting hearth, and the chapter ends with no rescuer found among those she relied upon.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry, Wesley and the USCCB notes read the royal woman as a picture of Babylon’s fall from dominance into humiliation. The contrast is between the status she claimed and the helplessness the coming judgment reveals.

Henry and Wesley emphasize that being used in judgment did not excuse Babylon’s cruelty. The charge concerning the elderly makes the failure of mercy concrete.

They also connect the failed sorcery and counsel with misplaced confidence. Wealth, secrecy and supposed knowledge of the future cannot put Babylon beyond the Lord’s judgment. The warning concerns power that refuses to consider either its conduct or its end.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 18:4–10 takes up Isaiah’s picture directly in its portrayal of Babylon. She claims to sit as a queen, expects no widowhood or grief, and then faces devastating judgment in a single day.

The surrounding call is for God’s people to come out of her and avoid sharing in her sins. Revelation joins the collapse of proud luxury with God’s remembrance of wrongdoing and His power to judge.

This connection shows how the earlier Babylon becomes part of the New Testament’s larger warning about corrupt power. Its security is temporary; belonging to God calls for a different allegiance and a different way of life.
