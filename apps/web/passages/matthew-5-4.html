Passage

Matthew 5:4

Comfort for Those Who Mourn

Berean Standard Bible

4 Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.

What is the context?

This is the second blessing at the opening of the Sermon on the Mount. It follows the blessing of the poor in spirit and comes before the promise to the meek. Jesus speaks of people whose present condition is sorrow, yet promises that they will receive comfort.

The Greek verb is πενθέω, pentheō (pen-THEH-oh), to mourn or lament. Thayer's Greek Lexicon lists Matthew 5:4 among its uses. The word names the grief; it does not by itself specify the particular loss or wrongdoing being mourned. Understanding the surrounding blessings and Scripture's uses of mourning is therefore important.

The promise belongs with the larger hope of the kingdom announced through the list. Jesus is not praising pain for its own sake. He speaks of sorrow that will be answered by God's comfort, rather than left as the final condition of His people.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley's note on Matthew 5:4 understands the mourning as sorrow over one's own sins and the sins of others. In that reading, the promise answers a heart humbled before God, seeking His mercy rather than excusing wrongdoing.

Other Christian readings include the grief of suffering and loss. Calvin's commentary on Matthew 5:4 speaks of calamities and the comfort found in God. In a 2020 address, Pope Francis includes both grief over another person's death or suffering and sorrow for one's own sin. These readings differ over what people are mourning; each looks to God for comfort.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah 61:1–3 promises good news, comfort for mourners, and joy in place of ashes. Jesus' identification with Isaiah's mission in Luke 4:16–21 gives this older promise a place in the larger story of His coming. The comparison supports the hope of restoration behind His words here.

Scripture also gives clear examples of sorrow for sin turning toward God. Psalm 51:1–4, 17 seeks mercy with a broken and repentant heart, and 2 Corinthians 7:9–11 describes sorrow that leads to repentance. These passages show what grieving over sin can look like: seeking God's mercy and turning toward Him.

The final picture is one of comfort reaching completion. Revelation 21:3–4 describes God dwelling with His people and ending death, mourning, crying, and pain. The promise reaches beyond a temporary lift in mood to God's restoration of life with Him.
