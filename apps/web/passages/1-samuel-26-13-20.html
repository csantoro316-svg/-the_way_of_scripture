Passage

1 Samuel 26:13-20

What does David’s appeal reveal about the cost of being driven away?

Berean Standard Bible

13 Then David crossed to the other side and stood atop the mountain at a distance; there was a wide gulf between them. 14 And David shouted to the troops and to Abner son of Ner, “Will you not answer me, Abner?” “Who are you who calls to the king?” Abner replied. 15 So David said to Abner, “You are a man, aren’t you? And who in Israel is your equal? Why then did you not protect your lord the king when one of the people came to destroy him? 16 This thing you have done is not good. As surely as the LORD lives, all of you deserve to die, since you did not protect your lord, the LORD’s anointed. Now look around. Where are the king’s spear and water jug that were by his head?”

17 Then Saul recognized David’s voice and asked, “Is that your voice, David my son?” “It is my voice, my lord and king,” David said. 18 And he continued, “Why is my lord pursuing his servant? What have I done? What evil is in my hand? 19 Now please, may my lord the king hear the words of his servant: If the LORD has stirred you up against me, then may He accept an offering. But if men have done it, may they be cursed in the presence of the LORD! For today they have driven me away from sharing in the inheritance of the LORD, saying, ‘Go, serve other gods.’ 20 So do not let my blood fall to the ground far from the presence of the LORD. For the king of Israel has come out to look for a flea, like one who hunts a partridge in the mountains.”

What is the context?

From across a wide gulf, David calls to Abner and challenges his failure to protect Saul. The missing spear and water jug show that someone reached the king. David’s words expose the difference between the danger Saul imagines from David and the protection David has actually given him.

Saul recognizes his voice. David asks what evil he has done and why the king is pursuing him. If the LORD is dealing with sin, he appeals for an acceptable offering; if men are stirring up the pursuit, he calls them to account before God.

He describes the cost as being driven away from sharing in the LORD’s inheritance, as though told to serve other gods. His concern reaches beyond losing a comfortable home. The pursuit threatens his place among God’s people and their shared worship.

David again pictures himself as a flea and compares the hunt to chasing a partridge in the mountains. He asks that his blood not be shed far from the LORD’s presence. A king’s campaign against one servant is threatening both his life and his belonging.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Henry and Gill read the proposed offering as an appeal to seek peace with God if sin lies behind the conflict. David frames possibilities rather than announcing that he knows a specific hidden offense for which God has ordered Saul to kill him.

Wesley and Gill understand the command to serve other gods as the effect of driving David out, not necessarily words someone has literally spoken. Separation from Israel’s land and worship exposes him to life among idolatrous peoples.

Henry emphasizes the spiritual harm of cutting someone off from worship. The complaint takes belonging seriously, while David’s own prayers throughout his flight show that distance does not place him beyond God’s hearing.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

David’s grief over being pushed away from worship helps us see that God’s purposes concern more than individual survival. His people are called into a shared life before Him, and exclusion from that life is a serious wound.

Jesus opens a further horizon in John 4:19–24. Speaking with the Samaritan woman about the place of worship, He announces worship of the Father in spirit and truth that is not confined to their competing holy places. Salvation comes through the story of Israel, and the Father seeks true worshipers.

This is a wider biblical development, rather than a claim that David’s sentence directly predicts that conversation. It places his longing for access alongside the welcome Jesus announces, in which seeking the Father reaches beyond one location.
