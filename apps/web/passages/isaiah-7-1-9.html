Passage

Isaiah 7:1-9

What is Ahaz being asked to trust when Jerusalem is threatened?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now in the days that Ahaz son of Jotham, the son of Uzziah, was king of Judah, Rezin king of Aram marched up to wage war against Jerusalem. He was accompanied by Pekah son of Remaliah the king of Israel, but he could not overpower the city. 2 When it was reported to the house of David that Aram was in league with Ephraim, the hearts of Ahaz and his people trembled like trees in the forest shaken by the wind.

3 Then the LORD said to Isaiah, “Go out with your son Shear-jashub to meet Ahaz at the end of the aqueduct that feeds the upper pool, on the road to the Launderer’s Field, 4 and say to him: Calm down and be quiet. Do not be afraid or disheartened over these two smoldering stubs of firewood—over the fierce anger of Rezin and Aram and of the son of Remaliah. 5 For Aram, along with Ephraim and the son of Remaliah, has plotted your ruin, saying: 6 ‘Let us invade Judah, terrorize it, and divide it among ourselves. Then we can install the son of Tabeal over it as king.’

7 But this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘It will not arise; it will not happen. 8 For the head of Aram is Damascus, and the head of Damascus is Rezin. Within sixty-five years Ephraim will be shattered as a people. 9 The head of Ephraim is Samaria, and the head of Samaria is the son of Remaliah. If you do not stand firm in your faith, then you will not stand at all.’”

What is the context?

Ahaz faces an alliance between Rezin of Aram and Pekah of Israel, also called Ephraim. Their attack has not overpowered Jerusalem, but news of the alliance leaves the king and people shaking with fear.

The Lord sends Isaiah and his son Shear-jashub to meet Ahaz near the upper pool. Isaiah is to tell him to be calm: the two threatening kings are like smoldering pieces of wood, and their plan to replace him with the son of Tabeal will not stand.

The message addresses the house of David as well as the immediate military threat. It limits the two kings’ power and announces Ephraim’s coming collapse. Ahaz must stand firm in faith rather than treating the threat as greater than God’s promise.

2 Kings 16:5–9 supplies the wider setting: Ahaz appeals to Assyria for rescue and sends silver and gold from the temple and palace. His fear is part of a real political crisis, and his response reveals where he looks for security.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley emphasize the contrast between the enemies’ threatening appearance and God’s assurance that their plan will fail. Wesley connects the message with God’s commitment to David’s house and reads the warning about faith against Ahaz’s decision to seek Assyrian help.

The call concerns trusting a particular word God has given in this crisis. It is not a general promise that any plan a person believes in strongly enough must succeed.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The threat to David’s house belongs to the larger story of God keeping His promise concerning that royal line. In Luke 1:26–38, Gabriel announces that Jesus will receive David’s throne and reign over Jacob’s house forever.

Luke 1:67–75 likewise joins salvation from David’s house with God remembering His holy covenant. The connection reaches beyond Ahaz’s own character: God carries forward the promise that comes to its center in Christ.
