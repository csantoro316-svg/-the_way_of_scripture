Passage

2 Peter 2:17-22

Can a promise of freedom lead back into slavery?

Berean Standard Bible

17 These men are springs without water and mists driven by a storm. Blackest darkness is reserved for them. 18 With lofty but empty words, they appeal to the sensual passions of the flesh and entice those who are just escaping from others who live in error. 19 They promise them freedom, while they themselves are slaves to depravity. For a man is a slave to whatever has mastered him.

20 If indeed they have escaped the corruption of the world through the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, only to be entangled and overcome by it again, their final condition is worse than it was at first. 21 It would have been better for them not to have known the way of righteousness than to have known it and then to turn away from the holy commandment passed on to them. 22 Of them the proverbs are true: “A dog returns to its vomit,” and, “A sow that is washed goes back to her wallowing in the mud.”

What is the context?

Peter closes his warning about false teachers with pictures of disappointment: a spring that gives no water and mist swept away by a storm. Their impressive words offer what their lives cannot supply. They promise freedom while being mastered by the very corruption they encourage.

The people being lured are only just escaping from those who live in error. That makes the danger especially serious. Verse 18 describes their movement away from error; it does not say they narrowly escape a future sentence of judgment.

Verses 20–22 describe becoming entangled again after escaping the world’s corruption through knowing Jesus Christ. Peter says this return leaves people worse off than before. The dog proverb comes from Proverbs 26:11; the washed pig supplies a second picture of returning to what was left behind. The warning reaches beyond knowing the right words to the direction a life takes.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin and Wesley both understand the promised freedom as permission to follow sinful desires. They also take the return to corruption seriously. Their disagreement concerns what that return tells us about a person’s relationship with God.

Wesley reads the escape in verse 20 as escape through faith in Christ. He describes the return in verse 22 as receiving grace and then making shipwreck of faith. This is a reading in which people can turn away from a faith they genuinely had.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 17, and the 1689 Baptist Confession, chapter 17, teach that those God has truly called and made His own will finally persevere. Both allow that believers can fall into serious sin. Calvin’s comments on this passage distinguish falling under sin’s rule and abandoning the gospel from the sins of believers who continue resisting it. These confessions represent their subscribing churches, rather than every Baptist or every reader of Calvin.

The Catholic Church also teaches that faith is God’s gift and that it can be lost; see Catechism 162. That is its broader teaching about remaining in faith, rather than a line-by-line ruling on these verses. The point at issue is whether this return follows genuine saving faith or an escape that did not become lasting faith. None of these sources treats corruption as the freedom Christ gives.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John 8:31–36 connects Jesus’ work with freedom from slavery to sin. Romans 6:15–23 develops the same theme: grace leads people into obedience to God, with holiness and eternal life as its fruit. These passages show why Peter’s warning matters within God’s saving purpose. A message that keeps people under sin’s control contradicts the freedom the Son gives.
