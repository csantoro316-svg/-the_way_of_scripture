Passage

Genesis 37:1-11

Joseph’s Dreams

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now Jacob lived in the land where his father had resided, the land of Canaan.

2 This is the account of Jacob. When Joseph was seventeen years old, he was tending the flock with his brothers, the sons of his father’s wives Bilhah and Zilpah, and he brought their father a bad report about them.

3 Now Israel loved Joseph more than his other sons, because Joseph had been born to him in his old age; so he made him a robe of many colors. 4 When Joseph’s brothers saw that their father loved him more than any of them, they hated him and could not speak a kind word to him.

5 Then Joseph had a dream, and when he told it to his brothers, they hated him even more. 6 He said to them, “Listen to this dream I had: 7 We were binding sheaves of grain in the field, and suddenly my sheaf rose and stood upright, while your sheaves gathered around and bowed down to mine.”

8 “Do you intend to reign over us?” his brothers asked. “Will you actually rule us?” So they hated him even more because of his dream and his statements.

9 Then Joseph had another dream and told it to his brothers. “Look,” he said, “I had another dream, and this time the sun and moon and eleven stars were bowing down to me.”

10 He told his father and brothers, but his father rebuked him and said, “What is this dream that you have had? Will your mother and brothers and I actually come and bow down to the ground before you?” 11 And his brothers were jealous of him, but his father kept in mind what he had said.

What is the context?

Genesis returns from Esau's descendants to the account of Jacob's family. Joseph is seventeen and tends the flock with his brothers. He brings Jacob a bad report about some of them. The account does not give the report's details, but it soon makes clear how badly the family relationships have deteriorated.

Jacob loves Joseph more than his other sons and gives him a distinctive robe. The brothers recognize the favoritism and cannot speak kindly to Joseph. The BSB calls it a robe of many colors; Brown–Driver–Briggs understands the Hebrew description as a tunic reaching to the palms and soles. Whichever feature of its appearance is emphasized, the narrative itself makes its significance clear: it visibly marks Jacob's special treatment of Joseph.

Joseph's first dream shows his brothers' sheaves of grain bowing to his. They understand it as a claim that he will rule them, and their hatred grows. The second shows the sun, moon, and eleven stars bowing to him. Jacob interprets the figures in terms of the family and rebukes him, yet keeps the matter in mind.

The dreams introduce a future the family finds offensive before anyone knows how it will come about. Later, Genesis 42:6–9 explicitly recalls them when the brothers bow before Joseph in Egypt to buy grain. For now, Joseph has dreams of honor while his daily situation is becoming more hostile.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read the dreams as anticipating Joseph's later authority, while recognizing the harm caused by family favoritism and jealousy. Wesley's notes on Genesis 37:3–10 connect the first dream with the brothers' later bowing in Egypt and distinguish their hostility from Jacob's willingness to remember what Joseph said.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The story begins with a promised future that seems increasingly distant as events unfold. Psalm 105:16–22 later places Joseph's enslavement, hardship, and eventual authority within God's provision before the famine. The dream's grain imagery becomes especially fitting when the family must seek food from the brother it has rejected.

There is also a long-recognized Christian parallel between Joseph's rejection before his rise to authority and Jesus' suffering before His rise to glory; Wesley's introduction to Genesis 37 draws that comparison. Philippians 2:5–11 describes Jesus' suffering and the honor God gives Him. The parallel helps us see how rejection need not defeat God's saving purpose, without turning every detail of Joseph's dreams into a separate prediction about Jesus.

What is our position?

Worship as the Early Church Knew It distinguishes bowing as an act of respect from worship offered to God. Applied here, the bowing in Joseph's dreams expresses the family's submission to his future authority. The scene does not require us to understand them as treating Joseph as God.
