Passage

1 Samuel 14:24-36

What harm does Saul’s oath do to the people?

Berean Standard Bible

24 Now the men of Israel were in distress that day, for Saul had placed the troops under an oath, saying, “Cursed is the man who eats any food before evening, before I have taken vengeance on my enemies.” So none of the troops tasted any food. 25 Then all the troops entered the forest, and there was honey on the ground. 26 And when they entered the forest and saw the flowing honey, not one of them put his hand to his mouth, because they feared the oath. 27 Jonathan, however, had not heard that his father had bound the people with the oath. So he reached out the end of the staff in his hand, dipped it into the honeycomb, and put his hand to his mouth, and his eyes brightened. 28 Then one of the soldiers told him, “Your father bound the troops with a solemn oath, saying, ‘Cursed is the man who eats food today.’ That is why the people are faint.” 29 “My father has brought trouble to the land,” Jonathan replied. “Just look at how my eyes have brightened because I tasted a little of this honey. 30 How much better it would have been if the troops had eaten freely today from the plunder they took from their enemies! Would not the slaughter of the Philistines have been much greater?”

31 That day, after the Israelites had struck down the Philistines from Michmash to Aijalon, the people were very faint. 32 So they rushed greedily to the plunder, taking sheep, cattle, and calves. They slaughtered them on the ground and ate meat with the blood still in it. 33 Then someone reported to Saul: “Look, the troops are sinning against the LORD by eating meat with the blood still in it.” “You have broken faith,” said Saul. “Roll a large stone over here at once.” 34 Then he said, “Go among the troops and tell them, ‘Each man must bring me his ox or his sheep, slaughter them in this place, and then eat. Do not sin against the LORD by eating meat with the blood still in it.’” So that night everyone brought his ox and slaughtered it there. 35 Then Saul built an altar to the LORD; it was the first time he had built an altar to the LORD. 36 And Saul said, “Let us go down after the Philistines by night and plunder them until dawn, leaving no man alive!” “Do what seems good to you,” the troops replied. But the priest said, “We must consult God here.”

What is the context?

Saul puts the troops under an oath not to eat until evening, before he has taken vengeance on his enemies. The men fear the oath and leave available honey untouched. Jonathan has not heard the command. He eats some honey and is refreshed, then learns what his father has said.

Jonathan criticizes the restriction because the food would have strengthened the pursuit. The troops continue fighting but become very faint. They then rush to the captured animals and eat meat with the blood still in it. The account identifies that action as sin against the LORD, while the unnecessary food restriction came from Saul.

Saul intervenes by providing a place for the slaughter and telling the people not to eat with the blood. He also builds an altar. When he proposes continuing the pursuit through the night, the priest calls for seeking God’s direction first. The scene places concern for religious actions alongside the damaging consequences of Saul’s own oath.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry regards the order as unwise: any time gained by preventing a meal is offset by the strength lost. Wesley allows that Saul’s intention may have been good, while judging the restriction and threatened punishment too severe. Gill also sees the aim as completing the victory but considers the order harsh and imprudent. These readings do not all assign Saul the same inward motive.

Wesley emphasizes the contrast between fear of the king’s oath and failure to observe God’s command concerning blood. The people’s hunger explains the setting of their action without making Saul’s rule and God’s instruction identical. A leader’s unnecessary demand can make faithful conduct harder for the people under him.

Henry reads Saul’s altar-building critically in the wider account of his conduct. The narrator states that this is his first altar to the LORD. Henry sees a warning here: building something for worship does not by itself show that a ruler is listening to God.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Saul’s restriction offers a contrast with Jesus’ care for weary people. In Matthew 11:25–30, Jesus invites the burdened to come to Him, describes Himself as gentle and humble, and offers rest under His yoke. His authority gives a different picture from a ruler exhausting people through a needless demand.

This comparison concerns the character and purpose of leadership. It does not call God’s own instructions oppressive or turn every difficult command into a mistake. The larger hope is a Lord who knows His people’s needs and teaches them in gentleness, rather than treating their weakness as an obstacle to His own importance.
