Passage

Mark 8:14-21

Remembering the Bread, Missing the Meaning

Berean Standard Bible

14 Now the disciples had forgotten to take bread, except for one loaf they had with them in the boat. 15 “Watch out!” He cautioned them. “Beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and of Herod.” 16 So they began to discuss with one another the fact that they had no bread. 17 Aware of their conversation, Jesus asked them, “Why are you debating about having no bread? Do you still not see or understand? Do you have such hard hearts? 18 ‘Having eyes, do you not see? And having ears, do you not hear?’ And do you not remember?

19 When I broke the five loaves for the five thousand, how many basketfuls of broken pieces did you collect?” “Twelve,” they answered. 20 “And when I broke the seven loaves for the four thousand, how many basketfuls of broken pieces did you collect?” “Seven,” they said. 21 Then He asked them, “Do you still not understand?”

What is the context?

In the boat, the disciples have only one loaf because they forgot to bring bread. Jesus warns them about the leaven of the Pharisees and Herod, but their discussion stays on their lack of food. They hear a warning and mistake its subject.

Leaven is the small amount worked into dough that affects the batch. The Greek word ζύμη (zyme, DZOO-may) can describe an influence spreading through something; the context determines whether the picture is good or bad. Here Jesus warns against an influence they need to resist.

The surrounding accounts help explain the danger. The Pharisees have just demanded a sign to test Jesus. Herod's treatment of John in Mark 6:17–29 shows respect for a righteous man giving way to pressure and a deadly decision. Jesus is warning His own disciples, so they should not assume that being near Him makes them immune to wrong responses.

He presses them with questions about understanding, hardened hearts, seeing, hearing, and remembering. They can recall twelve baskets from the first feeding and seven from the second. Yet correct answers about the quantities have not become a clear grasp of what His works mean.

Matthew 16:5–12 explicitly identifies the leaven there as teaching. Luke 12:1, in another setting, identifies the Pharisees' leaven as hypocrisy. These passages help explain the picture; Mark's exchange ends with Jesus' unanswered question about whether the disciples understand.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly take the leaven as a warning about corrupting influence, rather than a prohibition about the bread the disciples may eat. Calvin on this passage connects it with teaching that draws people away from God's word; Wesley on verses 17–18 stresses that the disciples' present failure to understand does not by itself declare them finally rejected.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus' words about eyes and ears recall the warnings in Jeremiah 5:20–25 and Ezekiel 12:1–3. God's people can have the ability to see and hear while resisting what He shows them. This scriptural parallel gives weight to the disciples' failure: receiving evidence is meant to lead to a response, not merely to remembering details.

The leaven picture also appears in 1 Corinthians 5:6–8. Paul connects Christ's sacrifice as the Passover lamb with a shared life of sincerity and truth rather than malice and wickedness. The parallel extends Jesus' warning beyond the boat to the life of a community shaped by what Christ has done.
