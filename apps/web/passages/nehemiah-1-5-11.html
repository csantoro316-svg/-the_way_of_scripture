Passage

Nehemiah 1:5-11

How does God’s promise shape Nehemiah’s prayer?

Berean Standard Bible

5 Then I said: “O LORD, God of heaven, the great and awesome God who keeps His covenant of loving devotion with those who love Him and keep His commandments, 6 let Your eyes be open and Your ears attentive to hear the prayer that I, Your servant, now pray before You day and night for Your servants, the Israelites. I confess the sins that we Israelites have committed against You. Both I and my father’s house have sinned. 7 We have behaved corruptly against You and have not kept the commandments, statutes, and ordinances that You gave Your servant Moses.

8 Remember, I pray, the word that You commanded Your servant Moses when You said, ‘If you are unfaithful, I will scatter you among the nations, 9 but if you return to Me and keep and practice My commandments, then even if your exiles have been banished to the farthest horizon, I will gather them from there and bring them to the place I have chosen as a dwelling for My Name.’ 10 They are Your servants and Your people. You redeemed them by Your great power and mighty hand.

11 O Lord, may Your ear be attentive to my prayer and to the prayers of Your servants who delight to revere Your name. Give Your servant success this day, I pray, and grant him mercy in the sight of this man.” (At that time I was the cupbearer to the king.)

What is the context?

Nehemiah begins with God’s greatness and faithfulness to His covenant. He confesses the sins of Israel and explicitly includes his own sins and those of his family.

He then recalls the warning that disobedience brings scattering and the promise that God will gather His people when they return to Him. The language draws on the promise in Deuteronomy 30:1–10, including gathering people from the farthest horizon.

The prayer ends with a concrete request for success and mercy before a particular man. The final sentence explains why: Nehemiah is the king’s cupbearer. His concern for Jerusalem is moving toward an approach to the king.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Gill emphasize confession, God’s covenant promise and His past redemption of the people. Nehemiah’s confidence rests on who God is and what He has said, while his immediate request concerns the opportunity before him at court.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise in Deuteronomy 30:1–10 reaches beyond a change of location: it includes a people returning to God, loving Him and obeying Him. Nehemiah prays within that larger purpose of restored relationship and service.

The song at John the Baptist’s birth in Luke 1:68–79 also celebrates God remembering His covenant, bringing redemption and enabling His people to serve Him in holiness. Luke carries the hope forward toward salvation through the promised Savior and the forgiveness of sins. This is a broader connection with covenant faithfulness, not a claim that rebuilding Jerusalem’s wall is the whole fulfillment of that hope.
