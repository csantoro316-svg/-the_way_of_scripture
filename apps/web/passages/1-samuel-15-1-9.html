Passage

1 Samuel 15:1-9

Will Saul carry out the command he has been given?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then Samuel said to Saul, “The LORD sent me to anoint you king over His people Israel. Now therefore, listen to the words of the LORD. 2 This is what the LORD of Hosts says: ‘I witnessed what the Amalekites did to the Israelites when they opposed them on their way up from Egypt. 3 Now go and attack the Amalekites and devote to destruction all that belongs to them. Do not spare them, but put to death men and women, children and infants, oxen and sheep, camels and donkeys.’”

4 So Saul summoned the troops and numbered them at Telaim—200,000 foot soldiers and 10,000 men of Judah. 5 Saul came to the city of Amalek and lay in wait in the valley. 6 And he warned the Kenites, “Since you showed kindness to all the Israelites when they came up out of Egypt, go on and get away from the Amalekites. Otherwise I will sweep you away with them.” So the Kenites moved away from the Amalekites. 7 Then Saul struck down the Amalekites all the way from Havilah to Shur, which is east of Egypt. 8 He captured Agag king of Amalek alive, but devoted all the others to destruction with the sword. 9 Saul and his troops spared Agag, along with the best of the sheep and cattle, the fat calves and lambs, and the best of everything else. They were unwilling to devote them to destruction, but they devoted to destruction all that was despised and worthless.

What is the context?

Samuel reminds Saul that the LORD appointed him over His people and tells him to listen. The command concerns Amalek’s attack on Israel after the exodus. Exodus 17:8–16 records that attack and the announced judgment; Deuteronomy 25:17–19 recalls the assault on weary stragglers.

The order is severe and explicit. It includes people of every age, including children and infants, and the livestock. Devoting the people and possessions to destruction means Saul is not being sent to enrich himself with captives or valuable animals. The chapter makes obedience to this particular command its central question.

Saul assembles his troops and warns the Kenites to leave because they had shown kindness to Israel. He then defeats the Amalekites but takes Agag alive. Saul and his troops also keep the best livestock and valuable things, destroying what they consider worthless. They have made their own assessment of what to preserve instead of carrying out the order as given.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Henry read the command as a particular divine judgment linked with Amalek’s earlier violence. They take its severity seriously. In addressing the deaths of children, Wesley appeals to God’s authority over life and the Christian teaching of human sinfulness from birth. That is his theological reasoning about a difficult part of the command, rather than an explanation separately spoken by Samuel in this scene.

Henry also notices the contrast between remembered cruelty and remembered kindness to the Kenites. The USCCB study notes emphasize that the order excludes taking plunder for personal use and that Saul fails to carry it out. Across these readings, the story is more than a report of winning a battle: it exposes selective obedience.

Wesley explicitly warns elsewhere in his comments on this chapter that its extraordinary judgment is not a precedent for private people to take the sword of justice into their own hands. The command belongs to this account and its named recipients; reading it does not give later readers the same commission.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The passage connects judgment with God’s earlier promise concerning an enemy that attacked vulnerable people. Within the larger biblical story, evil does not escape God simply because its reckoning is delayed. Revelation 19:11–16 presents the faithful and true rider, identified as the Word of God, judging in righteousness as King of kings.

This is a connection through the theme of divine judgment, rather than a claim that Revelation repeats Saul’s campaign or assigns its command to the church. The New Testament places final judgment under Christ’s authority. It gives the larger hope that violent opposition will not rule forever, while leaving this passage’s difficult details to be faced honestly.
