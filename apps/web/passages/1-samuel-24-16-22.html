Passage

1 Samuel 24:16-22

What changes when Saul sees that David has spared him?

Berean Standard Bible

16 When David had finished saying these things, Saul called back, “Is that your voice, David my son?” Then Saul wept aloud 17 and said to David, “You are more righteous than I, for you have rewarded me with good, though I have rewarded you with evil. 18 And you have declared this day how you have treated me well, for when the LORD delivered me into your hand, you did not kill me. 19 When a man finds his enemy, does he let him go away unharmed? May the LORD reward you with good for what you have done for me this day. 20 Now I know for sure that you will be king and that the kingdom of Israel will be established in your hands. 21 So now, swear to me by the LORD that you will not cut off my descendants or wipe out my name from my father’s house.” 22 So David gave his oath to Saul. Then Saul returned home, but David and his men went up to the stronghold.

What is the context?

Saul answers David with tears and calls him his son. He acknowledges that David has treated him well while he has treated David badly. He recognizes the mercy of being spared and asks the LORD to reward David for it.

He also says that David will surely become king and that Israel’s kingdom will be established in his hands. He asks David to swear that he will not destroy Saul’s descendants or erase his family’s name. David gives his oath.

The encounter ends peacefully, but the two men go separate ways: Saul returns home, and David and his men go to the stronghold. The chapter records a real acknowledgment of David’s righteousness without presenting a return to their former life together.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley emphasizes Saul being moved by David’s kindness and acknowledging both David’s integrity and his own wrong. Henry and Gill stress the difference between being deeply affected and showing lasting repentance. Their caution concerns whether this recognition will change Saul’s conduct.

Wesley and Gill explain David’s return to the stronghold as caution learned from Saul’s earlier changes of mind. Mercy does not require him to assume the danger is over. The peaceful parting leaves room for both the oath and that practical caution.

The promise concerns Saul’s descendants and the preservation of his family’s name. Gill relates it to David’s later care for members of Saul’s house. It asks whether the future king will show mercy once he has power, continuing a concern already raised in David’s covenant with Jonathan.
