Passage

Mark 1:29-34

Healing in a Home and Help for the Crowds

Berean Standard Bible

29 As soon as Jesus and His companions had left the synagogue, they went with James and John to the home of Simon and Andrew. 30 Simon’s mother-in-law was sick in bed with a fever, and they promptly told Jesus about her. 31 So He went to her, took her by the hand, and helped her up. The fever left her, and she began to serve them.

32 That evening, after sunset, people brought to Jesus all the sick and demon-possessed, 33 and the whole town gathered at the door. 34 And He healed many who were ill with various diseases and drove out many demons. But He would not allow the demons to speak, because they knew who He was.

What is the context?

After leaving the synagogue, Jesus goes with James and John to Simon and Andrew's home. Simon's mother-in-law is in bed with a fever. They tell Him about her, and He takes her hand and helps her up. The fever leaves, and she begins serving them.

Serve is διακονέω, diakoneō (dee-ah-koh-NEH-oh). Thayer's entry explains its use here as waiting on people, including providing food and drink. Her return to ordinary service shows the change in her condition. The word does not make this an appointment to a church office.

That evening, after sunset, the scene widens. People bring the sick and those troubled by demons, and a large crowd gathers at the door. Jesus heals many and drives out many demons. Mark distinguishes the illnesses from the spirits; the passage does not identify every sickness as possession.

Jesus again refuses to let the demons speak because they know who He is. The movement from the synagogue to a family home and then the crowded doorway shows His care reaching people in both public and personal settings.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see Jesus' compassion and authority in these healings, with the woman's restored ability to serve showing how fully she has been helped. Calvin on Mark 1:29–34 emphasizes the personal experience of His grace and the further requests for help, while the Catholic Catechism, 1503–1505, connects His care for the sick with the saving work of God's kingdom.

On the silencing of the demons, Calvin on 1:34 suggests both the timing of the fuller revelation and Jesus' refusal to use evil spirits as His witnesses. Wesley on 1:26 likewise emphasizes that He does not seek their endorsement. Mark states that they knew Him, while leaving their silencing's fuller purpose for readers to consider as His story continues.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament directly connects these healings with the suffering servant. Matthew 8:14–17 recounts the same woman's healing and the evening help for others, then applies Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus bearing people's infirmities. The care shown in this home belongs within His larger work against the suffering and sin that burden humanity.

There is also a simple pattern worth noticing: she receives help and then helps others. Later Jesus teaches that greatness among His followers means serving, and explains that He Himself came to serve and give His life for many (Mark 10:42–45). Her response is an early picture of that kind of life, without making recovery from sickness a condition for being useful to God.
