Passage

Mark 13:28-31

What should the fig tree teach us?

Berean Standard Bible

28 Now learn this lesson from the fig tree: As soon as its branches become tender and sprout leaves, you know that summer is near. 29 So also, when you see these things happening, know that He is near, right at the door. 30 Truly I tell you, this generation will not pass away until all these things have happened. 31 Heaven and earth will pass away, but My words will never pass away.

What is the context?

Jesus turns to an ordinary seasonal observation. When a fig tree’s branches become tender and put out leaves, people recognize that summer is near. In the same way, those who see the events He has described are to recognize their significance. The illustration concerns learning from what can be observed.

The statement about this generation raises a question about which people and events Jesus means. The Greek word γενεά (genea, geh-neh-AH) can refer to people living at the same time; Thayer lists this verse under that sense. The central disagreement is how that group relates to the events in Jesus’ answer, rather than whether the word supplies a fixed forty-year countdown. (Thayer, γενεά)

Jesus finishes by contrasting the passing of heaven and earth with the permanence of His words. The next paragraph states that the day and hour are unknown. We need to keep the certainty of His promise together with that limit on knowing its timing.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley takes the events in the generation saying to concern the temple and city, placing that part of the promise within the first hearers’ lifetime. (Wesley, note on Mark 13:30)

A future reading takes the generation as those alive when the final signs occur; Walvoord includes this explanation in his discussion of the matching saying. (Walvoord, Matthew 24:34)

Scofield offers another reading: the family or people of Israel will continue until the promised events are fulfilled. His note appeals to the word’s family sense. These interpretations differ over the group being described and how much of the preceding discourse is included; none turns the saying into a date Jesus names. (Scofield, note on Matthew 24:34)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The enduring word is also the means by which God gives His people new life. Peter contrasts human life, which fades like grass, with God’s word, which remains, and identifies that word with the good news preached to his readers. Jesus’ assurance belongs within a larger biblical confidence that God’s promises outlast the world’s apparent permanence. (1 Peter 1:22–25)

Peter also connects the passing of the present heavens and earth with the promise of a new heaven and a new earth where righteousness dwells. God’s future calls for faithful living now, and His patience leaves room for repentance. The promise’s purpose extends beyond satisfying curiosity about dates. (2 Peter 3:8–15)
