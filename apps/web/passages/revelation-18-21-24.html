Passage

Revelation 18:21-24

Why does the angel throw a millstone into the sea?

Berean Standard Bible

21 Then a mighty angel picked up a stone the size of a great millstone and cast it into the sea, saying: “With such violence the great city of Babylon will be cast down, never to be seen again. 22 And the sound of harpists and musicians, of flute players and trumpeters, will never ring out in you again. Nor will any craftsmen of any trade be found in you again, nor the sound of a millstone be heard in you again. 23 The light of a lamp will never shine in you again, and the voices of a bride and bridegroom will never call out in you again. For your merchants were the great ones of the earth, because all the nations were deceived by your sorcery.” 24 And there was found in her the blood of prophets and saints, and of all who had been slain on the earth.

What is the context?

A mighty angel casts a stone the size of a great millstone into the sea. He explains the action: Babylon will be thrown down violently and will not be found again. The visible sign makes the announcement of her downfall unmistakable.

The loss is then described through the silence and darkness of everyday life. Music stops. Craftsmen and the sound of grinding grain disappear. Lamps no longer shine, and the voices of bride and bridegroom are no longer heard. The city’s bustle and apparent permanence have come to an end.

The closing charges return attention to why judgment has come. Its merchants possessed great power, its sorcery deceived the nations, and it was found responsible for the blood of prophets, saints and those slain on the earth. The chapter closes by exposing the violence beneath the wealth.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Walvoord and the USCCB notes connect the sinking-stone action with Jeremiah’s earlier sign against Babylon. The shared emphasis is decisive judgment and the end of the city’s former power, despite differences over Babylon’s identity and the physical or symbolic details of its downfall.

The final bloodshed charge is read as a statement of Babylon’s guilt in persecution and violence. The proposed historical identifications seek to explain how a city or power fits that description.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The sign directly recalls Jeremiah 51:61–64. Jeremiah tells Seraiah to read the prophecy against Babylon, tie a stone to the scroll and throw it into the Euphrates as a picture of Babylon sinking and not rising again. Revelation’s millstone in the sea uses a closely related picture to announce the finality of the downfall.

The silence of this corrupt city also forms a contrast with the city introduced in Revelation 21:1–4. There, the new Jerusalem is prepared as a bride, God dwells with His people, and death and sorrow pass away. Babylon’s end is part of a larger purpose that reaches beyond destruction to life with God, free from the powers that deceived and shed blood.
