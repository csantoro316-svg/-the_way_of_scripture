Passage

Genesis 16:1-16

Hagar and Ishmael

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now Abram’s wife Sarai had borne him no children, but she had an Egyptian maidservant named Hagar. 2 So Sarai said to Abram, “Look now, the LORD has prevented me from bearing children. Please go to my maidservant; perhaps I can build a family by her.” And Abram listened to the voice of Sarai. 3 So after he had lived in Canaan for ten years, his wife Sarai took her Egyptian maidservant Hagar and gave her to Abram to be his wife. 4 And he slept with Hagar, and she conceived. But when Hagar realized that she was pregnant, she began to despise her mistress.

5 Then Sarai said to Abram, “May the wrong done to me be upon you! I delivered my servant into your arms, and ever since she saw that she was pregnant, she has treated me with contempt. May the LORD judge between you and me.”

6 “Here,” said Abram, “your servant is in your hands. Do whatever you want with her.” Then Sarai treated Hagar so harshly that she fled from her.

7 Now the angel of the LORD found Hagar by a spring of water in the desert—the spring along the road to Shur. 8 “Hagar, servant of Sarai,” he said, “where have you come from, and where are you going?” “I am running away from my mistress Sarai,” she replied.

9 So the angel of the LORD told her, “Return to your mistress and submit to her authority.” 10 Then the angel added, “I will greatly multiply your offspring so that they will be too numerous to count.”

11 The angel of the LORD proceeded: “Behold, you have conceived and will bear a son. And you shall name him Ishmael, for the LORD has heard your cry of affliction.

12 He will be a wild donkey of a man, and his hand will be against everyone, and everyone’s hand against him; he will live in hostility toward all his brothers.”

13 So Hagar gave this name to the LORD who had spoken to her: “You are the God who sees me,” for she said, “Here I have seen the One who sees me!” 14 Therefore the well was called Beer-lahai-roi. It is located between Kadesh and Bered.

15 And Hagar bore Abram a son, and Abram gave the name Ishmael to the son she had borne. 16 Abram was eighty-six years old when Hagar bore Ishmael to him.

What is the context?

Ten years after Abram's arrival in Canaan, Sarai still has no child. She gives her Egyptian servant Hagar to Abram as a wife, hoping to build a family through her. Hagar conceives, begins to despise Sarai, and becomes the subject of a bitter dispute. Abram leaves Hagar in Sarai's power, and Sarai treats her so harshly that she runs away. The plan produces a pregnancy, but it also tears the household apart.

The angel of the LORD finds Hagar at a spring in the desert. He tells her to return and gives her a promise of many descendants. Her son is to be named Ishmael because the LORD has heard her affliction. Hagar responds by naming the LORD as the God who sees her. In a story where others have treated her as a means to obtain a child, God addresses her personally and speaks about her future.

The word translated angel is מַלְאָךְ, malʾāḵ (mal-AHK), meaning messenger; Brown–Driver–Briggs includes both human messengers and God's angelic messengers among its uses. The word alone does not decide the speaker's identity. Here the speaker promises to multiply Hagar's offspring, and the account then calls the One who spoke to her the LORD. That movement is important to the Christian readings below.

The direction to return is given to Hagar in this particular encounter, together with God's promise for her future. The account describes Sarai's treatment as harsh and Hagar's suffering as affliction. God's attention to Hagar brings that suffering into view. At the chapter's end, she bears the child and Abram names him Ishmael; Abram is eighty-six.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see the harm caused by the household's attempt to secure a child and God's concern for Hagar in her distress. Where do readings differ? One reading identifies the Angel as the Son of God appearing before His human birth. Hilary of Poitiers, On the Trinity 5.11–13, explicitly reads Hagar's encounter this way, understanding messenger as the Son's task rather than a denial of His divine nature.

Another reading understands such appearances as God speaking through angels who represent Him. Augustine, On the Trinity 3.11.27, explains the appearances to the early fathers through angelic messengers speaking or acting in God's name. The difference concerns how God makes Himself known in the encounter; both readings take His words and care seriously.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

There is a striking parallel with the Exodus. Here God hears an Egyptian woman's affliction in Abram's household. In Exodus 3:7–10, He sees Israel's affliction in Egypt, hears their cry, and acts to deliver them. In both scenes, suffering that might be ignored by those with power is heard by God. His concern is not confined to one side of an ethnic boundary.

Galatians 4:21–31 explicitly uses the later story of Hagar, Sarah, and their sons to explain the contrast between slavery under the law and the freedom of children born through God's promise. Paul addresses the way people seek their standing before God. His use of the family story does not cancel the care God shows Hagar and her child in Genesis.

What is our position?

Our What Does Prayer Look Like? study uses Abram and Sarai's plan as a warning against trying to force the outcome we expect instead of trusting God's timing.

Who is The Angel of the Lord? identifies the Angel who speaks with Hagar as the eternal Son appearing before His human birth. The study draws that conclusion from His promise to multiply her offspring and Hagar's recognition of the LORD as the One who spoke to her.
