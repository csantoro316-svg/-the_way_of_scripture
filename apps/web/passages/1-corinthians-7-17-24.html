Passage

1 Corinthians 7:17-24

Must coming to Christ change a person’s social position?

Berean Standard Bible

17 Regardless, each one should lead the life that the Lord has assigned to him and to which God has called him. This is what I prescribe in all the churches. 18 Was a man already circumcised when he was called? He should not become uncircumcised. Was a man still uncircumcised when called? He should not be circumcised. 19 Circumcision is nothing and uncircumcision is nothing. Keeping God’s commandments is what counts. 20 Each one should remain in the situation he was in when he was called.

21 Were you a slave when you were called? Do not let it concern you—but if you can gain your freedom, take the opportunity. 22 For he who was a slave when he was called by the Lord is the Lord’s freedman. Conversely, he who was a free man when he was called is Christ’s slave. 23 You were bought at a price; do not become slaves of men. 24 Brothers, each one should remain in the situation he was in when God called him.

What is the context?

Paul broadens the discussion beyond marriage: live faithfully in the circumstances in which God called you. He uses circumcision and slavery as examples of outward conditions that do not determine whether someone can belong to Christ and serve Him.

The circumcised person need not undo that condition, and the uncircumcised person need not acquire it. What matters is obedience to God. Paul is rejecting a requirement to gain a different religious or social identity before one can live faithfully.

To the enslaved person, he gives assurance that this condition does not exclude the Lord’s call, while the BSB explicitly encourages taking an opportunity for freedom. The free person also belongs to Christ. Both are reminded that they were bought at a price, so human status cannot become their ultimate master.

Remaining with God is the thread holding the examples together. This does not require refusing every opportunity for change: verse 21 itself allows movement into freedom. The call is to live before God, rather than assuming that another outward condition is what makes faithfulness possible.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as directing attention from outward status to faithful life with God. Calvin’s comment on verse 21 and Wesley’s note both recognize freedom as preferable when it becomes available, rather than treating remaining in slavery as the condition for serving Christ.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Galatians 3:26–29 describes people united in Christ across the distinctions of Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female. Those who belong to Christ share in the promise given to Abraham.

Philemon 15–17 gives a concrete example of a changed relationship: Paul asks that Onesimus be received as a beloved brother and as Paul himself would be received. God’s call reaches people within unequal circumstances while bringing them into a shared belonging that changes how they should treat one another.
