Passage

Isaiah 24:1-23

What can stand when rebellion brings judgment on the earth?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Behold, the LORD lays waste the earth and leaves it in ruins. He will twist its surface and scatter its inhabitants— 2 people and priest alike, servant and master, maid and mistress, buyer and seller, lender and borrower, creditor and debtor. 3 The earth will be utterly laid waste and thoroughly plundered. For the LORD has spoken this word. 4 The earth mourns and withers; the world languishes and fades; the exalted of the earth waste away. 5 The earth is defiled by its people; they have transgressed the laws; they have overstepped the decrees and broken the everlasting covenant. 6 Therefore a curse has consumed the earth, and its inhabitants must bear the guilt; the earth’s dwellers have been burned, and only a few survive.

7 The new wine dries up, the vine withers. All the merrymakers now groan. 8 The joyful tambourines have ceased; the noise of revelers has stopped; the joyful harp is silent. 9 They no longer sing and drink wine; strong drink is bitter to those who consume it. 10 The city of chaos is shattered; every house is closed to entry. 11 In the streets they cry out for wine. All joy turns to gloom; rejoicing is exiled from the land. 12 The city is left in ruins; its gate is reduced to rubble. 13 So will it be on the earth and among the nations, like a harvested olive tree, like a gleaning after a grape harvest.

14 They raise their voices, they shout for joy; from the west they proclaim the majesty of the LORD. 15 Therefore glorify the LORD in the east. Extol the name of the LORD, the God of Israel in the islands of the sea. 16 From the ends of the earth we hear singing: “Glory to the Righteous One.” But I said, “I am wasting away! I am wasting away! Woe is me.” The treacherous betray; the treacherous deal in treachery.

17 Terror and pit and snare await you, O dweller of the earth. 18 Whoever flees the sound of panic will fall into the pit, and whoever climbs from the pit will be caught in the snare. For the windows of heaven are open, and the foundations of the earth are shaken. 19 The earth is utterly broken apart, the earth is split open, the earth is shaken violently. 20 The earth staggers like a drunkard and sways like a shack. Earth’s rebellion weighs it down, and it falls, never to rise again.

21 In that day the LORD will punish the host of heaven above and the kings of the earth below. 22 They will be gathered together like prisoners in a pit. They will be confined to a dungeon and punished after many days. 23 The moon will be confounded and the sun will be ashamed; for the LORD of Hosts will reign on Mount Zion and in Jerusalem, and before His elders with great glory.

What is the context?

The view widens from messages about particular nations to a devastated earth. The opening places people of every rank together: priest and people, servant and master, buyer and seller, lender and borrower. Status does not provide an escape.

The reason is stated in verse 5. The inhabitants have defiled the earth by transgressing laws, overstepping decrees and breaking the everlasting covenant. The curse falls on a world marked by rebellion, and only a few remain.

Ordinary celebration disappears. Wine, music and the city’s former activity give way to silence, grief and ruins. A remnant is pictured as the few olives or grapes left after harvest.

Praise nevertheless sounds from the west, the east and the ends of the earth. The song does not make the rest of the warning disappear: the speaker immediately laments treachery, and terror, pit and snare confront the earth’s inhabitants.

The closing scene reaches both the heavenly host and earthly kings. They are confined and await punishment, while the Lord reigns on Mount Zion and in Jerusalem with glory before His elders. The account moves beyond the collapse of human security to the open display of His rule.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads the opening land and world language primarily in relation to Canaan and Judah, including the judgments brought through Assyria and Babylon. He also applies the later verses to Christ’s kingdom and to the people gathered under His rule.

Henry places greater emphasis on the wider world’s ruin through sin and the eventual new heavens and new earth. He sees the preserved remnant as people who can rejoice in God even while the surrounding world falls apart.

The readings therefore differ over the reach and sequence of the judgment. They share the emphasis that sin brings ruin, earthly distinctions cannot secure safety, and the Lord preserves people who give Him glory.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The movement from judgment to God’s glorious rule has a wider New Testament counterpart in Revelation 20:11–15 and 21:1–5. John describes final judgment, followed by a new heaven and new earth where God dwells with His people (Revelation 21).

The light imagery also has a close parallel. Isaiah’s sun and moon are put to shame before the Lord’s glory; in Revelation 21:22–24, the city needs no sun or moon because God’s glory lights it and the Lamb is its lamp.

These are supported connections between the passages, rather than a detailed timetable supplied by Isaiah 24. The larger hope is that judgment does not leave the world in the hands of its ruined powers: God makes His dwelling with His people and brings His purpose to completion.
