Passage

Acts 28:1-10

What do the people of Malta learn from Paul’s survival and care?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Once we were safely ashore, we learned that the island was called Malta. 2 The islanders showed us extraordinary kindness. They kindled a fire and welcomed all of us because it was raining and cold. 3 Paul gathered a bundle of sticks, and as he laid them on the fire, a viper, driven out by the heat, fastened itself to his hand. 4 When the islanders saw the creature hanging from his hand, they said to one another, “Surely this man is a murderer. Although he was saved from the sea, Justice has not allowed him to live.” 5 But Paul shook the creature off into the fire and suffered no ill effects. 6 The islanders were expecting him to swell up or suddenly drop dead. But after waiting a long time and seeing nothing unusual happen to him, they changed their minds and said he was a god.

7 Nearby stood an estate belonging to Publius, the chief official of the island. He welcomed us and entertained us hospitably for three days. 8 The father of Publius was sick in bed, suffering from fever and dysentery. Paul went in to see him, and after praying and placing his hands on him, he healed the man. 9 After this had happened, the rest of the sick on the island came and were cured as well. 10 The islanders honored us in many ways and supplied our needs when we were ready to sail.

What is the context?

The survivors learn that the island is Malta. Its people welcome them with a fire against the rain and cold. Paul helps gather wood, and a viper fastens onto his hand. The onlookers first decide he must be a murderer whom Justice will not allow to live. When he suffers no harm, they change their minds and call him a god.

Here Justice refers to the islanders’ idea of a divine power that punishes wrongdoing. The Greek word, dikē (DEE-kay), can mean justice or punishment; Thayer’s entry explains its use here for the goddess Justice. Luke reports the people’s changing conclusions without endorsing either one. They have not discovered Paul’s guilt or his identity by watching the snake.

Publius, the island’s chief official, welcomes the travelers for three days. His father is sick with fever and dysentery. Paul visits him, prays, lays hands on him and heals him. Other sick people come and are cured as well. The islanders honor the travelers and provide supplies when they leave. Luke records generous hospitality and healing, without telling us that every islander becomes a believer.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as God preserving His servant and extending care to the people who welcome the survivors. The onlookers’ reversal also exposes how quickly people can mistake an accident or an escape for proof of someone’s guilt or greatness. Paul’s prayer directs attention beyond himself.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise that Paul will bear witness in Rome, given in Acts 23:11, continues through an unexpected stay where other people receive help. The islanders care for the shipwrecked company, and Paul becomes a means of care for their sick. The delay is part of a story that concerns more than Paul’s arrival.

There is a limited parallel with Acts 14:8–18, where healing leads people to mistake Paul and Barnabas for gods. There the apostles explicitly turn the crowd toward the living God. Malta’s story is shorter and does not record the same speech. Both scenes help us distinguish God’s work through a person from the mistaken conclusion that the person is a god.
