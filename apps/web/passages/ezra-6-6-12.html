Passage

Ezra 6:6-12

How does Darius’s reply change the situation?

Berean Standard Bible

6 Therefore Darius decreed: To Tattenai governor of the region west of the Euphrates, Shethar-bozenai, and your associates and officials in the region: You must stay away from that place! 7 Leave this work on the house of God alone. Let the governor and elders of the Jews rebuild this house of God on its original site. 8 I hereby decree what you must do for these elders of the Jews who are rebuilding this house of God: The cost is to be paid in full to these men from the royal treasury out of the taxes of the provinces west of the Euphrates, so that the work will not be hindered.

9 Whatever is needed—young bulls, rams, and lambs for burnt offerings to the God of heaven, as well as wheat, salt, wine, and oil, as requested by the priests in Jerusalem—must be given to them daily without fail. 10 Then they will be able to offer sacrifices of a sweet aroma to the God of heaven and to pray for the lives of the king and his sons. 11 I also decree that if any man interferes with this directive, a beam is to be torn from his house and raised up, and he is to be impaled on it. And his own house shall be made a pile of rubble for this offense. 12 May God, who has caused His Name to dwell there, overthrow any king or people who lifts a hand to alter this decree or to destroy this house of God in Jerusalem. I, Darius, have issued the decree. Let it be carried out with diligence.

What is the context?

Darius tells the regional officials to leave the temple work alone and allow the Jewish governor and elders to rebuild on its original site. He also orders the full cost paid from regional tax revenue so that the work will not be hindered.

The decree supplies animals and other materials needed for the offerings, according to the priests’ requests. Darius gives a purpose for this support: sacrifices to the God of heaven and prayers for the lives of the king and his sons.

The order ends with a severe penalty for interference and a call for God to overthrow anyone who tries to destroy His house. It is the Persian king’s decree protecting this rebuilding project.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes help arriving through a ruler from whom it might not have been expected. Gill draws attention to the expanded practical support: the officials must provide resources for the work, not simply stop obstructing it. He also connects the requested prayers with prayer for rulers under whom God’s people live.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Prayer for rulers also appears in 1 Timothy 2:1–6. Believers are urged to pray for kings and everyone in authority, with a view to peaceful, godly lives. Paul places this within God’s desire that people come to the knowledge of the truth and the work of Jesus Christ as mediator.

The wider connection moves beyond the welfare of one royal family. God’s people can pray for public authorities while seeking the good of others and the spread of the truth about Christ.
