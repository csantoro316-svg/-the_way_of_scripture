Passage

Exodus 16:1-21

Why did God give Israel bread one day at a time?

Berean Standard Bible

1 On the fifteenth day of the second month after they had left the land of Egypt, the whole congregation of Israel set out from Elim and came to the Desert of Sin, which is between Elim and Sinai. 2 And there in the desert the whole congregation of Israel grumbled against Moses and Aaron. 3 “If only we had died by the LORD’s hand in the land of Egypt!” they said. “There we sat by pots of meat and ate our fill of bread, but you have brought us into this desert to starve this whole assembly to death!” 4 Then the LORD said to Moses, “Behold, I will rain down bread from heaven for you. Each day the people are to go out and gather enough for that day. In this way I will test whether or not they will follow My instructions. 5 Then on the sixth day, when they prepare what they bring in, it will be twice as much as they gather on the other days.” 6 So Moses and Aaron said to all the Israelites, “This evening you will know that it was the LORD who brought you out of the land of Egypt, 7 and in the morning you will see the LORD’s glory, because He has heard your grumbling against Him. For who are we, that you should grumble against us?” 8 And Moses added, “The LORD will give you meat to eat this evening and bread to fill you in the morning, for He has heard your grumbling against Him. Who are we? Your grumblings are not against us but against the LORD.”

9 Then Moses said to Aaron, “Tell the whole congregation of Israel, ‘Come before the LORD, for He has heard your grumbling.’” 10 And as Aaron was speaking to the whole congregation of Israel, they looked toward the desert, and there in a cloud the glory of the LORD appeared. 11 Then the LORD said to Moses, 12 “I have heard the grumbling of the Israelites. Tell them, ‘At twilight you will eat meat, and in the morning you will be filled with bread. Then you will know that I am the LORD your God.’” 13 That evening quail came and covered the camp, and in the morning there was a layer of dew around the camp. 14 When the layer of dew had evaporated, there were thin flakes on the desert floor, as fine as frost on the ground. 15 When the Israelites saw it, they asked one another, “What is it?” For they did not know what it was. So Moses told them, “It is the bread that the LORD has given you to eat.

16 This is what the LORD has commanded: ‘Each one is to gather as much as he needs. You may take an omer for each person in your tent.’” 17 So the Israelites did this. Some gathered more, and some less. 18 When they measured it by the omer, he who gathered much had no excess, and he who gathered little had no shortfall. Each one gathered as much as he needed to eat. 19 Then Moses said to them, “No one may keep any of it until morning.” 20 But they did not listen to Moses; some people left part of it until morning, and it became infested with maggots and began to smell. So Moses was angry with them. 21 Every morning each one gathered as much as was needed, and when the sun grew hot, it melted away.

What is the context?

One month after the departure from Egypt, Israel reaches the Desert of Sin. Hunger turns their memory of slavery into a story about full meals, and they accuse Moses and Aaron of bringing them out to die. God answers by promising meat in the evening and bread in the morning. His provision will also test whether they follow His instructions.

The cloud reveals the LORD’s glory, quail cover the camp in the evening, and fine flakes appear after the morning dew evaporates. The people’s question is מָן הוּא, man hu (MAHN HOOH), rendered here as “What is it?” Brown–Driver–Briggs explains the connection between that question and the name manna. The name remembers their first puzzlement at the food Moses identifies as God’s gift.

Each household is to gather enough for its members. When the food is measured, those who gathered much have no excess and those who gathered little have no shortfall. The measure is an omer per person; the chapter later explains that an omer is a tenth of an ephah.

The people must trust God for the next day’s supply. Some keep food overnight against the instruction, and it fills with maggots and smells. The remaining manna on the ground melts in the heat. The provision is generous, but it comes with directions: receiving God’s gift includes listening to the One who gives it.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The central reading sees God providing for His people while teaching daily trust and obedience. Wesley and Calvin both connect the food with this test of whether Israel will follow God’s word. His care continues even while their complaints expose how much they still need to learn.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus explicitly returns to this event in John 6:26–35. When the crowd mentions the manna, He directs them to His Father as its giver and identifies Himself as the bread of life. In verses 47–51, He explains the difference: the ancestors ate manna and eventually died, while He gives life that lasts forever.

The gathering also becomes instruction for the church’s care of people in need. 2 Corinthians 8:12–15 quotes Exodus 16:18 while encouraging generous sharing. Paul calls those who currently have a surplus to supply those who lack, so that provision can be shared as circumstances change. The wilderness gift helps explain how believers should use what God has put in their hands.

What is our position?

Our Prayer and Faith study understands the manna as pointing forward to Jesus, the living bread. The manna sustained Israel’s bodies for a season; Jesus gives the lasting life to which that provision pointed. The study draws this conclusion from Jesus’ own explanation in John 6.
