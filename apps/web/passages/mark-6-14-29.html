Passage

Mark 6:14-29

John’s Death and Herod’s Fear

Berean Standard Bible

14 Now King Herod heard about this, for Jesus’ name had become well known, and people were saying, “John the Baptist has risen from the dead! That is why miraculous powers are at work in him.” 15 Others were saying, “He is Elijah,” and still others, “He is a prophet, like one of the prophets of old.” 16 But when Herod heard this, he said, “John, whom I beheaded, has risen from the dead!”

17 For Herod himself had ordered that John be arrested and bound and imprisoned, on account of his brother Philip’s wife Herodias, whom Herod had married. 18 For John had been telling Herod, “It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife!” 19 So Herodias held a grudge against John and wanted to kill him. But she had been unable, 20 because Herod feared John and protected him, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man. When he heard John’s words, he was greatly perplexed; yet he listened to him gladly.

21 On Herod’s birthday, her opportunity arose. Herod held a banquet for his nobles and military commanders and the leading men of Galilee. 22 When the daughter of Herodias came and danced, she pleased Herod and his guests, and the king said to the girl, “Ask me for whatever you wish, and I will give it to you.” 23 And he swore to her, “Whatever you ask of me, I will give you, up to half my kingdom!” 24 Then she went out and asked her mother, “What should I request?” And her mother answered, “The head of John the Baptist.” 25 At once the girl hurried back to the king with her request: “I want you to give me the head of John the Baptist on a platter immediately.”

26 The king was consumed with sorrow, but because of his oaths and his guests, he did not want to refuse her. 27 So without delay, the king commanded that John’s head be brought in. He sent an executioner, who went and beheaded him in the prison. 28 The man brought John’s head on a platter and presented it to the girl, who gave it to her mother. 29 When John’s disciples heard about this, they came and took his body and placed it in a tomb.

What is the context?

News of Jesus reaches Herod, and people offer different explanations of His identity: John raised from the dead, Elijah, or a prophet like those of old. Herod settles on John, the man he had beheaded. These are the speakers' conclusions about Jesus, not Mark saying that John has actually returned to life.

Mark then looks back to explain John's death. John had challenged Herod's marriage to his brother Philip's wife, Herodias. Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 give the scriptural background to his protest. Herodias wants John dead, while Herod recognizes him as righteous and holy, protects him for a time, and listens gladly despite being perplexed.

The Herod in this account is Antipas. The historian Josephus, Antiquities 18.5.1–2, also reports his imprisonment and execution of John, emphasizing Herod's fear that John's influence over crowds could lead to unrest. Mark follows the marriage protest and the events at the birthday banquet.

Herodias's daughter dances before Herod and his guests. He promises her whatever she asks, even up to half his kingdom. After consulting her mother, she requests John's head. Herod is deeply grieved, yet chooses to honor his public promise rather than refuse the killing. Mark places his oaths and his guests directly in the explanation of his decision.

John is executed in prison, his head is brought to the banquet, and his disciples take his body for burial. Herod's respect for John has not become obedience to the truth John spoke. The story also interrupts the account of the Twelve's mission: their sending in verses 7–13 is followed by their return in verse 30. The danger faced by a faithful messenger stands between those two scenes.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see John's faithful witness contrasted with Herod's refusal to act on what he recognizes as right. Calvin on Matthew 14:9 and Mark 6:26 and Wesley on Mark 6:26 emphasize the failure of an honor that keeps a rash promise at the cost of innocent life; sorrow over the outcome does not excuse the decision.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

John's suffering points ahead to the suffering of the One whose way he prepares. In Mark 9:11–13, Jesus joins the discussion of Elijah's coming with the Son of Man's rejection. The parallel Matthew 17:10–13 explicitly identifies John as the person in view and connects his mistreatment with what Jesus will suffer. This is a connection Jesus Himself draws.

There are further parallels in Mark 15:9–15,42–47: a ruler yields to pressure, an innocent man is killed, and someone who cares for Him seeks His body for burial. The parallel prepares us for the cost of Jesus' mission. His death also has a saving purpose He states directly: He gives His life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45). The Gospel leads beyond the silencing of a messenger to the Savior's own self-giving.
