Passage

Exodus 14:15-31

How did God make a way through the sea?

Berean Standard Bible

15 Then the LORD said to Moses, “Why are you crying out to Me? Tell the Israelites to go forward. 16 And as for you, lift up your staff and stretch out your hand over the sea and divide it, so that the Israelites can go through the sea on dry ground. 17 And I will harden the hearts of the Egyptians so that they will go in after them. Then I will gain honor by means of Pharaoh and all his army and chariots and horsemen. 18 The Egyptians will know that I am the LORD when I am honored through Pharaoh, his chariots, and his horsemen.”

19 And the angel of God, who had gone before the camp of Israel, withdrew and went behind them. The pillar of cloud also moved from before them and stood behind them, 20 so that it came between the camps of Egypt and Israel. The cloud was there in the darkness, but it lit up the night. So all night long neither camp went near the other. 21 Then Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, and all that night the LORD drove back the sea with a strong east wind that turned it into dry land. So the waters were divided, 22 and the Israelites went through the sea on dry ground, with walls of water on their right and on their left.

23 And the Egyptians chased after them—all Pharaoh’s horses, chariots, and horsemen—and followed them into the sea. 24 At morning watch, however, the LORD looked down on the army of the Egyptians from the pillar of fire and cloud, and He threw their camp into confusion. 25 He caused their chariot wheels to wobble, so that they had difficulty driving. “Let us flee from the Israelites,” said the Egyptians, “for the LORD is fighting for them against Egypt!” 26 Then the LORD said to Moses, “Stretch out your hand over the sea, so that the waters may flow back over the Egyptians and their chariots and horsemen.” 27 So Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, and at daybreak the sea returned to its normal state. As the Egyptians were retreating, the LORD swept them into the sea. 28 The waters flowed back and covered the chariots and horsemen—the entire army of Pharaoh that had chased the Israelites into the sea. Not one of them survived.

29 But the Israelites had walked through the sea on dry ground, with walls of water on their right and on their left. 30 That day the LORD saved Israel from the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians dead on the shore. 31 When Israel saw the great power that the LORD had exercised over the Egyptians, the people feared the LORD and believed in Him and in His servant Moses.

What is the context?

God tells Moses to send the people forward and stretch out his hand over the sea. The angel of God and the cloud move behind Israel, keeping the two camps apart during the night. The presence that has guided the people now stands between them and the pursuing army.

The LORD drives back the sea with a strong east wind through the night. Israel crosses on dry ground with walls of water on either side. When the Egyptians follow, the LORD throws them into confusion and makes their chariots difficult to drive. The soldiers themselves recognize that He is fighting for Israel.

At God’s command, Moses stretches out his hand again. The water returns and overwhelms the entire pursuing force that entered the sea. Israel reaches safety and sees the Egyptians dead on the shore. The chapter ends with the people fearing the LORD and believing Him and His servant Moses.

The sea’s Hebrew name is יַם־סוּף, yam suph (YAHM SOOHF). Brown–Driver–Briggs explains it as probably meaning sea of reeds and notes that the name is used for both the Gulf of Suez and the Gulf of Aqaba in different passages. The name alone does not identify the crossing site. Here the text emphasizes the dry path God opens, the waters on either side and the destruction of the pursuing army.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin and Wesley both understand the crossing as a miraculous deliverance: God makes the sea a safe passage for Israel and brings judgment on the pursuing Egyptians. Moses acts at God’s command, and the power that saves the people belongs to God.

There are different explanations of the angel in verse 19. Calvin identifies this leader with God’s Son before His birth as Jesus. The Catholic USCCB study note understands the expression here as God’s power at work, corresponding to the cloud, rather than a separate spiritual being. That is a reading offered in a Catholic Bible’s study notes; it is not presented there as a binding church definition of this verse.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 11:29 explicitly describes Israel’s crossing as an act of faith and contrasts it with the Egyptians’ attempt to follow. Both enter the same path, but the outcome depends on God’s purpose and promise, rather than the strength of the people or their equipment.

1 Corinthians 10:1–12 makes another connection for the church. Paul describes the people as baptized into Moses in the cloud and the sea. Their passage belongs to their shared life under the leader God has given them. Paul then recalls that many later fell into idolatry, disobedience and complaint. His point is a warning to believers: receiving God’s provision is no reason to become careless about following Him.

The rescue also becomes a pattern for later hope. In Isaiah 43:16–21, God recalls the way through the sea and the defeat of chariots as He promises a new work for His people, a way in the wilderness and water in the desert. The God who delivered them before remains able to bring His people where He intends them to go.
