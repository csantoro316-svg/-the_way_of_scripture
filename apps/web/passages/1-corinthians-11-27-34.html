Passage

1 Corinthians 11:27-34

What does it mean to examine ourselves before sharing the meal?

Berean Standard Bible

27 Therefore, whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty of sinning against the body and blood of the Lord. 28 Each one must examine himself before he eats of the bread and drinks of the cup. 29 For anyone who eats and drinks without recognizing the body eats and drinks judgment on himself.

30 That is why many among you are weak and sick, and a number of you have fallen asleep. 31 Now if we judged ourselves properly, we would not come under judgment. 32 But when we are judged by the Lord, we are being disciplined so that we will not be condemned with the world. 33 So, my brothers, when you come together to eat, wait for one another. 34 If anyone is hungry, he should eat at home, so that when you come together it will not result in judgment. And when I come, I will give instructions about the remaining matters.

What is the context?

The seriousness of the meal leads Paul to warn against eating or drinking in an unworthy manner. The warning concerns the way people participate. In this chapter, that includes selfishness, drunkenness, humiliation of the poor and failure to wait for one another.

Each person is to examine himself and then eat and drink. The instruction is a call to take part rightly, rather than to search for a sinless person who deserves Christ’s gift. Recognizing the body must be read with both the bread given in remembrance of Christ’s body and the divided people who are supposed to share as one body.

Paul identifies weakness, sickness and deaths among them as consequences of this wrongdoing. He distinguishes the Lord’s discipline from condemnation with the world: the correction has a purpose beyond punishment. The warning is serious without making the word judgment automatically mean final condemnation in every use.

His practical remedy returns to the problem: wait for one another, and satisfy hunger at home rather than turn the gathering into a selfish meal. Other matters can be addressed when he comes. This passage identifies a particular situation in Corinth; it does not give readers grounds to label every sick person as guilty of the same offense. John 9:1–3 also warns against assuming someone’s suffering proves a particular person’s sin.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand self-examination as necessary to honor Christ and participate responsibly. They place different emphasis on recognizing the body. Wesley’s notes on verses 27–29 emphasize treating the meal’s sacred signs according to their purpose; Calvin’s comment on verse 29 likewise stresses recognizing Christ’s body as holy.

The USCCB note on verse 28 connects recognizing Jesus’ self-giving with the unity it requires among those who share the meal. The emphases can overlap: honoring Christ’s death and caring for the people joined to Him both speak directly to the conduct Paul is correcting.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 12:5–11 describes the Lord’s discipline as a Father’s care for His children, intended to bring them to share His holiness and bear the fruit of righteousness and peace. Paul’s distinction between correction and condemnation fits that stated purpose.

The goal is not a community frightened away from the table, but a people whose conduct is being changed by the Lord they confess. His saving work calls for a shared life consistent with His gift, including honest self-examination and practical care for one another.
