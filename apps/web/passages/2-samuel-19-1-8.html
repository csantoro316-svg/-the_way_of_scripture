Passage

2 Samuel 19:1-8

Why does Joab urge the grieving king to face his people?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then it was reported to Joab, “The king is weeping and mourning over Absalom.” 2 And that day’s victory was turned into mourning for all the people, because on that day they were told, “The king is grieving over his son.” 3 So they returned to the city quietly that day, as people steal away in humiliation after fleeing a battle. 4 But the king covered his face and cried out at the top of his voice, “O my son Absalom! O Absalom, my son, my son!”

5 Then Joab went into the house and said to the king, “Today you have disgraced all your servants who have saved your life and the lives of your sons and daughters, of your wives, and of your concubines. 6 You love those who hate you and hate those who love you! For you have made it clear today that the commanders and soldiers mean nothing to you. I know today that if Absalom were alive and all of us were dead, it would have pleased you! 7 Now therefore get up! Go out and speak comfort to your servants, for I swear by the LORD that if you do not go out, not a man will remain with you tonight. This will be worse for you than all the adversity that has befallen you from your youth until now!” 8 So the king got up and sat in the gate, and all the people were told: “Behold, the king is sitting in the gate.” So they all came before the king. Meanwhile, the Israelites had fled, each man to his home.

What is the context?

Word of David’s mourning reaches the returning army. Instead of entering the city as victors, the soldiers come quietly and ashamed, while David continues crying for Absalom.

Joab confronts him, saying he has disgraced the people who saved him and his household. He accuses David of caring more for Absalom than for all his loyal servants and warns that they will abandon him if he does not speak to them.

David rises and sits in the gate, where the people come before him. He resumes his public responsibility without the account saying that his private grief has simply vanished.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry thinks David needed to respond to the people, but also criticizes the disrespect in Joab’s rebuke. A necessary point can be made in a way that deserves criticism itself.

Wesley explicitly treats Joab’s claim that David would prefer all his servants dead as an overstatement rather than an exact description of David’s wishes. The sharp language aims to awaken the king to how his behavior is affecting those who served him.

The scene presents a leader’s responsibilities during personal loss. It does not establish that grieving for a wayward child is itself wrong or that every bereaved person should respond on the same schedule.
