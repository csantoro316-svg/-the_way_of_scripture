Passage

1 Samuel 17:1-11

Why does Goliath’s challenge leave Israel afraid?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now the Philistines gathered their forces for war at Socoh in Judah, and they camped between Socoh and Azekah in Ephes-dammim. 2 Saul and the men of Israel assembled and camped in the Valley of Elah, arraying themselves for battle against the Philistines. 3 The Philistines stood on one hill and the Israelites stood on another, with the valley between them. 4 Then a champion named Goliath, who was from Gath, came out from the Philistine camp. He was six cubits and a span in height, 5 and he had a bronze helmet on his head. He wore a bronze coat of mail weighing five thousand shekels, 6 and he had armor of bronze on his legs and a javelin of bronze slung between his shoulders. 7 The shaft of his spear was like a weaver’s beam, and its iron point weighed six hundred shekels. In addition, his shield bearer went before him.

8 And Goliath stood and shouted to the ranks of Israel, “Why do you come out and array yourselves for battle? Am I not a Philistine, and are you not servants of Saul? Choose one of your men and have him come down against me. 9 If he is able to fight me and kill me, then we will be your servants. But if I prevail against him and kill him, then you shall be our servants and work for us.” 10 Then the Philistine said, “I defy the ranks of Israel this day! Give me a man to fight!” 11 On hearing the words of the Philistine, Saul and all the Israelites were dismayed and greatly afraid.

What is the context?

The Philistines and Israelites face each other across the Valley of Elah. Goliath comes forward from the Philistine camp with imposing armor, weapons and a shield-bearer. The extended description helps us feel the danger before anyone agrees to fight him.

The Hebrew phrase behind champion is אִישׁ הַבֵּנַיִם, ish habbenayim (eesh hah-beh-NAH-yim). Brown–Driver–Briggs explains it as the man of the space between two armies. Goliath steps into that space and proposes that one man from each side decide the outcome.

His terms concern far more than personal honor: the losing side will serve the winner. He defies Israel and demands an opponent. Saul and the people are dismayed and afraid. The king they wanted to fight their battles is facing a challenge from which the whole army shrinks.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Henry place the army’s fear within the larger question of dependence on God. An impressive king and previous victories do not make the people self-sufficient. Gill explains Goliath’s title in terms of standing between the armies or offering a duel, consistent with the scene’s stated challenge.

There is a difference in the ancient copies over Goliath’s height. The USCCB study notes report four cubits and a span in the Greek text and a Dead Sea Scroll, alongside the six-cubit reading used here in the BSB. Whatever reading is considered, the account describes a formidable opponent and sets his visible strength against the trust in God that David will show.
