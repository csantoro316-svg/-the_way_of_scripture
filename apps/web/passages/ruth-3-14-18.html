Passage

Ruth 3:14-18

What can Ruth do while Boaz settles the matter?

Berean Standard Bible

14 So she lay down at his feet until morning, but she got up before anyone else could recognize her. Then Boaz said, “Do not let it be known that a woman came to the threshing floor.” 15 And he told her, “Bring the shawl you are wearing and hold it out.” When she did so, he poured in six measures of barley and placed it on her. Then he went into the city.

16 When Ruth returned to her mother-in-law, Naomi asked her, “How did it go, my daughter?” Then Ruth told her all that Boaz had done for her. 17 And she said, “He gave me these six measures of barley, for he said, ‘Do not go back to your mother-in-law empty-handed.’” 18 “Wait, my daughter,” said Naomi, “until you find out how things go, for he will not rest unless he has resolved the matter today.”

What is the context?

Ruth remains at Boaz’s feet until morning and leaves before others can recognize her. Boaz asks that the visit not become known. He also sends her away with six measures of barley, placed in the shawl she holds out. The unit of measure is not specified in the account.

The BSB then says he goes into the city, preparing the next scene. Ruth returns to Naomi and reports what happened, including Boaz’s message that she should not return to her mother-in-law empty-handed. The gift continues his practical care while the larger question is being settled.

Naomi tells Ruth to wait. Her confidence is in Boaz’s determination: he will not rest until he resolves the matter that day. Ruth has made her request, and the next action belongs to the man who has promised to take responsibility.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads the discretion about the visit as concern for both reputations. Gill likewise explains the gift and the prompt action through Boaz’s care and commitment. Gill notes that the six measures do not identify a certain unit; his suggested quantity is an explanation, not a number supplied by the text.

Henry emphasizes patient trust after Ruth has done what she can. He compares her reliance on a willing family redeemer with believers bringing their need to Christ, who shares their humanity. His application invites faith in the Redeemer rather than treating waiting as proof that nothing is happening.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The comparison with Christ has a firm basis in what the New Testament says about His work. Hebrews 9:24–28 describes Him appearing before God on His people’s behalf and offering Himself once to bear sins. The decisive sacrifice is already made; those who belong to Him await His return to bring salvation.

Ruth waits for an earthly arrangement still to be completed. Christian hope rests in the Savior whose offering does not need to be repeated and whose promised return is still ahead. The shared lesson is confidence in the one undertaking the rescue, with patience grounded in His faithfulness.
