Passage

Ezra 7:21-26

How far does Ezra’s royal commission extend?

Berean Standard Bible

21 I, King Artaxerxes, decree to all the treasurers west of the Euphrates: Whatever Ezra the priest, the scribe of the Law of the God of heaven, may require of you, it must be provided promptly, 22 up to a hundred talents of silver, a hundred cors of wheat, a hundred baths of wine, a hundred baths of olive oil, and salt without limit. 23 Whatever is commanded by the God of heaven must be done diligently for His house. For why should wrath fall on the realm of the king and his sons? 24 And be advised that you have no authority to impose tribute, duty, or toll on any of the priests, Levites, singers, doorkeepers, temple servants, or other servants of this house of God.

25 And you, Ezra, according to the wisdom of your God, which you possess, are to appoint magistrates and judges to judge all the people west of the Euphrates—all who know the laws of your God. And you are to teach these laws to anyone who does not know them. 26 If anyone does not keep the law of your God and the law of the king, let a strict judgment be executed against him, whether death, banishment, confiscation of property, or imprisonment.

What is the context?

Artaxerxes directs the treasurers west of the Euphrates to supply Ezra promptly, within stated amounts of silver, wheat, wine and oil, with salt unrestricted. He also exempts temple personnel from the specified taxes.

The decree authorizes Ezra to appoint magistrates and judges and to teach those who do not know God’s laws. It closes with penalties under the law of God and the king, ranging from imprisonment and confiscation to banishment and death.

These instructions describe the legal authority the Persian king grants Ezra for this setting. They form part of the royal letter rather than a separate instruction from Jesus to His disciples.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill connect the wisdom entrusted to Ezra with his knowledge of God’s Law and the need for informed judgment. Henry distinguishes this royal authority from Christian ministry, which he describes in terms of faithful teaching, holy conduct, prayer and patient endurance. That distinction matters when applying a Persian decree beyond its original setting.
