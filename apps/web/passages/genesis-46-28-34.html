Passage

Genesis 46:28-34

Jacob Arrives in Egypt

Berean Standard Bible

28 Now Jacob had sent Judah ahead of him to Joseph to get directions to Goshen. When Jacob’s family arrived in the land of Goshen, 29 Joseph prepared his chariot and went there to meet his father Israel. Joseph presented himself to him, embraced him, and wept profusely.

30 Then Israel said to Joseph, “Finally I can die, now that I have seen your face and know that you are still alive!”

31 Joseph said to his brothers and to his father’s household, “I will go up and inform Pharaoh: ‘My brothers and my father’s household from the land of Canaan have come to me. 32 The men are shepherds; they raise livestock, and they have brought their flocks and herds and all that they own.’

33 When Pharaoh summons you and asks, ‘What is your occupation?’ 34 you are to say, ‘Your servants have raised livestock ever since our youth—both we and our fathers.’ Then you will be allowed to settle in the land of Goshen, since all shepherds are detestable to the Egyptians.”

What is the context?

Jacob sends Judah ahead to Joseph for directions to Goshen. When the family arrives, Joseph prepares his chariot and goes to meet his father. He embraces him and weeps at length. Jacob, who had expected to die without seeing him again, now says he can die knowing his son is alive.

The reunion is followed by arrangements for the household's future. Joseph will tell Pharaoh that his family has arrived from Canaan with flocks, herds, and possessions. He prepares them to answer the king's question about their occupation honestly: they have always raised livestock.

Joseph explains that this will allow them to settle in Goshen because shepherds are detestable to the Egyptians. Their occupation helps account for a distinct place to live within Egypt. The passage supplies that reason without describing every Egyptian religious or social custom behind it.

The family is being welcomed into a foreign land while retaining its identity and work. The next scene will show Pharaoh authorizing the settlement and providing an opportunity for capable men to care for his livestock.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as both an emotional reunion and preparation for the family's distinct settlement in Egypt. Wesley's note on Genesis 46:30 emphasizes Jacob's profound satisfaction in seeing Joseph. The following instructions concern the household's practical place and livelihood in the land.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The family can receive help in Egypt without simply disappearing into the surrounding people. Exodus 1:1–7 continues to identify them as Israel's household and describes their growth there. God's promise works through their life in a foreign land.

That situation offers a useful comparison with the wider call to live among others while remaining faithful to God. In John 17:14–19, Jesus asks that His disciples be kept from evil and shaped by God's truth while He sends them into the world. The comparison concerns faithfulness within a surrounding society, rather than treating physical isolation or ancestry as the source of holiness.
