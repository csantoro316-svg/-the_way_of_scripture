Passage

Hebrews 10:11-18

What does Christ’s one offering accomplish?

Berean Standard Bible

11 Day after day every priest stands to minister and to offer again and again the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. 12 But when this Priest had offered for all time one sacrifice for sins, He sat down at the right hand of God. 13 Since that time, He waits for His enemies to be made a footstool for His feet, 14 because by a single offering He has made perfect for all time those who are being sanctified.

15 The Holy Spirit also testifies to us about this. First He says: 16 “This is the covenant I will make with them after those days, declares the Lord. I will put My laws in their hearts and inscribe them on their minds.” 17 Then He adds: “Their sins and lawless acts I will remember no more.” 18 And where these have been forgiven, an offering for sin is no longer needed.

What is the context?

The daily standing service of the priests is contrasted with Christ seated at God’s right hand. Their offerings were repeated; His one sacrifice has lasting effect. His seated place also expresses His authority as He waits for His enemies to be made His footstool.

The passage holds completed work and ongoing holiness together: one offering has made perfect for all time those who are being sanctified. This assurance does not claim that believers have already stopped needing growth or that the following calls to obedience are unnecessary.

The Holy Spirit’s testimony comes through Jeremiah’s promise of inward renewal and forgiveness. The writer returns to the new covenant already discussed in chapter 8. Once sins are forgiven through Christ’s offering, another offering for sin is unnecessary.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin, Wesley and the USCCB notes read this as a declaration of the lasting sufficiency of Christ’s offering. Calvin also warns against treating His seated position as inactivity: He reigns and continues to act for His people.

Christians differ over how this relates to the Lord’s Supper. The Catholic Catechism, 1366–1367, teaches that the one sacrifice is made present in the Eucharist, the church’s celebration of the Lord’s Supper, without another death of Christ. The Westminster Confession, 29.1–2, describes the Supper as remembrance of the one offering and rejects offering Christ there for the forgiveness of sins. Both appeal to His one sacrifice, while differing over the meaning of the church’s celebration.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Psalm 110:1 supplies the picture of the exalted Lord with His enemies under His feet. 1 Corinthians 15:24–28 develops the expectation that Christ’s reign ends in the defeat of every enemy, including death.

The sacrifice is complete while the full display of His victory is still awaited. That distinction helps us understand why Hebrews can assure believers about forgiveness and still direct them toward a future Day.

What is our position?

Our study Worship as the Early Church Knew It identifies Christ’s sacrifice as the once-for-all offering that fulfills the earlier sacrifices. It distinguishes that saving work from believers offering their lives in grateful service, as in Romans 12:1. Our obedience responds to His completed offering; it does not make another payment for sin.
