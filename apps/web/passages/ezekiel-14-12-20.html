Passage

Ezekiel 14:12-20

Could Noah, Daniel and Job save an unfaithful land?

Berean Standard Bible

12 And the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 13 “Son of man, if a land sins against Me by acting unfaithfully, and I stretch out My hand against it to cut off its supply of food, to send famine upon it, and to cut off from it both man and beast, 14 then even if these three men—Noah, Daniel, and Job—were in it, their righteousness could deliver only themselves, declares the Lord GOD.

15 Or if I send wild beasts through the land to leave it childless and desolate, with no man passing through it for fear of the beasts, 16 then as surely as I live, declares the Lord GOD, even if these three men were in it, they could not deliver their own sons or daughters. They alone would be delivered, but the land would be desolate. 17 Or if I bring a sword against that land and say, ‘Let a sword pass through it,’ so that I cut off from it both man and beast, 18 then as surely as I live, declares the Lord GOD, even if these three men were in it, they could not deliver their own sons or daughters. They alone would be delivered.

19 Or if I send a plague into that land and pour out My wrath upon it through bloodshed, cutting off from it both man and beast, 20 then as surely as I live, declares the Lord GOD, even if Noah, Daniel, and Job were in it, they could not deliver their own sons or daughters. Their righteousness could deliver only themselves.

What is the context?

The Lord asks Ezekiel to consider a land that acts unfaithfully toward Him. Even if Noah, Daniel and Job were present, their righteousness would deliver only themselves.

The point is repeated through four kinds of judgment: famine, wild beasts, sword and plague. In each case, the presence of these righteous men would not secure the land’s release. They could not even deliver their sons or daughters from the judgment being described.

This is a hypothetical picture, not a claim that the three men lived together. Its repetition strips away the hope that association with outstandingly righteous people will shelter a population that refuses God.

The warning prepares for the direct application to Jerusalem in the next passage. The people must answer for their own unfaithfulness; someone else’s presence will not make it disappear.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read the three men as familiar examples of righteousness and effective prayer. They understand the warning to mean that even such people could not turn aside the judgment now facing this persistently unfaithful land.

There is a difference over the name Daniel. Henry and Wesley identify him with the biblical Daniel associated with Babylon. The USCCB notes instead identify the figure with Danel, a righteous judge known from Ugaritic tradition. That is the study note’s identification, rather than an official church definition of the name.

The force of the warning remains the same in either reading: the reputation and righteousness of exceptional people cannot be treated as automatic protection for others. The passage concerns the judgment of a land; it is not a denial that prayer for other people can matter.
