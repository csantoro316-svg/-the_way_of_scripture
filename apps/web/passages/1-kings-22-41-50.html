Passage

1 Kings 22:41-50

How does Jehoshaphat’s reign combine faithfulness with compromise?

Berean Standard Bible

41 In the fourth year of Ahab’s reign over Israel, Jehoshaphat son of Asa became king of Judah. 42 Jehoshaphat was thirty-five years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem twenty-five years. His mother’s name was Azubah daughter of Shilhi. 43 And Jehoshaphat walked in all the ways of his father Asa; he did not turn away from them, but did what was right in the eyes of the LORD. The high places, however, were not removed; the people still sacrificed and burned incense on the high places. 44 Jehoshaphat also made peace with the king of Israel.

45 As for the rest of the acts of Jehoshaphat, along with the might he exercised and how he waged war, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah? 46 He banished from the land the male shrine prostitutes who remained from the days of his father Asa. 47 And there was no king in Edom; a deputy served as king. 48 Jehoshaphat built ships of Tarshish to go to Ophir for gold, but they never set sail, because they were wrecked at Ezion-geber. 49 At that time Ahaziah son of Ahab said to Jehoshaphat, “Let my servants sail with your servants,” but Jehoshaphat refused. 50 And Jehoshaphat rested with his fathers and was buried with them in the city of his father David. And his son Jehoram reigned in his place.

What is the context?

The account returns to Judah and summarizes Jehoshaphat’s twenty-five-year reign. He follows his father Asa in doing what is right in the LORD’s eyes, but the high places remain in use. His peace with Israel also belongs to this mixed picture.

The king removes the remaining male shrine prostitutes. The account then mentions Edom’s deputy ruler and Jehoshaphat’s unsuccessful shipping venture: ships intended to obtain gold from Ophir are wrecked at Ezion-geber. He refuses Ahaziah’s offer to have their servants sail together.

2 Chronicles 20:31–37 provides further detail, connecting the loss of the ships with a rebuke for an alliance with Ahaziah. Jehoshaphat’s son Jehoram succeeds him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes the overall good character of Jehoshaphat’s reign. Wesley also takes the favorable assessment seriously while treating the alliance with Ahab as a blemish with consequences for the family.

Wesley and Gill read the refusal of Ahaziah’s proposal in light of the earlier failed partnership described in Chronicles. The two accounts together explain why this king can be commended for faithfulness while still needing correction.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The consequences of this family alliance continue beyond Jehoshaphat’s death. 2 Chronicles 21:1–7 explains that Jehoram married a daughter of Ahab and followed the ways of Ahab’s house. It then says the LORD did not destroy David’s house because of His covenant with David.

That continuation places the flawed reigns within the larger story of God keeping His promise. The covenant does not make the kings’ evil acceptable; it explains why their failures do not simply bring David’s family line to an end.
