Passage

Matthew 24:45-51

The Master Returns to Find How His Servants Have Lived

Berean Standard Bible

45 Who then is the faithful and wise servant, whom the master has put in charge of his household, to give the others their food at the proper time? 46 Blessed is that servant whose master finds him doing so when he returns. 47 Truly I tell you, he will put him in charge of all his possessions.

48 But suppose that servant is wicked and says in his heart, ‘My master will be away a long time.’ 49 And he begins to beat his fellow servants and to eat and drink with drunkards. 50 The master of that servant will come on a day he does not expect and at an hour he does not anticipate. 51 Then he will cut him to pieces and assign him a place with the hypocrites, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

What is the context?

Jesus answers the call to readiness with a household scene. A master places a servant in charge of providing food for the others at the proper time. The faithful and wise servant is blessed when the master returns and finds him doing that work, and he receives greater responsibility.

The other possibility is a servant who assumes the master will be away a long time. He begins beating his fellow servants and eating and drinking with drunkards. The delay becomes an excuse to abuse those placed in his care and live for himself.

The master returns unexpectedly and brings severe judgment, assigning the wicked servant a place with the hypocrites, with weeping and gnashing of teeth. Jesus ends this part of the teaching with both a blessing for faithful care and a warning against using His apparent absence as permission for wrongdoing.

Luke 12:35–48 gives a related teaching about servants, the returning master, and responsibility for what has been entrusted to them. The illustrations that follow in Matthew 25 continue the concern with readiness and accountability.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand readiness here as faithful service rather than merely watching for a date. Calvin on 24:45–51 particularly emphasizes the responsibility of those caring for others, and the US Catholic bishops' notes on 24:45–51 likewise connect watchfulness with carrying out the assigned duty.

Readers differ over how to describe the wicked servant's earlier relationship with the master. Churches following the Westminster Confession, 17, or the 1689 Baptist Confession, 17, hold that those truly brought to saving faith will finally persevere. Within that understanding, a final rejection is distinguished from a true believer's temporary fall into sin.

Wesley on 24:48–51 describes a servant who has put away faith and a good conscience. The Methodist Articles of Religion, XII, allow departure from grace and restoration through repentance; Catholic teaching also warns that faith can be lost (Catechism, 162). These readings differ about falling away, while retaining the seriousness of the warning against abuse and unfaithfulness.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God's purpose includes care for His household and an honest reckoning of those entrusted with it. 1 Corinthians 4:1–5 calls Christ's servants to faithfulness and looks toward His coming, when hidden things and motives are brought to light. 1 Corinthians 3:5–15 places the servants' different tasks within God's work: He gives the growth, and each person's labor is tested. Faithful service matters even when it attracts little public attention.

1 Peter 5:1–4 applies this care directly to elders, calling them to serve God's flock willingly, through example and without greed or domination, as they await the Chief Shepherd. The returning Lord seeks a people cared for in His name, with their leaders reflecting His character. Waiting for Him belongs together with serving the people He has entrusted to us.
