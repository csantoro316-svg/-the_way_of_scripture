Passage

2 Samuel 21:10-14

What does Rizpah do for the men who have died?

Berean Standard Bible

10 And Rizpah the daughter of Aiah took sackcloth and spread it out for herself on a rock. From the beginning of the harvest until the rain from heaven poured down on the bodies, she did not allow the birds of the air to rest on them by day, nor the beasts of the field by night. 11 When David was told what Saul’s concubine Rizpah, daughter of Aiah, had done,

12 he went and took the bones of Saul and his son Jonathan from the men of Jabesh-gilead, who had stolen them from the public square of Beth-shan where the Philistines had hung the bodies after they had struck down Saul at Gilboa. 13 So David had the bones of Saul and his son Jonathan brought from there, and they also gathered the bones of those who had been hanged. 14 And they buried the bones of Saul and his son Jonathan in Zela in the land of Benjamin, in the tomb of Saul’s father Kish. After they had done everything the king had commanded, God answered their prayers for the land.

What is the context?

Rizpah spreads sackcloth on a rock and keeps watch over the exposed bodies, preventing birds and wild animals from feeding on them. She remains from the beginning of harvest until rain falls on the bodies.

When David hears what she has done, he retrieves the bones of Saul and Jonathan from Jabesh-gilead. The bones of the executed men are gathered too. Saul and Jonathan are buried in the tomb of Saul’s father Kish at Zela.

After the king’s instructions have been carried out, God answers the prayers for the land. The episode ends with burial and answered prayer, while Rizpah’s persistent care remains central to the story.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley sees Rizpah’s example as prompting David to give Saul and Jonathan an honorable burial. Her grief and care for the dead move the king to act.

Gill notes the difficulty created by the exposed bodies, since Deuteronomy 21:22–23 requires burial on the day of execution. He treats the prolonged display as an exceptional case under divine permission. That permission is his explanation of the event, rather than an instruction quoted in this chapter.

The passage shows consequences reaching beyond the people who first committed the violence. Rizpah’s care refuses to let the dead be forgotten. The narrator also places God’s answer after the acts David orders, without giving a general rule that every unanswered prayer proves someone has failed to obey.
