Passage

Isaiah 33:1-24

Who can give lasting security when treaties and strength collapse?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Woe to you, O destroyer never destroyed, O traitor never betrayed! When you have finished destroying, you will be destroyed. When you have finished betraying, you will be betrayed. 2 O LORD, be gracious to us! We wait for You. Be our strength every morning and our salvation in time of trouble. 3 The peoples flee the thunder of Your voice; the nations scatter when You rise. 4 Your spoil, O nations, is gathered as by locusts; like a swarm of locusts men sweep over it. 5 The LORD is exalted, for He dwells on high; He has filled Zion with justice and righteousness. 6 He will be the sure foundation for your times, a storehouse of salvation, wisdom, and knowledge. The fear of the LORD is Zion’s treasure.

7 Behold, their valiant ones cry aloud in the streets; the envoys of peace weep bitterly. 8 The highways are deserted; travel has ceased. The treaty has been broken, the witnesses are despised, and human life is disregarded. 9 The land mourns and languishes; Lebanon is ashamed and decayed. Sharon is like a desert; Bashan and Carmel shake off their leaves. 10 “Now I will arise,” says the LORD. “Now I will lift Myself up. Now I will be exalted. 11 You conceive chaff; you give birth to stubble. Your breath is a fire that will consume you. 12 The peoples will be burned to ashes, like thorns cut down and set ablaze.

13 You who are far off, hear what I have done; you who are near, acknowledge My might.” 14 The sinners in Zion are afraid; trembling grips the ungodly: “Who of us can dwell with a consuming fire? Who of us can dwell with everlasting flames?” 15 He who walks righteously and speaks with sincerity, who refuses gain from extortion, whose hand never takes a bribe, who stops his ears against murderous plots and shuts his eyes tightly against evil— 16 he will dwell on the heights; the mountain fortress will be his refuge; his food will be provided and his water assured.

17 Your eyes will see the King in His beauty and behold a land that stretches afar. 18 Your mind will ponder the former terror: “Where is he who tallies? Where is he who weighs? Where is he who counts the towers?” 19 You will no longer see the insolent, a people whose speech is unintelligible, who stammer in a language you cannot understand. 20 Look upon Zion, the city of our appointed feasts. Your eyes will see Jerusalem, a peaceful pasture, a tent that does not wander; its tent pegs will not be pulled up, nor will any of its cords be broken. 21 But there the Majestic One, our LORD, will be for us a place of rivers and wide canals, where no galley with oars will row, and no majestic vessel will pass. 22 For the LORD is our Judge, the LORD is our lawgiver, the LORD is our King. It is He who will save us.

23 Your ropes are slack; they cannot secure the mast or spread the sail. Then an abundance of spoils will be divided, and even the lame will carry off plunder. 24 And no resident of Zion will say, “I am sick.” The people who dwell there will be forgiven of iniquity.

What is the context?

The chapter opens by warning a destroyer and traitor that the treatment given to others will return upon him. A prayer answers the threat: the people ask the Lord for grace, daily strength and salvation in trouble. Justice, righteousness, wisdom and reverence for Him are presented as the real sources of stability.

The suffering is visible. Peace envoys weep, highways are deserted, the treaty is broken and human life is disregarded. The land itself mourns. The Lord announces that He will rise, and the plans of the violent will become fuel for their own destruction.

The sight of His holiness also unsettles sinners within Zion. Their question about living with consuming fire is answered with a portrait of upright conduct: honest speech, refusal of extortion and bribes, and rejection of murderous plans and evil. The promised refuge belongs with a life that turns from wrongdoing.

The closing vision gives eyes something different to see: the King in His beauty, a land no longer hemmed in by terror, and Jerusalem as a secure tent. The Lord is named as Judge, lawgiver and King. The final promises reach beyond military relief to forgiveness of iniquity and the end of the residents’ sickness.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley identifies the destroyer with Sennacherib and connects the broken treaty with the Assyrian threat. Henry also reads the scene through Jerusalem’s distress and the Lord overthrowing a proud, treacherous enemy.

For the King in His beauty, Wesley begins with Hezekiah and then looks to Christ. Henry connects the promise with seeing Christ by faith and ultimately with the life in which sickness and trouble are gone.

Gill gives the secure Zion a wider application to the church, especially its future peace, and relates the final promises to the New Jerusalem. These readings differ over how the historical rescue opens into the larger promise. They do not reduce the last verse to military victory alone: forgiveness is central to the people’s restored life.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 12:22–29 brings together several of the same themes: Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, God as Judge, an unshakable kingdom and reverent worship before the God who is a consuming fire. This is a thematic connection with Isaiah 33, rather than a claim that Hebrews directly quotes it.

Hebrews places Jesus, the mediator of the new covenant, at the center of that approach to God. The response is gratitude and acceptable worship, together with the warning not to refuse the One who speaks.

Isaiah’s question about safety before a holy God therefore belongs within a larger account of both mercy and holiness. Lasting security is not simply being inside the right city: God saves and forgives His people and calls them into a life that receives His word.
