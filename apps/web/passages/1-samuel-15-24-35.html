Passage

1 Samuel 15:24-35

What does Saul’s confession change?

Berean Standard Bible

24 Then Saul said to Samuel, “I have sinned; I have transgressed the LORD’s commandment and your instructions, because I feared the people and obeyed their voice. 25 Now therefore, please forgive my sin and return with me so I can worship the LORD.” 26 “I will not return with you,” Samuel replied. “For you have rejected the word of the LORD, and He has rejected you as king over Israel.” 27 As Samuel turned to go, Saul grabbed the hem of his robe, and it tore. 28 So Samuel said to him, “The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today and has given it to your neighbor who is better than you. 29 Moreover, the Glory of Israel does not lie or change His mind, for He is not a man, that He should change His mind.”

30 “I have sinned,” Saul replied. “Please honor me now before the elders of my people and before Israel. Come back with me, so that I may worship the LORD your God.” 31 So Samuel went back with Saul, and Saul worshiped the LORD. 32 Then Samuel said, “Bring me Agag king of the Amalekites.” Agag came to him cheerfully, for he thought, “Surely the bitterness of death is past.” 33 But Samuel declared: “As your sword has made women childless, so your mother will be childless among women.” And Samuel hacked Agag to pieces before the LORD at Gilgal. 34 Then Samuel went to Ramah, but Saul went up to his home in Gibeah of Saul. 35 And to the day of his death, Samuel never again visited Saul. Samuel mourned for Saul, and the LORD regretted that He had made Saul king over Israel.

What is the context?

Saul confesses that he has sinned, saying that fear of the people led him to listen to them. He asks Samuel for forgiveness and to return with him for worship. Samuel refuses at first and repeats that the LORD has rejected Saul as king.

When Saul grabs Samuel’s robe and tears it, Samuel uses the torn garment to describe the kingdom being taken from him and given to a better neighbor. God’s declaration will not be undone by treating Him as a human being who lies or changes course under pressure.

Saul again admits his sin but also asks to be honored before the elders and Israel. Samuel returns with him, and Saul worships. Samuel then confronts Agag over the bloodshed of his own sword and executes him. The judgment is connected with Agag’s own violence, not only a story of distant ancestors.

Samuel goes home to Ramah, Saul returns to Gibeah, and their visits end. Yet Samuel mourns Saul, and the chapter closes by repeating God’s regret. Rejection from office has not made Saul’s fall an occasion for Samuel to celebrate.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ over Saul’s confession. Henry and Gill see it as compromised by blame-shifting and concern for public standing. His request for honor helps explain their reading. Wesley disagrees that hypocrisy has been proved: he allows Saul’s admission and request for pardon to be sincere.

Wesley also distinguishes rejection from kingship from a declaration that Saul is rejected from salvation. Whatever judgment a reader reaches about his motives, the sentence stated here concerns his royal office. The passage should not be made to answer every question about his final standing in a single phrase.

Gill reads God’s refusal to lie or change His mind as confirmation of the announced loss of Saul’s kingdom and God’s continuing purpose for His people. The USCCB study notes also emphasize the definitive character of the declaration. Samuel’s eventual return to worship with Saul therefore does not mean the judgment has been canceled.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The statement that God does not lie belongs to a wider theme of confidence in His word. Hebrews 6:13–20 recalls the promise to Abraham and grounds the heirs’ encouragement in God’s unchanging purpose and inability to lie.

Hebrews directs that confidence toward the hope opened by Jesus, who has entered God’s presence as forerunner and enduring high priest. Samuel’s statement confirms a judgment; Hebrews confirms a promise of hope. In both settings, God’s word is more dependable than the shifting words and intentions of human beings.
