Passage

Acts 8:9-25

Why does Peter rebuke Simon’s offer of money?

Berean Standard Bible

9 Prior to that time, a man named Simon had practiced sorcery in the city and astounded the people of Samaria. He claimed to be someone great, 10 and all the people, from the least to the greatest, heeded his words and said, “This man is the divine power called the Great Power.” 11 They paid close attention to him because he had astounded them for a long time with his sorcery. 12 But when they believed Philip as he preached the gospel of the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ, they were baptized, both men and women. 13 Even Simon himself believed and was baptized. He followed Philip closely and was astounded by the great signs and miracles he observed.

14 When the apostles in Jerusalem heard that Samaria had received the word of God, they sent Peter and John to them. 15 On their arrival, they prayed for them to receive the Holy Spirit. 16 For the Holy Spirit had not yet fallen upon any of them; they had simply been baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus. 17 Then Peter and John laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit.

18 When Simon saw that the Spirit was given through the laying on of the apostles’ hands, he offered them money. 19 “Give me this power as well,” he said, “so that everyone on whom I lay my hands may receive the Holy Spirit.” 20 But Peter replied, “May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could buy the gift of God with money! 21 You have no part or share in our ministry, because your heart is not right before God. 22 Repent, therefore, of your wickedness, and pray to the Lord. Perhaps He will forgive you for the intent of your heart. 23 For I see that you are poisoned by bitterness and captive to iniquity.” 24 Then Simon answered, “Pray to the Lord for me, so that nothing you have said may happen to me.” 25 And after Peter and John had testified and spoken the word of the Lord, they returned to Jerusalem, preaching the gospel in many of the Samaritan villages.

What is the context?

Simon has long impressed the people through sorcery and his claim to greatness. Philip’s message directs them toward God’s kingdom and Jesus Christ. Men and women believe and are baptized. Luke says Simon also believes and is baptized, then follows Philip in amazement at the signs.

When the apostles in Jerusalem hear that Samaria has received God’s word, they send Peter and John. The two pray and lay hands on the baptized believers, who receive the Holy Spirit. Luke explicitly describes that order here; it needs to be read alongside the other accounts of receiving the Spirit.

Simon sees what happens and offers money for the authority to give the Spirit through his own hands. Peter rebukes the attempt to buy God’s gift, exposes the condition of his heart, and calls him to repent and pray for forgiveness. Simon asks the apostles to pray for him. Luke does not describe the rest of his life.

The visit ends with Peter and John speaking God’s word and preaching in Samaritan villages on their return journey. The message is spreading through Philip and through the apostles who have joined the work.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that God’s gift cannot be bought. They differ over how the Samaritans’ reception of the Spirit relates to baptism and to the experience of other believers.

The Catholic Church’s Catechism, paragraphs 1315–1316, directly cites Acts 8:14–17 in teaching about confirmation. Paragraph 1288 understands the apostolic laying on of hands as its origin: a gift of the Spirit that completes and strengthens the grace God gives in baptism.

Reformed explanations also vary. Calvin on verse 16 understands the later reception as extraordinary gifts given to people in whom the Spirit was already at work giving new life. The 1978 Orthodox Presbyterian committee report, section I, places Samaria within the historical stages of the Spirit’s coming to the church, while teaching that every believer shares in baptism with the Spirit through belonging to Christ from the time they come to faith. That report is a committee’s explanation rather than an official confession of faith.

The Assemblies of God’s Statement of Fundamental Truths, sections 7–8, cites the Samaritan account in teaching a baptism in the Spirit distinct from and following the beginning of Christian life, giving power for service. It identifies tongues as the initial physical evidence of that experience. Luke does not explicitly name tongues in this scene.

Simon’s belief is another question. Calvin’s note on verse 13 understands a real conviction that the message is true, but without the new life and wholehearted response he distinguishes from that conviction. Thomas Constable on verse 13 instead understands Simon as a genuine believer who falls into serious sin. These are different readings of his condition, not different judgments about whether his offer needs repentance.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus had already been welcomed by Samaritans who confessed Him as Savior of the world in John 4:39–42. Now the risen Lord’s message brings baptized believers and the apostles into a shared work in Samaria. Their place in the story is not an afterthought to His mission.

John’s participation also provides a striking comparison with Luke 9:52–55. There, James and John wanted fire called down on an unwelcoming Samaritan village, and Jesus rebuked them. Here John prays for Samaritans to receive the Spirit and joins in proclaiming the good news among them. This comparison highlights the change in his service without claiming to know every motive behind his journey.
