Passage

Ezekiel 17:11-21

Why is the king’s broken oath also an offense against God?

Berean Standard Bible

11 Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 12 “Now say to this rebellious house: ‘Do you not know what these things mean?’ Tell them, ‘Behold, the king of Babylon came to Jerusalem, carried off its king and officials, and brought them back with him to Babylon. 13 He took a member of the royal family and made a covenant with him, putting him under oath. Then he carried away the leading men of the land, 14 so that the kingdom would be brought low, unable to lift itself up, surviving only by keeping his covenant. 15 But this king rebelled against Babylon by sending his envoys to Egypt to ask for horses and a large army. Will he flourish? Will the one who does such things escape? Can he break a covenant and yet escape?’

16 ‘As surely as I live,’ declares the Lord GOD, ‘he will die in Babylon, in the land of the king who enthroned him, whose oath he despised and whose covenant he broke. 17 Pharaoh with his mighty army and vast horde will not help him in battle, when ramps are built and siege walls constructed to destroy many lives. 18 He despised the oath by breaking the covenant. Seeing that he gave his hand in pledge yet did all these things, he will not escape!’

19 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘As surely as I live, I will bring down upon his head My oath that he despised and My covenant that he broke. 20 I will spread My net over him and catch him in My snare. I will bring him to Babylon and execute judgment upon him there for the treason he committed against Me. 21 All his choice troops will fall by the sword, and those who survive will be scattered to every wind. Then you will know that I, the LORD, have spoken.’

What is the context?

The Lord explains the story. Babylon’s king took Jerusalem’s king and officials away, then installed another member of the royal family under an oath. The reduced kingdom could continue by keeping that covenant.

Second Kings 24:10–20 identifies the kings: Jehoiachin was taken to Babylon, and his uncle Mattaniah was made king in his place and renamed Zedekiah.

Zedekiah sought horses and troops from Egypt in rebellion against Babylon. Ezekiel declares that Pharaoh will not deliver him and that he will die in the land of the king who placed him on the throne. Jeremiah 37:4–11 likewise warns that Egypt’s intervention will not prevent Jerusalem’s capture.

The Lord calls the despised oath His own and describes the king’s conduct as treason against Him. Second Chronicles 36:11–14 explains that Nebuchadnezzar had made Zedekiah swear by God. The broken pledge is therefore more than a failed political maneuver: the king has treated an oath made before the Lord as disposable.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry emphasizes that faithfulness is owed even to someone outside one’s own religion. Invoking God as witness while dealing treacherously makes the offense more serious. Wesley similarly reads the judgment as the consequence of perjury and the failure of the hoped-for escape.

The chapter holds the present king responsible for his own choices. Whatever earlier generations did, his own broken oath and refusal of the Lord’s warning give no basis for claiming innocence.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Matthew 5:33–37 addresses oaths and calls Jesus’ hearers to truthful speech: their yes and no should be dependable. James 5:12 repeats that instruction and connects it with avoiding judgment.

These passages do not directly interpret Zedekiah’s treaty or settle every later question about formal oaths. They develop the larger concern that words spoken before God cannot be used to disguise an intention to act otherwise. Following Christ includes integrity in ordinary speech as well as in public commitments.
