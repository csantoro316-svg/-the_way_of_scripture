Passage

Exodus 27:9-19

How did the courtyard mark out the place of worship?

Berean Standard Bible

9 You are also to make a courtyard for the tabernacle. On the south side of the courtyard make curtains of finely spun linen, a hundred cubits long on one side, 10 with twenty posts and twenty bronze bases, and silver hooks and bands on the posts. 11 Likewise there are to be curtains on the north side, a hundred cubits long, with twenty posts and twenty bronze bases, and with silver hooks and bands on the posts. 12 The curtains on the west side of the courtyard shall be fifty cubits wide, with ten posts and ten bases. 13 The east side of the courtyard, toward the sunrise, is to be fifty cubits wide.

14 Make the curtains on one side fifteen cubits long, with three posts and three bases, 15 and the curtains on the other side fifteen cubits long, with three posts and three bases. 16 The gate of the courtyard shall be twenty cubits long, with a curtain embroidered with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and finely spun linen. It shall have four posts and four bases. 17 All the posts around the courtyard shall have silver bands, silver hooks, and bronze bases. 18 The entire courtyard shall be a hundred cubits long and fifty cubits wide, with curtains of finely spun linen five cubits high, and with bronze bases. 19 All the utensils of the tabernacle for every use, including all its tent pegs and the tent pegs of the courtyard, shall be made of bronze.

What is the context?

Linen curtains enclose a courtyard one hundred cubits long, fifty wide and five high. Posts stand in bronze bases, with silver hooks and bands supporting the fabric. The eastern entrance has a twenty-cubit embroidered screen made with colored yarn and fine linen. Bronze pegs and utensils complete the instructions.

This enclosure marks out the area around the tent where the people approach for worship and sacrifice. It is distinct from the rooms inside the tabernacle. The later priestly regulations in Numbers 18:1–7 distinguish the work of Aaron’s priestly family from the assistance of the other Levites; belonging to Levi does not by itself authorize all priestly service.

Foreign residents are also addressed in the sacrificial laws. Leviticus 17:8–9 requires a foreigner living among Israel who offers a sacrifice to bring it to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting. Numbers 15:14–16 gives the same offering rule for Israel and the foreign resident. Those instructions belong alongside the boundaries and responsibilities of sanctuary worship.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The courtyard is generally understood as setting apart the place of worship while allowing the people to bring their offerings before God. Wesley also sees it as a picture of God’s people set apart to Him. He carries that comparison forward to the wider gathering of people who call on Christ, rather than treating the courtyard’s physical size as a limit on the people God will bring near.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Ephesians 2:11–22 describes Gentiles who were once far away being brought near through Christ’s blood. Jesus reconciles Jews and Gentiles in one body, and both have access to the Father through one Spirit. They become members of God’s household and a dwelling for His Spirit.

That is a broader biblical connection with the question of belonging and approach to God, not Paul directly explaining this linen fence. The sanctuary’s ordered worship belongs within a story that leads to people from different backgrounds being reconciled to God and to one another through Christ.
