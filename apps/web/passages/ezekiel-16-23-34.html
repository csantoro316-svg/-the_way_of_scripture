Passage

Ezekiel 16:23-34

Why does Jerusalem pay the very lovers it pursues?

Berean Standard Bible

23 Woe! Woe to you, declares the Lord GOD. And in addition to all your other wickedness, 24 you built yourself a mound and made yourself a lofty shrine in every public square. 25 At the head of every street you built your lofty shrines and degraded your beauty. With increasing promiscuity, you spread your legs to all who passed by. 26 You prostituted yourself with your lustful neighbors, the Egyptians, and increased your promiscuity to provoke Me to anger. 27 Therefore I stretched out My hand against you and reduced your portion. I gave you over to the desire of those who hate you, the daughters of the Philistines, who were ashamed of your lewd conduct.

28 Then you prostituted yourself with the Assyrians, because you were not yet satisfied. Even after that, you were still not satisfied. 29 So you extended your promiscuity to Chaldea, the land of merchants—but even with this you were not satisfied! 30 How weak-willed is your heart, declares the Lord GOD, while you do all these things, the acts of a shameless prostitute! 31 But when you built your mounds at the head of every street and made your lofty shrines in every public square, you were not even like a prostitute, because you scorned payment.

32 You adulterous wife! You receive strangers instead of your own husband! 33 Men give gifts to all their prostitutes, but you gave gifts to all your lovers. You bribed them to come to you from everywhere for your illicit favors. 34 So your prostitution is the opposite of that of other women: No one solicited your favors, and you paid a fee instead of receiving one; so you are the very opposite!

What is the context?

The accusation spreads through the city’s public spaces and outward to Egypt, Assyria and Chaldea. Jerusalem keeps pursuing other partners and remains unsatisfied.

The Lord has already acted against this conduct by reducing Jerusalem’s portion and handing her over to those who hate her. Even the Philistine cities, pictured as daughters, are said to be ashamed of her behavior.

The final comparison exposes how costly and self-defeating the pursuit has become. Instead of receiving gifts from lovers, Jerusalem gives gifts and bribes them to come.

The marriage picture makes the broken loyalty personal. The Lord has provided abundantly, but Jerusalem spends what it has received to secure relationships that displace Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley emphasizes the wasting of wealth and honor on the very relationships that corrupt Jerusalem’s worship. The Cambridge discussion of the chapter also connects the foreign partners with the influence of surrounding powers and their gods.

The repeated lack of satisfaction is central to the accusation. More partners and greater expense do not repair the estrangement from the Lord. The gifts that should have expressed gratitude are spent sustaining unfaithfulness.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

James 4:1–10 also uses marital unfaithfulness to describe divided loyalty toward God. James joins that warning with quarrels, coveting and requests driven by the desire to spend on pleasures.

The passage then calls for a return: submit to God, draw near to Him, cleanse hands and purify divided hearts. God opposes pride and gives grace to the humble.

James does not explicitly quote Ezekiel 16. The parallel develops the same concern beyond Jerusalem’s foreign relationships: desires that pull people away from God bring conflict, while His grace calls them back to wholehearted faithfulness.
