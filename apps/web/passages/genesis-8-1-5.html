Passage

Genesis 8:1-5

The Ark Rests on Ararat

Berean Standard Bible

1 But God remembered Noah and all the animals and livestock that were with him in the ark. And God sent a wind over the earth, and the waters began to subside. 2 The springs of the deep and the floodgates of the heavens were closed, and the rain from the sky was restrained. 3 The waters receded steadily from the earth, and after 150 days the waters had gone down.

4 On the seventeenth day of the seventh month, the ark came to rest on the mountains of Ararat. 5 And the waters continued to recede until the tenth month, and on the first day of the tenth month the tops of the mountains became visible.

What is the context?

The flood's rise has reached its turning point. God remembers Noah and the creatures with him, sends a wind, closes the sources of water, and brings about its retreat. The ark comes to rest, and later the mountain tops become visible. The changes come in stages, while Noah's family remains inside.

The Hebrew verb זָכַר, zākar (zah-KHAR), means remember. Brown–Driver–Briggs includes Genesis 8:1 among uses of God remembering a person with kindness, protection, or deliverance. The following actions explain the statement: God acts for those He has preserved. It is not a notice that Noah had slipped His mind.

The wind is רוּחַ, rûaḥ (ROO-akh), a word that can mean breath, wind, or spirit; Brown–Driver–Briggs identifies wind in this verse. The passage places the ark in the mountains of Ararat without naming an individual peak.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read God's remembering as faithful action for Noah and the creatures, and the retreat of the waters as the beginning of their release. Wesley's notes on verse 1 likewise describe a return of mercy rather than a lapse in God's awareness.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

God remembering and acting to deliver becomes a recurring pattern. In Exodus 2:23–25, He hears the oppressed Israelites and remembers His covenant before the account of their rescue. In Luke 1:68–75, the coming salvation is likewise explained through God's mercy and remembrance of His covenant.

These are connections across the Bible, not statements that each rescue is the same event. They show a consistent feature of God's purpose: He does not abandon His promises while His people wait. The wind and retreating water here are the concrete beginning of that faithfulness becoming visible to Noah.
