Passage

1 Samuel 30:21-31

Why does David share the plunder with those who could not fight?

Berean Standard Bible

21 When David came to the two hundred men who had been too exhausted to follow him and who were left behind at the Brook of Besor, they came out to meet him and the troops with him. As David approached the men, he greeted them, 22 but all the wicked and worthless men among those who had gone with David said, “Because they did not go with us, we will not share with them the plunder we recovered, except for each man’s wife and children. They may take them and go.” 23 But David said, “My brothers, you must not do this with what the LORD has given us. He has protected us and delivered into our hands the raiders who came against us. 24 Who will listen to your proposal? The share of the one who went to battle will match the share of the one who stayed with the supplies. They will share alike.” 25 And so it has been from that day forward. David established this statute and ordinance for Israel to this very day.

26 When David arrived in Ziklag, he sent some of the plunder to his friends, the elders of Judah, saying, “Here is a gift for you from the plunder of the LORD’s enemies.” 27 He sent gifts to those in Bethel, Ramoth Negev, and Jattir; 28 to those in Aroer, Siphmoth, and Eshtemoa; 29 to those in Racal and in the cities of the Jerahmeelites and Kenites; 30 to those in Hormah, Bor-ashan, and Athach; 31 and to those in Hebron and in all the places where David and his men had roamed.

What is the context?

David returns to the two hundred men left at Besor and greets them. Some who fought argue that the exhausted men should receive only their families back and no share of the recovered plunder.

David refuses. The LORD has protected them and given the victory, so the fighters cannot treat its benefits as theirs alone. Those who stayed with the supplies are to share equally with those who went into battle. The decision becomes an ordinance for Israel.

Back at Ziklag, David sends gifts to the elders of Judah and to places where he and his men had roamed. The victory’s benefits reach beyond the raiding force into the wider community that has shared his years of wandering.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill emphasize that the men at Besor had a real place in the work: they remained with the supplies. Their weakness prevented them from continuing; it did not make them enemies or erase their belonging.

Henry and Gill connect generosity with recognizing God as the giver. David’s answer restrains the impulse to claim full credit for the victory and then use that claim to exclude others.

Wesley reads the gifts to Judah as both gratitude for earlier help and a wise way of building goodwill. Those purposes can stand together. The future king uses what he has received to strengthen relationships beyond his immediate company.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

David’s decision gives a picture of a community receiving and sharing what God has supplied. People doing different work remain part of one company, and the most visible contribution is not the whole story.

Romans 12:3–8 develops that theme for the church. Many members form one body in Christ, with differing gifts received by grace. Service, teaching, encouragement, giving, leadership and mercy all have their place.

This is a parallel about belonging and gifts, rather than a command to copy an ancient division of plunder in every setting. It directs attention away from using success to rank people’s worth and toward using God’s gifts for the good of the whole body.
