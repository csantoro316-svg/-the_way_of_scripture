Passage

Hebrews 9:23-28

Why will Christ return without offering Himself again?

Berean Standard Bible

23 So it was necessary for the copies of the heavenly things to be purified with these sacrifices, but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these. 24 For Christ did not enter a man-made copy of the true sanctuary, but He entered heaven itself, now to appear on our behalf in the presence of God.

25 Nor did He enter heaven to offer Himself again and again, as the high priest enters the Most Holy Place every year with blood that is not his own. 26 Otherwise, Christ would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world. But now He has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to do away with sin by the sacrifice of Himself.

27 Just as man is appointed to die once, and after that to face judgment, 28 so also Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many; and He will appear a second time, not to bear sin, but to bring salvation to those who eagerly await Him.

What is the context?

The writer carries the comparison from the earthly copies to the heavenly reality. Christ has entered heaven itself to appear before God on our behalf. The greater cleansing rests on His own offering.

His entrance does not begin an endless repetition of His suffering. He appeared once to deal with sin through the sacrifice of Himself. The contrast is with the high priest’s yearly entrance carrying another creature’s blood.

The comparison then joins our one earthly life and coming judgment with Christ’s one offering and coming return. He will appear again to bring salvation to those who eagerly await Him, rather than to bear sin through another sacrifice. This does not imply He was personally sinful at His first coming.

The passage states that the heavenly things require the greater cleansing, but it does not describe a hidden incident that polluted heaven or give the movements of blood through an unseen building. Its clear emphasis is Christ’s sufficient offering and His presence before God for us.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin, Wesley and the USCCB notes agree that Christ’s offering is once for all and that His return brings its saving outcome rather than another death.

They explain the heavenly things in verse 23 differently. Calvin speaks of Christ’s spiritual kingdom. Wesley identifies them with believers’ heaven-born spirits and acknowledges that there may be more here than we yet understand. The USCCB notes emphasize the heavenly realities to which the earlier rites pointed. These readings do not all require an account of a sinful act inside a heavenly temple.

Christians also differ about judgment after death. The Catholic Catechism, 1021–1022, teaches a personal judgment at death. Wesley’s comment on verse 27 instead refers judgment to the final day and denies a separate immediate judgment, while saying that a person’s final state is determined at death. His view is an individual commentator’s reading; the Catechism states official Catholic teaching.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Isaiah 53:10–12 describes the servant offering His life and bearing the sins of many. Acts 8:30–35 explicitly connects Isaiah’s servant passage with Jesus. Hebrews’ language about bearing the sins of many belongs within that larger scriptural account of His mission.

1 Corinthians 15:20–26 connects Christ’s return with the resurrection of those who belong to Him and the final defeat of death. Waiting for Him is therefore hope for the completion of His saving work, rather than waiting for a sacrifice that still needs to be made.
