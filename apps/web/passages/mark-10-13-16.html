Passage

Mark 10:13-16

Receiving the Kingdom Like a Child

Berean Standard Bible

13 Now people were bringing the little children to Jesus for Him to place His hands on them, and the disciples rebuked those who brought them. 14 But when Jesus saw this, He was indignant and told them, “Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them! For the kingdom of God belongs to such as these. 15 Truly I tell you, anyone who does not receive the kingdom of God like a little child will never enter it.” 16 And He took the children in His arms, placed His hands on them, and blessed them.

What is the context?

People bring little children to Jesus for Him to lay His hands on them. The disciples rebuke those who bring them, but Jesus is indignant and tells them to let the children come. He then takes the children in His arms, lays His hands on them, and blesses them.

His indignation is more than mild disappointment. The Greek verb ἀγανακτέω (aganakteo, ah-gah-nak-TEH-oh) describes strong displeasure. The disciples are hindering the very people He wants to receive. This repeats a lesson from Mark 9:33–37, where He welcomed a child after their dispute about greatness.

Jesus speaks both about the children before Him and about anyone who would enter God's kingdom. The kingdom must be received like a little child. The focus is receiving what God gives rather than claiming a place through importance or achievement; the next account, about a wealthy man seeking eternal life, brings that contrast into sharper view.

Luke 18:15–17 says people were bringing even babies. Neither account identifies all those bringing them or gives their exact ages. Mark describes a blessing, not a baptism, and does not describe each child's inner response. The welcome is concrete: Jesus receives the children the disciples were turning away.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see both a welcome for actual children and a call to receive God's kingdom with humble dependence. Wesley on Mark 10:14–15 emphasizes receiving without a claim to personal worthiness; the USCCB note on verse 15 likewise stresses dependence and obedience to the gospel.

Traditions differ over the connection with infant baptism. Calvin's discussion of this scene sees Christ's welcome as support for including infants in baptism. The Catholic Catechism 1250–1252 teaches infant baptism as a gift of new life. The Reformed Westminster Confession 28.4 includes infants of believing parents, and the Methodist Articles XVII also retain baptism of young children. By contrast, the 1689 Baptist Confession 29.2 limits baptism to those who personally profess repentance and faith. These documents explain different practices; the scene itself narrates Jesus' welcome and blessing.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The welcome fits a pattern that becomes clear in the church's life. 1 Corinthians 1:26–31 says God calls people without giving human status, power, or impressive ancestry the final say. He chooses the weak and lowly so that no one can boast before Him; Christ becomes their righteousness, holiness, and redemption. Receiving the kingdom like a child belongs within that larger story of receiving what God provides.

That purpose also shapes how His people treat others. James 1:27 places care for orphans and widows within faithful life before God. The connection does not make every child in this scene an orphan. It carries the lesson forward: a community gathered by God's grace is to make room for people who can offer it little status or advantage. Jesus' embrace teaches the disciples what that welcome looks like.
