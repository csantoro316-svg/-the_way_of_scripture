Passage

Luke 24:13-35

How does Jesus help the travelers to Emmaus understand?

Berean Standard Bible

13 That same day two of them were going to a village called Emmaus, about seven miles from Jerusalem. 14 They were talking with each other about everything that had happened. 15 And as they talked and deliberated, Jesus Himself came up and walked along with them. 16 But their eyes were kept from recognizing Him. 17 He asked them, “What are you discussing so intently as you walk along?” They stood still, with sadness on their faces. 18 One of them, named Cleopas, asked Him, “Are You the only visitor to Jerusalem who does not know the things that have happened there in recent days?”

19 “What things?” He asked. “The events involving Jesus of Nazareth,” they answered. “This man was a prophet, powerful in speech and action before God and all the people. 20 Our chief priests and rulers delivered Him up to the sentence of death, and they crucified Him. 21 But we were hoping He was the One who would redeem Israel. And besides all this, it is the third day since these things took place. 22 Furthermore, some of our women astounded us. They were at the tomb early this morning, 23 but they did not find His body. They came and told us they had seen a vision of angels, who said that Jesus was alive. 24 Then some of our companions went to the tomb and found it just as the women had described. But Him they did not see.”

25 Then Jesus said to them, “O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken! 26 Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and then to enter His glory?” 27 And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them what was written in all the Scriptures about Himself.

28 As they approached the village where they were headed, He seemed to be going farther. 29 But they pleaded with Him, “Stay with us, for it is nearly evening and the day is almost over.” So He went in to stay with them. 30 While He was reclining at the table with them, He took bread, spoke a blessing and broke it, and gave it to them. 31 Then their eyes were opened and they recognized Jesus—and He disappeared from their sight. 32 They asked each other, “Were not our hearts burning within us as He spoke with us on the road and opened the Scriptures to us?”

33 And they got up that very hour and returned to Jerusalem. There they found the Eleven and those with them, gathered together 34 and saying, “The Lord has indeed risen and has appeared to Simon!” 35 Then the two told what had happened on the road, and how they had recognized Jesus in the breaking of the bread.

What is the context?

Two followers leave Jerusalem for Emmaus that same day, discussing what has happened. Jesus joins them, but they are kept from recognizing Him. One is named Cleopas; Luke does not identify the other.

They describe Jesus as a prophet powerful in word and deed and explain their disappointed hope that He would redeem Israel. The Greek verb is λυτρόω, lytroō (loo-TROH-oh), meaning to redeem or deliver, with a background of release by ransom. Thayer includes this verse among its uses for deliverance. The word carries the hope of rescue, while Jesus’ explanation will show how His suffering belongs within that hope. (Thayer, Greek 3084)

They know about the women’s report and the empty tomb, but have not seen Jesus. He challenges their slowness to believe the prophets and explains that the Christ had to suffer before entering His glory. Beginning with Moses and the Prophets, He explains what Scripture says about Him.

Near the village they urge Him to stay. At the table He takes bread, blesses it, breaks it and gives it to them. Their eyes open, they recognize Him and He disappears from their sight. They then recall how their hearts burned as He opened the Scriptures on the road.

They return to Jerusalem that very hour. The gathered believers tell them that the Lord has risen and appeared to Simon; the two add their own account of the road and the breaking of bread. Scripture, recognition and shared witness come together in the scene.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readers commonly understand the encounter as Jesus helping His followers see that His suffering belongs within God’s promised work, rather than proving that their hope has failed. Wesley emphasizes that the sufferings which troubled them were part of the promised Messiah’s work. (Wesley on Luke 24:25–27)

Readers differ over the meal’s relation to the Lord’s Supper. The Catholic Catechism uses the movement from Scripture explained on the road to bread shared at the table to explain the unity of the Eucharistic celebration. Wesley explicitly identifies the breaking of bread here with the Lord’s Supper. (Catechism §§1346–1347; Wesley on Luke 24:30–35)

Calvin instead understands it as an ordinary meal at which Jesus gives thanks in His familiar manner. His explanation differs from the meal being the Lord’s Supper. This is his commentary’s reading, rather than a claim that every Reformed Christian interprets the scene identically. (Calvin on Luke 24:30)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus Himself makes the central connection: suffering and glory belong together in the work of the Christ promised in Scripture. Peter later describes the prophets searching into the sufferings of Christ and the glories to follow. Isaiah’s Servant bearing sin and the promise applied to Jesus in Acts that He would not see decay are examples of this scriptural pattern. Luke does not list every passage Jesus used on the road. (1 Peter 1:10–12; Isaiah 53:4–12; Acts 13:32–39)

Their hope for Israel’s redemption also has deep roots. Psalm 130 calls Israel to hope in the Lord who redeems from iniquity. At the chapter’s close, Jesus explains that His suffering and resurrection lead into repentance and forgiveness being proclaimed in His name to all nations, beginning in Jerusalem. The story carries their disappointed hope toward the redemption He is actually accomplishing. (Psalm 130:6–8; Luke 24:44–49)
