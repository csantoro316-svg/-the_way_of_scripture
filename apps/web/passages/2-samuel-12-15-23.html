Passage

2 Samuel 12:15-23

Why does David plead for the child and then rise to worship?

Berean Standard Bible

15 After Nathan had gone home, the LORD struck the child that Uriah’s wife had borne to David, and he became ill. 16 David pleaded with God for the boy. He fasted and went into his house and spent the night lying in sackcloth on the ground. 17 The elders of his household stood beside him to help him up from the ground, but he was unwilling and would not eat anything with them. 18 On the seventh day the child died. But David’s servants were afraid to tell him that the child was dead, for they said, “Look, while the child was alive, we spoke to him, and he would not listen to us. So how can we tell him the child is dead? He may even harm himself.”

19 When David saw that his servants were whispering to one another, he perceived that the child was dead. So he asked his servants, “Is the child dead?” “He is dead,” they replied. 20 Then David got up from the ground, washed and anointed himself, changed his clothes, and went into the house of the LORD and worshiped. Then he went to his own house, and at his request they set food before him, and he ate. 21 “What is this you have done?” his servants asked. “While the child was alive, you fasted and wept, but when he died, you got up and ate.” 22 David answered, “While the child was alive, I fasted and wept, for I said, ‘Who knows? The LORD may be gracious to me and let him live.’ 23 But now that he is dead, why should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I will go to him, but he will not return to me.”

What is the context?

After Nathan leaves, the LORD strikes the child with illness. David pleads with God, fasts and lies on the ground through the night. His household cannot persuade him to get up and eat. On the seventh day the child dies.

The servants fear telling David, but he notices their whispering and asks directly. Once they confirm the death, he washes, changes his clothes, worships and eats. They are puzzled because he had fasted and wept while the child lived.

David explains that while the child was alive he hoped the LORD might show mercy and spare him. Now he cannot bring him back. David will go to the child, but the child will not return to him. His answer explains why he ends this particular fast; it need not mean that his love or grief has ended.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The Hebrew word for child is yeled (YEH-led). Brown–Driver–Briggs shows that it can describe children across a range of ages, including the young Moses in Exodus 2. It does not prove that David’s son was an older boy or establish a delay of several years before Nathan came.

Wesley and Gill understand David’s words about going to the child as including hope of reunion with him in heaven. The verse itself does not name heaven or set out a complete account of the destiny of every child. Their hopeful reading should be distinguished from what the sentence states explicitly.

The narrator directly connects this illness and death with the LORD’s announcement to David. That makes this a difficult account of a specific judgment; it does not give readers a rule that a child’s illness reveals a parent’s particular sin, or a timetable that other grieving parents must follow.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 places Christian hope in Jesus’ death and resurrection and in the future resurrection of those who belong to Him. Paul speaks of grief with hope and calls believers to encourage one another.

This supplies a clear New Testament foundation for hope beyond death without turning David’s brief answer into a complete explanation of every question about a child’s future. The passage about David does not state the child’s personal response to God.

The connection is the larger theme of death, grief and hope in God. It leaves room to face the loss honestly. Confidence in resurrection does not require pretending that death is painless or judging someone whose mourning takes a different course.
