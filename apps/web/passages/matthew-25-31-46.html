Passage

Matthew 25:31-46

The Sheep and the Goats: How Have We Treated the Least?

Berean Standard Bible

31 When the Son of Man comes in His glory, and all the angels with Him, He will sit on His glorious throne. 32 All the nations will be gathered before Him, and He will separate the people one from another, as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats. 33 He will place the sheep on His right and the goats on His left. 34 Then the King will say to those on His right, ‘Come, you who are blessed by My Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. 35 For I was hungry and you gave Me something to eat, I was thirsty and you gave Me something to drink, I was a stranger and you took Me in, 36 I was naked and you clothed Me, I was sick and you looked after Me, I was in prison and you visited Me.’

37 Then the righteous will answer Him, ‘Lord, when did we see You hungry and feed You, or thirsty and give You something to drink? 38 When did we see You a stranger and take You in, or naked and clothe You? 39 When did we see You sick or in prison and visit You?’ 40 And the King will reply, ‘Truly I tell you, whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of Mine, you did for Me.’

41 Then He will say to those on His left, ‘Depart from Me, you who are cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels. 42 For I was hungry and you gave Me nothing to eat, I was thirsty and you gave Me nothing to drink, 43 I was a stranger and you did not take Me in, I was naked and you did not clothe Me, I was sick and in prison and you did not look after Me.’ 44 And they too will reply, ‘Lord, when did we see You hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or sick or in prison, and did not minister to You?’ 45 Then the King will answer, ‘Truly I tell you, whatever you did not do for one of the least of these, you did not do for Me.’ 46 And they will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life.”

What is the context?

Jesus ends this teaching with the Son of Man coming in glory, accompanied by angels and seated on His throne. All nations gather before Him. He separates people as a shepherd separates sheep from goats, placing the sheep on His right and the goats on His left.

The King welcomes those on His right to inherit the kingdom prepared for them from the foundation of the world. He recounts their care for Him when hungry, thirsty, a stranger, unclothed, sick, and imprisoned. They are surprised: when did they see Him in those conditions? His answer identifies what they did for the least of His brothers as service to Him.

Those on His left are likewise surprised, but their neglect of those in need was neglect of Him. He sends them away into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels. The passage ends with eternal punishment and eternal life. Jesus presents both His authority to judge and the weight of everyday actions toward people who could easily be overlooked.

The point is more searching than whether someone recognized the King in person. Both groups ask when they saw Him. His judgment exposes the significance of the care they gave or withheld, and the next chapter moves from His teaching about judgment to the events leading to His own death.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Readers differ about who the least of His brothers are and how this judgment fits with other passages about Christ's return.

The Catholic Catechism, 1033, applies the passage as a warning against neglecting the serious needs of the poor and the little ones who are Christ's brothers. The US Catholic bishops' note on 25:31–46 acknowledges different readings and favors a focus on suffering Christians, probably missionaries, in Matthew's use of the passage. It connects receiving them with receiving Jesus in Matthew 10:40. This illustrates a difference of explanation within Catholic sources rather than a single reading held by every Catholic.

Wesley on 25:34–40 emphasizes faith working through love, care for fellow believers, and doing good to everyone. Calvin on 25:34–40 understands the promised inheritance as God's gracious gift and the deeds of mercy as the life that follows His grace. The Westminster Confession, chapters 16 and 33, similarly holds together good works as the fruit of faith and a judgment of all people (text).

Walvoord on 25:31–46 instead places this judgment of living Gentiles before Christ's thousand-year reign and identifies the brothers as Jewish people helped during the preceding time of persecution. He takes that costly care as evidence of faith, rather than as earning salvation. This differs from the final judgment of all people described by Wesley, Calvin, and the bishops' notes. The passage's demand to take mercy seriously remains central across these readings.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The shepherd who judges also belongs to Scripture's promise of rescue for the vulnerable. In Ezekiel 34:11–24, God promises to seek His scattered flock, strengthen the weak, judge between members of the flock, and establish one shepherd from David's line. This is a supported parallel: the King's judgment in Matthew concerns the treatment of people who need protection and care.

The invitation to inherit the kingdom also reaches beyond a reward for a single kind action. Romans 8:14–23 describes God's children as heirs with Christ, sharing His sufferings and awaiting glory and the freeing of creation from decay. Ephesians 1:3–14 places adoption, redemption, and the promised inheritance within God's purpose to bring all things together in Christ. The kingdom prepared by the Father belongs to that larger hope.

James 2:1–17 brings this hope into the life of a congregation: the poor can be heirs of the promised kingdom, favoritism dishonors them, and words of concern do not feed or clothe a person in need. God's future purpose gives present care lasting significance. Service to Christ can take place in the needs immediately before us, even when we do not recognize how He values it.
