Passage

Isaiah 9:13-17

Why are the leaders and the people both held responsible?

Berean Standard Bible

13 But the people did not return to Him who struck them; they did not seek the LORD of Hosts. 14 So the LORD will cut off Israel’s head and tail, both palm branch and reed in a single day. 15 The head is the elder and honorable man, and the tail is the prophet who teaches lies.

16 For those who guide this people mislead them, and those they mislead are swallowed up. 17 Therefore the Lord takes no pleasure in their young men; He has no compassion on their fatherless and widows. For every one of them is godless and wicked, and every mouth speaks folly. Despite all this, His anger is not turned away; His hand is still upraised.

What is the context?

The people do not return to the One who struck them or seek the Lord. The warning therefore reaches the whole community, pictured as head and tail, palm branch and reed. Verse 15 explains the head as the elder and honored man, and the tail as the prophet who teaches lies.

Their guides lead them astray, and those who follow are swallowed up. Misleading leadership is a serious part of the failure, but the passage also describes corruption spreading through the people themselves.

The warning becomes especially severe when it names young men, fatherless children and widows. Even the groups normally associated with strength or particular need do not escape this judgment. The stated reason is the community’s pervasive godlessness, wrongdoing and corrupt speech. The refrain again announces that the Lord’s anger has not turned away.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley emphasize the harm done by leaders who make people comfortable in wrongdoing. They read the loss of both high and low as a warning that rank will provide no shelter when the nation is judged.

Wesley understands the sweeping description of wickedness as a statement about the body of the people, rather than a claim that God has no faithful person left anywhere. He also notes that fatherless children and widows are ordinarily special objects of God’s care. Their inclusion makes this particular warning more severe.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The failure of these guides belongs to Scripture’s wider concern with people being lost under harmful leadership. John 10:1–18 presents a sharply different shepherd: Jesus knows His sheep, leads them, and lays down His life for them instead of abandoning them to danger.

This is a contrast between the passages, not a claim that John directly quotes Isaiah 9. It shows how God’s answer goes beyond exposing failed guides: the Son gathers His people and gives His own life for their salvation.
