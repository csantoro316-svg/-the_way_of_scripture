Passage

2 Samuel 4:5-12

Can murder become good news because it benefits David?

Berean Standard Bible

5 Now Rechab and Baanah, the sons of Rimmon the Beerothite, set out and arrived at the house of Ish-bosheth in the heat of the day, while the king was taking his midday nap. 6 They entered the interior of the house as if to get some wheat, and they stabbed him in the stomach. Then Rechab and his brother Baanah slipped away. 7 They had entered the house while Ish-bosheth was lying on his bed, and having stabbed and killed him, they beheaded him, took his head, and traveled all night by way of the Arabah. 8 They brought the head of Ish-bosheth to David at Hebron and said to the king, “Here is the head of Ish-bosheth son of Saul, your enemy who sought your life. Today the LORD has granted vengeance to my lord the king against Saul and his offspring.”

9 But David answered Rechab and his brother Baanah, the sons of Rimmon the Beerothite, “As surely as the LORD lives, who has redeemed my life from all distress, 10 when someone told me, ‘Look, Saul is dead,’ and thought he was a bearer of good news, I seized him and put him to death at Ziklag. That was his reward for his news! 11 How much more, when wicked men kill a righteous man in his own house and on his own bed, shall I not now require his blood from your hands and remove you from the earth!” 12 So David commanded his young men, and they killed Rechab and Baanah. They cut off their hands and feet and hung their bodies by the pool in Hebron, but they took the head of Ish-bosheth and buried it in Abner’s tomb in Hebron.

What is the context?

Rechab and Baanah enter Ish-bosheth’s house while he is resting at midday. Pretending to get wheat, they stab and kill him, cut off his head, and carry it through the night to David in Hebron.

They present the killing as the LORD’s vengeance on Saul’s family for David’s benefit. David refuses that explanation. He recalls what happened to the messenger who expected a reward for news of Saul’s death, then condemns these men for murdering a righteous man in his own house and bed.

David orders their execution. Their hands and feet are cut off, and their bodies are hung by the pool in Hebron. Ish-bosheth’s head is buried in Abner’s tomb. The violent end of the scene should be read as it is recorded, without turning every detail of an ancient royal punishment into an instruction for readers to imitate.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry understands David’s response as a refusal to gain the kingdom through unlawful help. Trust that God would complete His work did not make another person’s murder acceptable. The captains’ claim that the LORD had acted is their explanation of their crime, not the narrator’s endorsement.

Gill explains David’s description of Ish-bosheth as righteous in relation to this murder: he was killed at home, asleep and defenseless, rather than while fighting these men. David is identifying an innocent victim of their attack, not declaring that Ish-bosheth had never sinned.

Wesley extends the warning to people who use cruelty while claiming to serve Christ. He regards harm done under religious cover as a betrayal of that service. This is his application of the passage; it is not a claim that all churches have issued a distinct official teaching about these captains.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The captains confuse an outcome favorable to David with approval from God. David’s answer separates the promised kingdom from their chosen means of advancing it. A person cannot make evil faithful merely by attaching God’s name to the result.

In Romans 3:5–8, Paul rejects the suggestion that human wrongdoing becomes acceptable because God’s truth or righteousness is displayed through it. God’s ability to accomplish good does not remove our responsibility for evil.

This is a theme shared across Scripture, not a claim that Paul is directly commenting on Ish-bosheth’s death. It helps us follow God’s purpose through a troubled history while continuing to call betrayal and murder what they are.
