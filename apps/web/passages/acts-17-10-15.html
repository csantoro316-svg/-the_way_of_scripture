Passage

Acts 17:10-15

How do the Bereans receive the message and test it?

Berean Standard Bible

10 As soon as night had fallen, the brothers sent Paul and Silas away to Berea. On arriving there, they went into the Jewish synagogue. 11 Now the Bereans were more noble-minded than the Thessalonians, for they received the message with great eagerness and examined the Scriptures every day to see if these teachings were true. 12 As a result, many of them believed, along with quite a few prominent Greek women and men.

13 But when the Jews from Thessalonica learned that Paul was also proclaiming the word of God in Berea, they went there themselves to incite and agitate the crowds. 14 The brothers immediately sent Paul to the coast, but Silas and Timothy remained in Berea. 15 Those who escorted Paul brought him to Athens and then returned with instructions for Silas and Timothy to join him as soon as possible.

What is the context?

The believers send Paul and Silas to Berea at night, where they again enter a Jewish synagogue. Luke commends the hearers’ eagerness to receive the message and their daily examination of Scripture to see whether the teaching is true. Many believe, including prominent Greek women and men.

The description noble-minded comes from εὐγενής (eugenēs, yoo-geh-NAYS). Thayer’s entry distinguishes noble birth from the noble-minded sense used here. Luke explains the commendation through the hearers’ response to the message and their examination of Scripture.

Opponents from Thessalonica come to stir up the crowds when they learn that Paul is teaching in Berea. The believers send him toward the coast, while Silas and Timothy remain. His escorts take him to Athens and return with instructions for the others to join him soon.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly commend the combination of eager hearing and careful examination in this account. Traditions differ over how Scripture, the church’s teaching and authoritative interpretation relate when readers apply that example.

The Catholic Church’s Catechism, paragraphs 85–87, entrusts authoritative interpretation to the bishops in communion with the pope. It expressly describes that teaching office as serving God’s word, not standing above it.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, chapter 1, paragraph 10, identifies the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture as the final judge by which councils, teachers and religious claims are examined. The Methodist Articles of Religion, Article V, teach that nothing absent from Scripture or unable to be proved from it may be required as necessary to salvation.

Wesley on verse 11 stresses that being teachable and carefully searching for truth belong together. The Bereans’ examination is not portrayed as a refusal ever to believe; Luke connects it with the belief that follows.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

In Luke 24:25–27, the risen Jesus explains from Moses and the prophets why the Messiah must suffer and enter His glory. Paul’s message and the Bereans’ examination belong within that connection between Jesus and the Scriptures that point to Him.

The result is more than winning a debate. People come to faith, and a community remains to be cared for after Paul is sent away. Careful attention to what God has said serves the receiving of the good news and the continuing life of His people.

What is our position?

The Berean Standard directly develops this passage as the site’s method of hearing and testing teaching. It calls for eagerness to learn, daily attention to the whole passage and a willingness to let Scripture correct what we already believe. That includes testing the teaching on this site: confidence in a teacher is a reason to listen carefully, while the text remains the measure of the claim.
