Passage

Isaiah 63:1-6

Who is the mighty figure whose garments are stained red?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Who is this coming from Edom, from Bozrah with crimson-stained garments? Who is this robed in splendor, marching in the greatness of His strength? “It is I, proclaiming vindication, mighty to save.” 2 Why are Your clothes red, and Your garments like one who treads the winepress? 3 “I have trodden the winepress alone, and no one from the nations was with Me. I trampled them in My anger and trod them down in My fury; their blood spattered My garments, and all My clothes were stained.

4 For the day of vengeance was in My heart, and the year of My redemption had come. 5 I looked, but there was no one to help; I was appalled that no one assisted. So My arm brought Me salvation, and My own wrath upheld Me. 6 I trampled the nations in My anger; in My wrath I made them drunk and poured out their blood on the ground.”

What is the context?

A question introduces a majestic figure coming from Edom and Bozrah in crimson-stained clothing. He identifies Himself as the One who proclaims vindication and is mighty to save. A second question asks why His garments look like those of someone treading a winepress.

The answer turns the winepress into an image of judgment. He has trodden it alone, and the stain is the blood of the nations He has judged. No one from those nations stood with Him.

The day of vengeance and year of redemption have arrived together. When no one supplied help, His own arm brought salvation. The passage connects rescue with His decisive action against those who oppose Him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The USCCB notes identify the approaching figure as the Lord and the winepress as an image of His judgment. Henry and Wesley read the victorious figure through the Messiah, with Edom representing opposition to His people.

Both Henry and Wesley emphasize that the victory comes through His own power. Henry distinguishes the blood He shed in His saving sacrifice from this picture of judgment upon His enemies. The stain here belongs to the explanation supplied by the passage itself.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Revelation 19:11–16 takes up the blood-stained robe and the winepress of God’s wrath. The rider judges in righteousness, bears the name Word of God, and is identified as King of kings and Lord of lords.

This gives Isaiah’s victory picture a clear connection with the New Testament’s presentation of Christ as judge. The One whose saving work brings redemption also confronts evil with authority. The action belongs to Him; the passage does not commission readers to take vengeance into their own hands.
