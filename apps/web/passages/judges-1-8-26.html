Passage

Judges 1:8-26

How do victories and unfinished work stand side by side?

Berean Standard Bible

8 Then the men of Judah fought against Jerusalem and captured it. They put the city to the sword and set it on fire. 9 Afterward, the men of Judah marched down to fight against the Canaanites living in the hill country, in the Negev, and in the foothills. 10 Judah also marched against the Canaanites who were living in Hebron (formerly known as Kiriath-arba), and they struck down Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai. 11 From there they marched against the inhabitants of Debir (formerly known as Kiriath-sepher). 12 And Caleb said, “To the man who strikes down Kiriath-sepher and captures it, I will give my daughter Acsah in marriage.” 13 So Othniel son of Caleb’s younger brother Kenaz captured the city, and Caleb gave his daughter Acsah to him in marriage.

14 One day Acsah came to Othniel and urged him to ask her father for a field. When she got off her donkey, Caleb asked her, “What do you desire?” 15 “Give me a blessing,” she answered. “Since you have given me land in the Negev, give me springs of water as well.” So Caleb gave her both the upper and lower springs. 16 Now the descendants of Moses’ father-in-law, the Kenite, went up with the men of Judah from the City of Palms to the Wilderness of Judah in the Negev near Arad. They went to live among the people. 17 Then the men of Judah went with their brothers the Simeonites, attacked the Canaanites living in Zephath, and devoted the city to destruction. So it was called Hormah.

18 And Judah also captured Gaza, Ashkelon, and Ekron—each with its territory. 19 The LORD was with Judah, and they took possession of the hill country; but they could not drive out the inhabitants of the plains because they had chariots of iron. 20 Just as Moses had promised, Judah gave Hebron to Caleb, who drove out the descendants of the three sons of Anak. 21 The Benjamites, however, failed to drive out the Jebusites living in Jerusalem. So to this day the Jebusites live there among the Benjamites.

22 The house of Joseph also attacked Bethel, and the LORD was with them. 23 They sent spies to Bethel (formerly known as Luz), 24 and when the spies saw a man coming out of the city, they said to him, “Please show us how to get into the city, and we will treat you kindly.” 25 So the man showed them the entrance to the city, and they put the city to the sword but released that man and all his family. 26 And the man went to the land of the Hittites, built a city, and called it Luz, which is its name to this day.

What is the context?

The chapter follows Judah’s campaigns through Jerusalem, Hebron, and Debir, then records successes and failures among other tribes. Some events also appear in Joshua 15:13–19. The opening chapters of Judges gather the background to Israel’s life in the land; they are more than a simple day-by-day list of new battles.

Othniel captures Debir and marries Caleb’s daughter Acsah. She asks her father for springs to accompany the land she has received, and he gives both the upper and lower springs. Her request draws attention to what is needed to live in the inheritance, not only to claim it.

Judah helps Simeon as promised, but the account also says Judah cannot drive out the people of the plains with their iron chariots. The attack on Jerusalem in verse 8 is followed by Benjamin’s failure to remove the Jebusites in verse 21. An attack and permanent possession of a place are not the same event, and the chapter does not provide every stage of Jerusalem’s history.

The house of Joseph takes Bethel after promising kindness to a man who shows the way into the city. His family is spared, and he leaves to build another city called Luz. The LORD is said to be with the house of Joseph as well as Judah. The narrative’s picture is mixed; Judah is not presented as accomplishing every task while every other tribe fails.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley explains Jerusalem as a partial capture, with Jebusites continuing to hold a stronghold. Study notes published by the U.S. bishops point to the differing conquest notices and David’s later capture without supplying that exact reconstruction.

For the iron chariots, Wesley and Henry understand Judah’s failure in terms of fear and unbelief. They hold that the God who gave the earlier victories could also have overcome these forces; the military obstacle does not establish a limit to His power.

Gill compares the kindness promised to the man at Bethel with the sparing of Rahab’s family. The passage says that the man helped the spies and then left for another land; it does not make his faith the focus or explicitly condemn this particular agreement. That leaves room to distinguish the episode from the repeated failures that follow.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Acsah’s request provides a helpful illustration within the larger theme of receiving good things from a father. She asks for what is needed with the land, and Caleb responds generously. Jesus uses ordinary fathers’ care to teach His disciples about their heavenly Father’s goodness in Matthew 7:7–11.

Luke 11:9–13 carries that teaching toward the gift of the Holy Spirit. The connection illustrates the fatherly care Jesus teaches His people to trust. God’s purposes include a people who can bring their needs to Him with trust in His goodness.
