Passage

1 Corinthians 1:18-25

How can the cross reveal God’s wisdom and power?

Berean Standard Bible

18 For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God. 19 For it is written: “I will destroy the wisdom of the wise; the intelligence of the intelligent I will frustrate.” 20 Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe? Where is the philosopher of this age? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world? 21 For since in the wisdom of God the world through its wisdom did not know Him, God was pleased through the foolishness of what was preached to save those who believe.

22 Jews demand signs and Greeks search for wisdom, 23 but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles, 24 but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God. 25 For the foolishness of God is wiser than man’s wisdom, and the weakness of God is stronger than man’s strength.

What is the context?

Paul turns from impressive messengers to the message itself. The cross is dismissed as foolishness by those who are perishing, but believers encounter God’s saving power in it. The point is not that the gospel is actually foolish. God’s way of saving overturns the standards by which people expect to recognize wisdom and strength.

He quotes Isaiah 29:14, where God frustrates human wisdom. People have not come to know Him through the world’s wisdom, yet He saves those who believe through the message being preached.

Paul describes demands for signs and searches for wisdom, then announces Christ crucified. The crucified Messiah confronts both expectations, yet Christ is God’s wisdom and power for those called from among Jews and Greeks. Paul is describing responses to the message, not claiming that every Jewish or Gentile person reacts in the same way; verse 24 explicitly includes believers from both.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians share the central reading that salvation comes through the crucified Christ rather than human brilliance or strength. Where traditions differ is how God’s call brings a person to faith.

The Reformed Westminster Confession 10.1–2 teaches that God renews those He has chosen so that they willingly come to Christ. In a Wesleyan reading, God’s grace first enables a response: the Methodist Articles, Article VIII, deny that people can turn to Him by their own unaided strength. Wesley’s note on verse 24 describes the called as those who obey the heavenly call. Neither reading makes intelligence or social standing the reason someone is saved; they differ over how grace and the human response relate.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

After His resurrection, Jesus explains in Luke 24:25–27, 44–47 that the Messiah’s suffering and resurrection belong to the fulfillment of Scripture. Repentance for the forgiveness of sins is then to be proclaimed in His name to all nations.

The cross, which can look like the collapse of hope, belongs to the way God fulfills His saving purpose. Paul’s preaching of the crucified Christ to Jews and Gentiles fits that worldwide mission. The shame of the death does not cancel the purpose Jesus explains.
