Passage

Jude 1:3-16

How can grace be turned into an excuse for corruption?

Berean Standard Bible

3 Beloved, although I made every effort to write to you about the salvation we share, I felt it necessary to write and urge you to contend earnestly for the faith entrusted once for all to the saints. 4 For certain men have crept in among you unnoticed—ungodly ones who were designated long ago for condemnation. They turn the grace of our God into a license for immorality, and they deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ.

5 Although you are fully aware of this, I want to remind you that after Jesus had delivered His people out of the land of Egypt, He destroyed those who did not believe. 6 And the angels who did not stay within their own domain but abandoned their proper dwelling—these He has kept in eternal chains under darkness, bound for judgment on that great day. 7 In like manner, Sodom and Gomorrah and the cities around them, who indulged in sexual immorality and pursued strange flesh, are on display as an example of those who sustain the punishment of eternal fire.

8 Yet in the same way these dreamers defile their bodies, reject authority, and slander glorious beings. 9 But even the archangel Michael, when he disputed with the devil over the body of Moses, did not presume to bring a slanderous charge against him, but said, “The Lord rebuke you!” 10 These men, however, slander what they do not understand, and like irrational animals, they will be destroyed by the things they do instinctively.

11 Woe to them! They have traveled the path of Cain; they have rushed for profit into the error of Balaam; they have perished in Korah’s rebellion. 12 These men are hidden reefs in your love feasts, shamelessly feasting with you but shepherding only themselves. They are clouds without water, carried along by the wind; fruitless trees in autumn, twice dead after being uprooted. 13 They are wild waves of the sea, foaming up their own shame; wandering stars, for whom blackest darkness has been reserved forever.

14 Enoch, the seventh from Adam, also prophesied about them: “Behold, the Lord is coming with myriads of His holy ones 15 to execute judgment on everyone, and to convict all the ungodly of every ungodly act of wickedness and every harsh word spoken against Him by ungodly sinners.” 16 These men are discontented grumblers, following after their own lusts; their mouths spew arrogance; they flatter others for their own advantage.

What is the context?

Jude explains why he is writing urgently. People have entered the community unnoticed, use God’s grace as a license for immorality and deny Jesus Christ as Master and Lord. He calls the readers to contend for the faith entrusted to the saints. The word translated contend describes striving for something; here the concern is faithfulness to the received faith.

Three examples show the seriousness of rebellion: the unbelieving people after the deliverance from Egypt, angels who abandoned their proper place, and Sodom and Gomorrah. The BSB names Jesus as the one who delivered His people from Egypt in verse 5. Jude’s point is that earlier privilege gives no protection to persistent unbelief and rebellion.

The present offenders likewise defile their bodies, reject authority and slander what they do not understand. Michael provides the contrast. In the dispute over Moses’ body, he appeals to the Lord’s rebuke rather than making a slanderous charge. Jude does not explain the reason for that dispute or describe what the devil planned to do with the body. Deuteronomy 34:1–12 records Moses’ death and burial without giving those details.

Cain, Balaam and Korah provide further examples. Genesis 4:1–16 describes Cain’s anger and murder of Abel. Numbers 31:16 identifies Balaam’s counsel that led Israel into unfaithfulness; Jude emphasizes his pursuit of profit. Numbers 16 describes Korah and his companions rebelling against the authority God had given Moses and Aaron.

The pictures from nature expose danger and empty promises: hidden reefs, clouds without water, fruitless trees, shameful waves and wandering stars. The teachers share the community’s meals while caring for themselves. Belonging visibly to a gathering gives no assurance that a person is nourishing it.

Enoch’s prophecy announces judgment on ungodly deeds and words. Its wording also appears in 1 Enoch 1:9. Jude applies it to people whose grumbling, arrogance and flattery serve their own desires. The warning addresses both what they teach and how they live.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The received faith is central to the passage, though traditions differ over how it is preserved and interpreted. The Catholic Church teaches that the apostolic faith is entrusted to the church in Scripture and Tradition; see Catechism 84. The Reformed Westminster Confession 1.6 locates the necessary rule of faith and life in Scripture and what follows from it. Both claim continuity with the apostolic faith; they differ over the authority of received church teaching alongside Scripture.

The angels in verse 6 also receive different readings. Calvin and Wesley emphasize their abandonment of their original place and dignity. Calvin understands the chains as the misery that accompanies their fall, rather than confinement to one place. The USCCB note connects the verse with the heavenly beings and women in Genesis 6, and reads the strange flesh of verse 7 in relation to the angelic visitors at Sodom.

The glorious beings in verse 8 are understood as human rulers by Calvin, honored Christian ministers by Wesley, and angels in the USCCB note. Michael’s restraint stands against the offenders’ arrogance in these readings. The passage does not itself rank Michael below the devil or explain the reason for his restraint in terms of an angelic hierarchy.

Readers also differ over the wider book associated with Enoch’s prophecy. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church lists Enoch among its Scriptures. The Westminster Confession’s list in chapter 1 does not include it. Calvin favors a prophecy handed down orally; the USCCB note identifies a citation of the written book. These are distinct questions about the prophecy’s transmission and the status of the whole book. Jude uses the prophecy here to warn of God’s judgment.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

First Corinthians 10:1–13 explicitly connects Israel’s wilderness history with Christ and with warnings for the church. Paul identifies the spiritual rock as Christ and says that these events were written as examples for later believers. Jude’s examples serve that same purpose: God’s saving acts call for continuing trust and obedience, while the failures of earlier generations warn His people against turning privilege into presumption.

What is our position?

Our study The Berean Standard calls believers to examine teaching against Scripture, even when the speaker is respected. We apply that standard here by testing what is taught and taking seriously the conduct it encourages. The church’s responsibility is to hold faithfully to the truth it has received.
