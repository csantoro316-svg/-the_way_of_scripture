Passage

1 Corinthians 10:6-13

What can Israel’s failures teach us about temptation?

Berean Standard Bible

6 These things took place as examples to keep us from craving evil things as they did. 7 Do not be idolaters, as some of them were. As it is written: “The people sat down to eat and drink and got up to indulge in revelry.” 8 We should not commit sexual immorality, as some of them did, and in one day twenty-three thousand of them died. 9 We should not test Christ, as some of them did, and were killed by snakes. 10 And do not complain, as some of them did, and were killed by the destroying angel.

11 Now these things happened to them as examples and were written down as warnings for us, on whom the fulfillment of the ages has come. 12 So the one who thinks he is standing firm should be careful not to fall. 13 No temptation has seized you except what is common to man. And God is faithful; He will not let you be tempted beyond what you can bear. But when you are tempted, He will also provide an escape, so that you can stand up under it.

What is the context?

These events were recorded for the church’s instruction. Paul names cravings for evil, idol worship, sexual immorality, testing Christ and complaining. His examples make the warning concrete: people who had received God’s help still chose conduct that brought judgment.

The meal and revelry recall the golden calf in Exodus 32:1–8. Numbers 25:1–9 connects sexual immorality with joining the worship of other gods; Numbers 21:4–9 describes complaints followed by deadly snakes. Paul is preparing the Corinthians to recognize the danger of idol worship in their own setting, not inviting them to look down on Israel.

His warning about confidence applies to anyone who thinks falling is someone else’s problem. But the next verse gives encouragement: temptation is not unique to them, and God remains faithful. He provides a way to endure without giving in. The promise calls for trusting Him and taking the way He provides; the very next instruction is to flee idolatry.

The Greek word peirasmos (pie-rahs-MOSS), πειρασμός, can refer to a test or a temptation; Thayer’s entry explains both uses. Here the surrounding examples concern temptation to sin. Paul is not promising that every grief or hardship will feel manageable. He elsewhere describes suffering beyond his own ability to endure, which taught him to depend on God rather than himself (2 Corinthians 1:8–10).

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this passage as both a serious warning and reassurance of God’s faithfulness. Wesley’s notes on verses 12–13 and the USCCB notes hold those together: confidence in one’s own safety is misplaced, while God’s help gives a reason to resist temptation rather than surrender to it.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 3:7–19 also brings Israel’s wilderness history directly to believers in Christ. It warns against a heart that turns away from God and calls people to encourage one another daily, so that sin does not harden them.

That gives these warnings a place in the life of God’s people together. Remembering the past is meant to help a community remain faithful now. The goal is sharing in Christ and holding firmly to Him; helping another person resist temptation belongs to that purpose, alongside taking our own choices seriously.
