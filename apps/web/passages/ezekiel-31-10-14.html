Passage

Ezekiel 31:10-14

Why is the great cedar cut down?

Berean Standard Bible

10 Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Since it became great in height and set its top among the clouds, and it grew proud on account of its height, 11 I delivered it into the hand of the ruler of the nations, for him to deal with it according to its wickedness. I have banished it. 12 Foreigners, the most ruthless of the nations, cut it down and left it. Its branches have fallen on the mountains and in every valley; its boughs lay broken in all the earth’s ravines. And all the peoples of the earth left its shade and abandoned it. 13 All the birds of the air nested on its fallen trunk, and all the beasts of the field lived among its boughs. 14 This happened so that no other trees by the waters would become great in height and set their tops among the clouds, and no other well-watered trees would reach them in height. For they have all been consigned to death, to the depths of the earth, among the mortals who descend to the Pit.’

What is the context?

The tree’s height becomes the occasion for pride. Its top among the clouds now expresses the self-exaltation that brings the Lord’s judgment.

The Lord delivers it to the ruler of the nations, and ruthless foreigners cut it down. Branches lie broken across mountains, valleys and ravines, while the peoples once sheltered by it leave its shade.

The fallen tree becomes a place for birds and animals, reversing the scene of life beneath its standing branches. Its downfall warns the other well-watered trees against the same exaltation. Greatness does not exempt them from being brought down among mortals who descend to the Pit.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads the connection as greatness followed by pride and then ruin. The fall warns others against the same course. Wesley likewise takes the announcement as a warning to rulers and flourishing states.

Cambridge emphasizes the violent and dishonorable death pictured here, rather than treating the passage merely as a reminder that everyone eventually dies. The warning concerns what proud power does and the judgment to which that conduct leads.
