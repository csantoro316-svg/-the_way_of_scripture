Passage

Revelation 16:1-11

What do the first five bowls reveal about judgment and resistance?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I heard a loud voice from the temple saying to the seven angels, “Go, pour out on the earth the seven bowls of God’s wrath.” 2 So the first angel went and poured out his bowl on the earth, and loathsome, malignant sores broke out on those who had the mark of the beast and worshiped its image.

3 And the second angel poured out his bowl into the sea, and it turned to blood like that of the dead, and every living thing in the sea died. 4 And the third angel poured out his bowl into the rivers and springs of water, and they turned to blood. 5 And I heard the angel of the waters say: “Righteous are You, O Holy One, who is and was, because You have brought these judgments. 6 For they have spilled the blood of saints and prophets, and You have given them blood to drink, as they deserve.” 7 And I heard the altar reply: “Yes, Lord God Almighty, true and just are Your judgments.”

8 Then the fourth angel poured out his bowl on the sun, and it was given power to scorch the people with fire. 9 And the people were scorched by intense heat, and they cursed the name of God, who had authority over these plagues. Yet they did not repent and give Him glory.

10 And the fifth angel poured out his bowl on the throne of the beast, and its kingdom was plunged into darkness, and men began to gnaw their tongues in anguish 11 and curse the God of heaven for their pains and sores. Yet they did not repent of their deeds.

What is the context?

A voice from the temple commands the seven angels to pour out their bowls. The first brings painful sores on people who bear the beast’s mark and worship its image. The judgment directly confronts the allegiance demanded in chapter 13.

The second turns the sea to blood like that of the dead, and every living thing in it dies. The third turns rivers and springs to blood. An angel explains the justice of this judgment: those who spilled the blood of saints and prophets are given blood to drink. The altar answers by affirming that God’s judgments are true and just.

The fourth affects the sun, which scorches people with intense heat. They recognize God’s authority over the plagues, yet curse His name instead of repenting and giving Him glory. Knowledge of who is acting does not produce a change in their response.

The fifth strikes the beast’s throne, plunging its kingdom into darkness. People gnaw their tongues in anguish and curse God over their pains and sores. The continuing sores connect this scene with the earlier bowl. Once again, John records that they do not repent.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The main difference concerns whether the plagues describe physical events or picture judgments on religious and political powers. Wesley expects future physical plagues. Walvoord likewise expects severe future judgments. Gill understands the bowls through the downfall of powers associated with the papacy, interpreting the sea especially in terms of Rome’s authority and its maritime territories.

Gill did not simply regard these bowls as already fulfilled in the Reformation; he expected judgments still to come. His reading differs from a physical reading in what the plagues represent, as well as in the powers he identifies. It should not be presented as the position of all Baptists or Reformed Christians.

The USCCB notes emphasize the plagues’ modeling on Exodus and understand the beast’s throne as the forces of evil. Across these explanations, the blood of God’s servants and the refusal to repent remain central. The chapter presents a moral reckoning, not merely a sequence of disasters.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The bowls recall the plagues of Egypt: boils in Exodus 9:8–11, water turned to blood in Exodus 7:19–21, and darkness in Exodus 10:21–23. In Exodus, God confronts an oppressive power and brings His people out. Revelation’s repeated imagery places the beast’s rule within that larger pattern of opposition that God will overcome.

The explanation about shed blood also connects with the martyrs’ appeal for justice in Revelation 6:9–11. Their suffering has not been forgotten. The angel and altar affirm the righteousness of God’s answer to their appeal.
