Passage

Genesis 50:15-21

Joseph Comforts His Brothers

Berean Standard Bible

15 When Joseph’s brothers saw that their father was dead, they said, “What if Joseph bears a grudge? Then he will surely repay us for all the evil that we did to him.”

16 So they sent word to Joseph, saying, “Before he died, your father commanded, 17 ‘This is what you are to say to Joseph: I beg you, please forgive the transgression and sin of your brothers, for they did you wrong.’ So now, Joseph, please forgive the transgression of the servants of the God of your father.”

When their message came to him, Joseph wept. 18 His brothers also came to him, bowed down before him, and said, “We are your slaves!”

19 But Joseph replied, “Do not be afraid. Am I in the place of God? 20 As for you, what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good, in order to accomplish a day like this—to preserve the lives of many people. 21 Therefore do not be afraid. I will provide for you and your little ones.” So Joseph reassured his brothers and spoke kindly to them.

What is the context?

After Jacob's death, the brothers fear that Joseph may now repay them for their earlier evil. They send a message asking forgiveness and reporting that their father had asked for it before he died. Joseph weeps when he hears it. The brothers then come personally, bow before him, and offer themselves as slaves.

Joseph tells them not to fear and asks whether he is in God's place. He names their intention as evil while explaining that God intended good through the same history: many people have been preserved alive. This repeats the saving purpose he described when he first made himself known.

The verb behind preserving life is חָיָה, ḥāyâ (khah-YAH). Brown–Driver–Briggs specifically explains its form in Genesis 50:20 as keeping people alive. Here it concerns survival through the famine, made possible by Joseph's position in Egypt.

His reassurance becomes a promise to provide for them and their little ones. He speaks kindly to them. The death of their father has not undone the forgiveness already offered, and Joseph uses his power to sustain the family rather than take revenge.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see forgiveness, human responsibility, and God's saving purpose held together here. Wesley's notes on Genesis 50:20–21 emphasize good brought from evil and kindness expressed in both action and speech. The Catholic Catechism, paragraph 312, likewise uses Joseph's explanation while making clear that evil does not become good because God brings good from it.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The larger Christian comparison is with God's saving work through Christ's rejection and death. The Catholic Catechism, paragraph 312, explicitly places Joseph's story beside that greater good brought from human evil. Acts 2:22–24 likewise holds together God's purpose, the wrongdoing of those who put Jesus to death, and His resurrection. The comparison preserves responsibility while recognizing a purpose that evil does not defeat.

Joseph's refusal to take revenge also fits Romans 12:17–21, where believers are told to leave judgment with God and answer an enemy's need with practical care. His feeding of the family shows what that can look like after a serious wrong. Mercy is expressed through continued provision, including for children who were not responsible for the betrayal.
