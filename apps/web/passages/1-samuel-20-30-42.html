Passage

1 Samuel 20:30-42

What does loyalty cost Jonathan and David?

Berean Standard Bible

30 Then Saul’s anger burned against Jonathan, and he said to him, “You son of a perverse and rebellious woman! Do I not know that you have chosen the son of Jesse to your own shame and to the shame of the mother who bore you? 31 For as long as the son of Jesse lives on this earth, neither you nor your kingdom shall be established. Now send for him and bring him to me, for he must surely die!” 32 “Why must he be put to death?” Jonathan replied. “What has he done?” 33 Then Saul hurled his spear at Jonathan to kill him. So Jonathan knew that his father was determined to kill David. 34 Jonathan got up from the table in fierce anger and did not eat any food that second day of the month, for he was grieved by his father’s shameful treatment of David.

35 In the morning Jonathan went out to the field for the appointment with David, and a small boy was with him. 36 He said to the boy, “Run and find the arrows I shoot.” And as the boy ran, Jonathan shot an arrow beyond him. 37 When the boy reached the place where Jonathan’s arrow had fallen, Jonathan called to him, “Isn’t the arrow beyond you?” 38 Then Jonathan cried out, “Hurry! Make haste! Do not delay!” So the boy picked up the arrow and returned to his master. 39 But the boy did not know anything; only Jonathan and David knew the arrangement. 40 Then Jonathan gave his equipment to the boy and said, “Go, take it back to the city.”

41 When the young man had gone, David got up from the south side of the stone, fell facedown, and bowed three times. Then he and Jonathan kissed each other and wept together—though David wept more. 42 And Jonathan said to David, “Go in peace, for the two of us have sworn in the name of the LORD, saying, ‘May the LORD be a witness between you and me, and between your descendants and mine forever.’” Then David got up and departed, and Jonathan went back into the city.

What is the context?

Saul’s anger breaks into insults and a demand for David’s death. He tells Jonathan that David’s survival prevents Jonathan’s kingdom from being established. The choice Saul presents is clear: preserve the family’s claim to power by handing over David.

Jonathan asks again what David has done to deserve death. Saul answers by throwing his spear at his own son. The attack removes Jonathan’s uncertainty about his father’s intention. He leaves the table angry and grieved by Saul’s treatment of David.

The next morning Jonathan uses the agreed signal, shooting an arrow beyond the boy and calling him farther on. After sending the boy back to the city with his equipment, he meets David. David bows, and the friends kiss and weep. Their promise remains, but David must leave.

Jonathan sends him away with the LORD as witness between their families. David departs and Jonathan returns to the city. This painful separation is not their final meeting: 1 Samuel 23:16–18 later describes Jonathan going to encourage David in God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley highlights the bitter irony in Saul’s actions. He claims to be protecting Jonathan’s future, then tries to kill Jonathan himself. The danger comes from the king’s anger, while the supposed rival is the man Jonathan is protecting.

Henry reads Jonathan’s choice as accepting the cost of identifying with someone God favors. Gill emphasizes the friendship and respect shown in the farewell. David’s tears do not erase his faith or make his loss insignificant; he is leaving people and a life he loves.

Gill reads the final promise as a commitment between both men and their descendants, with God as witness. Separation does not dissolve it. The peace Jonathan speaks concerns that continuing faithfulness, even though he cannot offer David a safe return to Saul’s court.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jonathan refuses to make family advantage a reason to betray an innocent friend. His decision illustrates a wider biblical tension: faithfulness to God can cost a person the approval of those closest to him.

In Matthew 10:34–39, Jesus warns that following Him can bring division within a household and calls His disciples to love Him above even their closest family ties. This is a parallel about costly loyalty, rather than a claim that every family disagreement repeats David’s story. Jesus calls people to follow Him, not to seek conflict or excuse cruelty toward relatives.
