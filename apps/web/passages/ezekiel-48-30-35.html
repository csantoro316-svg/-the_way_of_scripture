Passage

Ezekiel 48:30-35

Why does the book end with the name “The Lord Is There”?

Berean Standard Bible

30 “These will be the exits of the city: Beginning on the north side, which will be 4,500 cubits long, 31 the gates of the city will be named after the tribes of Israel. On the north side there will be three gates: the gate of Reuben, the gate of Judah, and the gate of Levi. 32 On the east side, which will be 4,500 cubits long, there will be three gates: the gate of Joseph, the gate of Benjamin, and the gate of Dan. 33 On the south side, which will be 4,500 cubits long, there will be three gates: the gate of Simeon, the gate of Issachar, and the gate of Zebulun. 34 And on the west side, which will be 4,500 cubits long, there will be three gates: the gate of Gad, the gate of Asher, and the gate of Naphtali.

35 The perimeter of the city will be 18,000 cubits, and from that day on the name of the city will be: THE LORD IS THERE.”

What is the context?

The city has three gates on each of its four sides. They bear the names of Israel’s tribes, including Levi and Joseph. Here Joseph is named as one tribe rather than divided into Ephraim and Manasseh as in the land portions.

The final measurement gives the city’s perimeter, but its defining feature is its name: the Lord is there. The book that showed His glory departing from a defiled temple ends with His presence among a restored people.

The promise answers the fear that judgment and exile had ended the relationship. The final word is not merely a rebuilt city, an assigned border or a functioning sanctuary. It is the Lord dwelling with His people.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Cambridge understands the name as the fulfillment of the covenant relationship: the Lord is their God and they are His people. Wesley applies the hope both to restored Jerusalem and to the lasting blessing of God’s presence with believers.

Henry likewise reads the closing city through life in Christ’s church and the hope of being with God forever. These readings place the emphasis where the book places it—on the Lord’s presence.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A close parallel appears in Revelation 21:1–4, 9–27. The new Jerusalem has twelve gates bearing the tribes’ names, and God’s dwelling is with His people. Death, mourning, crying and pain are removed.

Revelation 22:1–5 adds that His servants see His face and live in His light. These connections carry Ezekiel’s closing hope into the new creation: belonging to God and being with Him are at the heart of the future He gives His people.
