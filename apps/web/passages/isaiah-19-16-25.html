Passage

Isaiah 19:16-25

How can Egypt and Assyria become a blessing alongside Israel?

Berean Standard Bible

16 In that day the Egyptians will be like women. They will tremble with fear beneath the uplifted hand of the LORD of Hosts, when He brandishes it against them. 17 The land of Judah will bring terror to Egypt; whenever Judah is mentioned, Egypt will tremble over what the LORD of Hosts has planned against it. 18 In that day five cities in the land of Egypt will speak the language of Canaan and swear allegiance to the LORD of Hosts. One of them will be called the City of the Sun.

19 In that day there will be an altar to the LORD in the center of the land of Egypt, and a pillar to the LORD near her border. 20 It will be a sign and a witness to the LORD of Hosts in the land of Egypt. When they cry out to the LORD because of their oppressors, He will send them a savior and defender to rescue them. 21 The LORD will make Himself known to Egypt, and on that day Egypt will acknowledge the LORD. They will worship with sacrifices and offerings; they will make vows to the LORD and fulfill them. 22 And the LORD will strike Egypt with a plague; He will strike them but heal them. They will turn to the LORD, and He will hear their prayers and heal them.

23 In that day there will be a highway from Egypt to Assyria. The Assyrians will go to Egypt, and the Egyptians to Assyria. The Egyptians and Assyrians will worship together. 24 In that day Israel will join a three-party alliance with Egypt and Assyria—a blessing upon the earth. 25 The LORD of Hosts will bless them, saying, “Blessed be Egypt My people, Assyria My handiwork, and Israel My inheritance.”

What is the context?

The threat against Egypt gives way to a striking promise. Fear of the Lord’s action is followed by cities speaking the language of Canaan and swearing allegiance to Him. An altar and a pillar become a sign and witness to the Lord within Egypt.

When the Egyptians cry out because of their oppressors, the Lord sends a savior and defender. He makes Himself known to them, they worship Him, and they fulfill their vows. The passage describes Him striking and healing: they turn to Him, He hears, and He heals.

The promise then reaches beyond Egypt. A highway links Egypt and Assyria, and the two peoples worship together. Israel joins them as a blessing upon the earth.

The final declaration gives each a place in that blessing. Egypt is called God’s people, Assyria His handiwork, and Israel His inheritance. Nations known in these chapters as threats and oppressors are now included in worship and favor from the Lord.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry, Wesley and Gill emphasize the gathering of Gentiles with Jews through the gospel. They read the altar and sacrifices as language for worship through Christ, including prayer and praise. In this reading, people formerly divided by hostility are united in serving God.

A future kingdom reading places more emphasis on these named nations in their lands. Walvoord, in The Nations in Prophecy, understands the later part of the chapter as reaching its ultimate fulfillment in Christ’s future reign, with Egypt, Assyria and Israel sharing in its blessing.

The readings differ over when and how the details take shape. Both recognize that the promise goes beyond Egypt merely surviving: the Egyptians know the Lord, turn to Him and join others in His worship.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise that former enemies will worship together belongs to Scripture’s wider purpose of reconciliation. Ephesians 2:11–22 explains that Gentiles once far away are brought near through Christ’s blood. He makes peace and reconciles Jews and Gentiles to God in one body through the cross.

Paul does not directly quote Isaiah 19 here or explain the highway’s geography. He does explain the saving work through which divided peoples receive access to the Father by one Spirit and become members of God’s household.

The connection carries the hope beyond a political alliance: God brings people who were estranged into His presence together. Isaiah’s final blessing on Egypt, Assyria and Israel is an especially vivid expression of that wider biblical hope.
