Passage

Hebrews 10:26-31

Why is rejecting Christ’s sacrifice so serious?

Berean Standard Bible

26 If we deliberately go on sinning after we have received the knowledge of the truth, no further sacrifice for sins remains, 27 but only a fearful expectation of judgment and of raging fire that will consume all adversaries.

28 Anyone who rejected the law of Moses died without mercy on the testimony of two or three witnesses. 29 How much more severely do you think one deserves to be punished who has trampled on the Son of God, profaned the blood of the covenant that sanctified him, and insulted the Spirit of grace? 30 For we know Him who said, “Vengeance is Mine; I will repay,” and again, “The Lord will judge His people.” 31 It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.

What is the context?

The warning follows the invitation to draw near, hold to hope and encourage one another. Verse 29 describes the rejection in severe terms: trampling on the Son, treating the covenant blood as unholy and insulting the Spirit of grace. The concern reaches beyond a believer discovering another failure and sincerely seeking help.

No further sacrifice remains because the letter has identified Christ’s offering as the sufficient one. The warning cannot be answered by finding a different sacrifice to replace Him. It speaks of judgment rather than minimizing the rejection.

The comparison with Moses’ law moves from a lesser case to a greater one. Deuteronomy 17:2–7 describes the two-or-three-witness requirement in a case of turning to other gods. The writer then draws on Deuteronomy 32:35–36 to underline that judgment belongs to God.

The person is described as having received knowledge of the truth and as sanctified by the covenant blood. Those descriptions help explain why Christians differ over whether the warning concerns someone who was truly saved. Both the descriptions and the threatened judgment matter to understanding it.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians differ over the status of the person described and the judgment threatened. Calvin reads the offense as a total repudiation of Christ, rather than every particular sin, and the outcome as eternal loss. He also speaks of God separating hypocrites from the truly faithful. The Reformed Westminster Confession, 17, teaches that those truly brought into saving faith are preserved to the end, while still capable of grave sin and discipline.

Wesley also understands a deliberate, total abandonment of Christ. He takes the person described as having previously been justified and reads the warning as a real danger of losing that saving relationship. This differs from the Reformed account of final perseverance.

In a Free Grace reading, J. Paul Tanner understands the people warned as genuine believers who can abandon their confession yet remain eternally saved. He proposes severe consequences at Christ’s judgment, including lost reward and a forfeited role in the coming kingdom. His paper presents this as an interpretation requiring explanation, rather than a view shared by all Christians.

The Catholic Catechism, 162, teaches that faith can be lost. It also teaches the possibility of reconciliation after serious post-baptismal sin in 1446. That is relevant doctrinal context, rather than the Catechism’s direct explanation of every phrase here. The warning should not be turned into a claim that every knowingly committed sin makes repentance impossible.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 12:22–25 later joins the new covenant, Jesus and His blood with another warning not to refuse the One who speaks. Grace and the call to heed God’s voice appear together within the letter’s purpose.

Romans 12:19–21 uses the same statement about God’s vengeance to forbid personal revenge and call believers to overcome evil with good. A warning about divine judgment does not give people permission to punish others in God’s name.
