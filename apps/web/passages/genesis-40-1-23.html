Passage

Genesis 40:1-23

The Cupbearer and the Baker

Berean Standard Bible

1 Some time later, the king’s cupbearer and baker offended their master, the king of Egypt. 2 Pharaoh was angry with his two officers, the chief cupbearer and the chief baker, 3 and imprisoned them in the house of the captain of the guard, the same prison where Joseph was confined. 4 The captain of the guard assigned them to Joseph, and he became their personal attendant.

After they had been in custody for some time, 5 both of these men—the Egyptian king’s cupbearer and baker, who were being held in the prison—had a dream on the same night, and each dream had its own meaning.

6 When Joseph came to them in the morning, he saw that they were distraught. 7 So he asked the officials of Pharaoh who were in custody with him in his master’s house, “Why are your faces so downcast today?”

8 “We both had dreams,” they replied, “but there is no one to interpret them.”

Then Joseph said to them, “Don’t interpretations belong to God? Tell me your dreams.”

9 So the chief cupbearer told Joseph his dream: “In my dream there was a vine before me, 10 and on the vine were three branches. As it budded, its blossoms opened and its clusters ripened into grapes. 11 Pharaoh’s cup was in my hand, and I took the grapes, squeezed them into his cup, and placed the cup in his hand.”

12 Joseph replied, “This is the interpretation: The three branches are three days. 13 Within three days Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore your position. You will put Pharaoh’s cup in his hand, just as you did when you were his cupbearer. 14 But when it goes well for you, please remember me and show me kindness by mentioning me to Pharaoh, that he might bring me out of this prison. 15 For I was kidnapped from the land of the Hebrews, and even here I have done nothing for which they should have put me in this dungeon.”

16 When the chief baker saw that the interpretation was favorable, he said to Joseph, “I too had a dream: There were three baskets of white bread on my head. 17 In the top basket were all sorts of baked goods for Pharaoh, but the birds were eating them out of the basket on my head.”

18 Joseph replied, “This is the interpretation: The three baskets are three days. 19 Within three days Pharaoh will lift off your head and hang you on a tree. Then the birds will eat the flesh of your body.”

20 On the third day, which was Pharaoh’s birthday, he held a feast for all his officials, and in their presence he lifted up the heads of the chief cupbearer and the chief baker. 21 Pharaoh restored the chief cupbearer to his position, so that he once again placed the cup in Pharaoh’s hand. 22 But Pharaoh hanged the chief baker, just as Joseph had described to them in his interpretation.

23 The chief cupbearer, however, did not remember Joseph; he forgot all about him.

What is the context?

Two of Pharaoh's officials, his chief cupbearer and chief baker, offend him and are confined in the prison where Joseph is held. The captain of the guard assigns Joseph to attend them. The account does not state their offenses; what matters for the story is that these men from Pharaoh's court now come into contact with Joseph.

Both have troubling dreams on the same night. Joseph notices their distress and asks about it. When they say no interpreter is available, he directs their attention to God as the source of interpretation and invites them to tell him the dreams. He offers to help without presenting the ability as his own independent power.

The cupbearer's dream concerns a vine, grapes, and Pharaoh's cup. Joseph explains that he will be restored to his position in three days. He then asks the man to remember him and mention his case to Pharaoh, explaining that he was kidnapped and has done nothing to deserve imprisonment. His trust in God leaves room to seek release from injustice.

The baker hopes for an equally favorable answer, but his dream of bread baskets and birds has a different meaning. Joseph says he will be executed in three days. At Pharaoh's birthday feast, both interpretations prove true: the cupbearer returns to service, while the baker is hanged.

The chapter ends with disappointment for Joseph. The restored cupbearer forgets him. A real connection to Pharaoh has been made, and Joseph's interpretation has been confirmed, but his request for help brings no immediate release.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see God's gift at work through Joseph, along with the difficulty of waiting when help seems possible but does not come. Wesley's notes on Genesis 40:8, 14 emphasize both Joseph's crediting God and the legitimacy of his desire for freedom. Faithful service in prison does not require him to approve his wrongful confinement.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

This meeting supplies the human connection that will eventually bring Joseph before Pharaoh. Psalm 105:17–22 places Joseph's confinement and the testing of his word before the king's release and appointment of him. The sequence gives the chapter a place in God's preparation to preserve life through Joseph.

Joseph is also attentive to other people's distress while still suffering himself. That fits the wider theme in 2 Corinthians 1:3–7, where God's comfort in trouble equips His people to care for others. The accounts differ, but both show that someone enduring hardship can still become a means of help to those nearby.
