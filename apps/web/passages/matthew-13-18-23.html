Passage

Matthew 13:18-23

Receiving the Word and Bearing Fruit

Berean Standard Bible

18 Consider, then, the parable of the sower: 19 When anyone hears the message of the kingdom but does not understand it, the evil one comes and snatches away what was sown in his heart. This is the seed sown along the path.

20 The seed sown on rocky ground is the one who hears the word and at once receives it with joy. 21 But since he has no root, he remains for only a season. When trouble or persecution comes because of the word, he quickly falls away. 22 The seed sown among the thorns is the one who hears the word, but the worries of this life and the deceitfulness of wealth choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful.

23 But the seed sown on good soil is the one who hears the word and understands it. He indeed bears fruit and produces a crop—a hundredfold, sixtyfold, or thirtyfold.”

What is the context?

Jesus now explains the sower's story. The seed concerns hearing the message of the kingdom. On the path, the hearer does not understand and the evil one snatches away what was sown. The comparison moves from the outward scene into the heart's response.

The rocky-ground hearer receives the message with joy but has no root. Trouble or persecution because of the word brings a quick falling away. Initial excitement does not by itself show the endurance the message calls for.

Among thorns, the message is choked by life's worries and the deceitfulness of wealth. These pressures make it unfruitful. Luke 8:11–15 also names pleasures and describes fruit failing to mature. The warning reaches ordinary desires and concerns that can crowd out a response to God.

Good soil describes the person who hears and understands, then bears fruit. The amounts vary, but fruit is the shared result. Jesus' explanation calls hearers toward a lasting response. Christian traditions explain differently the saving condition of people who initially receive the word and later turn away.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as a call to keep trusting and living out the word, beyond the excitement of first hearing it. Traditions differ over whether someone who turns away from Christ once had saving faith.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 11.2 and 17.1–3, teaches that saving faith is active in love and that God keeps His people to the end, even when they fall into serious sins for a time. The 1689 Baptist Confession, 17.1, likewise teaches that God keeps them to the end.

Wesley on Matthew 13:20–22 warns that worries and riches can gradually choke a person's response to God's word. The Methodist Articles of Religion, XII, allow for departing from grace and falling into sin, with repentance and return possible by grace. The Catholic Catechism, 162, teaches that faith is God's gift, can be lost, and must be nourished. These teachings lead to different explanations of whether a person who turns away ever had saving faith.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The connection between hearing and a changed life continues throughout the New Testament. James 1:21–25 calls people to receive the planted word and carry it out, rather than hearing and forgetting. The message is meant to take hold in life.

Colossians 1:5–10 describes the gospel bearing fruit and growing across the world, then speaks of believers bearing fruit in every good work and growing in knowledge of God. That wider picture includes both the spread of the good news and the lives it changes. Fruitfulness cannot be reduced to one visible number or achievement.
