Passage

Matthew 12:46-50

The Family Gathered Around Jesus

Berean Standard Bible

46 While Jesus was still speaking to the crowds, His mother and brothers stood outside, wanting to speak to Him. 47 Someone told Him, “Look, Your mother and brothers are standing outside, wanting to speak to You.”

48 But Jesus replied, “Who is My mother, and who are My brothers?” 49 Pointing to His disciples, He said, “Here are My mother and My brothers. 50 For whoever does the will of My Father in heaven is My brother and sister and mother.”

What is the context?

Jesus is still speaking to the crowds when His mother and brothers arrive outside, wanting to speak to Him. He answers the message by asking who His mother and brothers are, then points to His disciples. The moment becomes a teaching about the family formed around Him.

He names doing His Father's will as the mark of that relationship. The related account in Luke 8:19–21 describes His family as those who hear God's word and carry it out. Nearness to Jesus therefore involves receiving and following God's word; it is not established by a family connection alone.

The Greek word is ἀδελφός, adelphos (ah-del-FOSS), brother. Thayer's entry discusses both family relationships and its use for fellow believers, while arguing that the relatives in this passage are Mary's other children. Jesus deliberately brings family language into His description of disciples. Traditions differ over the precise family relationships of those waiting outside, as explained below.

The saying gives His disciples a remarkable place of belonging. He calls them brother, sister, and mother. His words establish the importance of this relationship with Him; the passage does not require treating ordinary love and care for relatives as worthless.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the central saying as Jesus welcoming those who do the Father's will into His spiritual family. Wesley on Matthew 12:46–50 emphasizes that spiritual relationship, while the Catholic Catechism, 500–501, places the family language within its teaching about Mary and Christ's people.

Traditions differ over the brothers' relationship to Mary. The Catholic Church teaches in Catechism 499–500 that Mary remained a virgin and these brothers are close relatives, not other children of hers. The Orthodox Church in America's life of Joseph presents the Orthodox tradition that they were Joseph's children from an earlier marriage.

An Orthodox Presbyterian Church question-and-answer article reads them as children born to Mary and Joseph after Jesus, and therefore His half-brothers. This is that church resource's explanation, rather than a claim about every Reformed Christian. Protestant interpreters have also differed: Wesley on 12:46 understands them as relatives through Mary's family. The difference over the family tree does not remove Jesus' central call to hear and do His Father's will.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The family language reaches into the saving purpose described in Hebrews 2:10–18. God brings many children to glory through Jesus, who shares human life and suffering and is not ashamed to call His people brothers. Their belonging rests on the Savior who comes near enough to help and save them.

That makes this more than a picture of a friendly group. Jesus gathers people into a relationship with Himself and the Father. The disciples He points to anticipate the wider family whose life together will be shaped by hearing God's word and following the Son.
