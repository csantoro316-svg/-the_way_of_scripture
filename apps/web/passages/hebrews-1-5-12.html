Passage

Hebrews 1:5-12

How do the Scriptures distinguish the Son from the angels?

Berean Standard Bible

5 For to which of the angels did God ever say: “You are My Son; today I have become Your Father”? Or again: “I will be His Father, and He will be My Son”? 6 And again, when God brings His firstborn into the world, He says: “Let all God’s angels worship Him.”

7 Now about the angels He says: “He makes His angels winds, His servants flames of fire.” 8 But about the Son He says: “Your throne, O God, endures forever and ever, and justice is the scepter of Your kingdom. 9 You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness; therefore God, Your God, has anointed You above Your companions with the oil of joy.”

10 And: “In the beginning, O Lord, You laid the foundations of the earth, and the heavens are the work of Your hands. 11 They will perish, but You remain; they will all wear out like a garment. 12 You will roll them up like a robe; like a garment they will be changed; but You remain the same, and Your years will never end.”

What is the context?

The writer now supports the comparison with passages from Scripture. Psalm 2:7 and 2 Samuel 7:12–16 supply the royal Son and Father language. No angel is given the Son’s place. The angels are instead commanded to worship Him.

Verses 7–9 contrast the angels’ service with the Son’s enduring throne and righteous rule. The quotation about the throne comes from Psalm 45:6–7. Hebrews applies its words directly to the Son, addressing Him as God.

Verses 10–12 then apply Psalm 102:25–27 to Him. The heavens and earth can wear out and be changed, while He remains. Together the quotations present a Son who is worshiped, reigns in righteousness, created all things and endures beyond them.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Calvin and Wesley both read these quotations as establishing the Son’s unique standing and deity. The USCCB notes likewise emphasize that words addressed to God in Psalm 102 are here applied to Jesus. The angels’ worship and service distinguish them from the Son.

Calvin and Wesley differ on the emphasis of today in verse 5. Wesley understands it in relation to the Son’s eternal relation to the Father; Calvin understands it as the public making known of His Sonship. Both affirm that the Son is eternal. Their difference is about what this quoted sentence highlights, rather than whether He began as a created angel.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The royal promise in 2 Samuel 7:12–16 concerns David’s descendant and an enduring kingdom. Hebrews itself draws that promise into its account of the Son. Luke 1:30–33 similarly announces Jesus as the One whose kingdom will not end. The connection is made within the New Testament, rather than resting on a resemblance a later reader noticed.

The angels’ worship in verse 6 also echoes Deuteronomy 32:43. The BSB includes that line and explains its textual witnesses in the footnote. The passage gathers Scripture’s royal promises, creation language and worship around the Son, showing how those earlier words belong within God’s purpose in Christ.

What is our position?

Our study Worship as the Early Church Knew It explicitly recognizes the worship directed to Christ in Hebrews 1 as reverence toward deity. Its discussion of bowing and submission does not reduce the angels’ response here to an ordinary social greeting.
