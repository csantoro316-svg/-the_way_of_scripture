Passage

Genesis 14:10-16

Abram Rescues Lot

Berean Standard Bible

10 Now the Valley of Siddim was full of tar pits, and as the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, some men fell into the pits, but the survivors fled to the hill country.

11 The four kings seized all the goods of Sodom and Gomorrah and all their food, and they went on their way. 12 They also carried off Abram’s nephew Lot and his possessions, since Lot was living in Sodom.

13 Then an escapee came and reported this to Abram the Hebrew. Now Abram was living near the Oaks of Mamre the Amorite, a brother of Eshcol and Aner, all of whom were bound by treaty to Abram. 14 And when Abram heard that his relative had been captured, he mobilized the 318 trained men born in his household, and they set out in pursuit as far as Dan.

15 During the night, Abram divided his forces and routed Chedorlaomer’s army, pursuing them as far as Hobah, north of Damascus. 16 He retrieved all the goods, as well as his relative Lot and his possessions, together with the women and the rest of the people.

What is the context?

The five kings are defeated, and the invading army takes the goods and food of Sodom and Gomorrah. Lot and his possessions are carried away as well. Genesis now says that Lot is living in Sodom, continuing the movement from his earlier choice of the plain and his tent pitched toward the city.

An escapee brings the news to Abram, who is living near Mamre and has allies there. When he hears that his relative has been captured, Abram mobilizes 318 trained men born in his household. He pursues the attackers, divides his forces for a night attack, and drives them beyond Damascus.

The rescue reaches beyond Lot alone. Abram brings back the goods, Lot's possessions, the women, and the other people. The relative from whom he had separated is still someone he will act to save. The next scene will credit God with the victory and show what Abram does with the recovered wealth.

How is this passage commonly understood?

A common Christian reading sees Abram acting out of concern for his captured relative rather than a desire to conquer or enrich himself. Wesley's notes on Genesis 14:13–16 emphasize that charitable purpose; the return of the people and Abram's later refusal of the spoils give it a concrete expression.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Abram's pursuit of a captive relative offers a helpful parallel to God's care for His people. In Matthew 18:12–14, Jesus describes a shepherd searching for a straying sheep to explain the Father's concern that none of His little ones should perish. Abram does not dismiss Lot as someone whose trouble no longer concerns him. He goes after him.

Colossians 1:12–14 describes the Father rescuing His people from the dominion of darkness and bringing them into the kingdom of His Son, where there is redemption and forgiveness. The parallel is the love that acts to rescue, rather than a promise that every military captivity will end as Lot's does. The fuller rescue God gives reaches the power of sin itself.
