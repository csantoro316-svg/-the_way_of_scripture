Passage

Ezekiel 32:27-32

What comfort can Pharaoh find among the fallen?

Berean Standard Bible

27 They do not lie down with the fallen warriors of old, who went down to Sheol with their weapons of war, whose swords were placed under their heads, whose shields rested on their bones, although the terror of the mighty was once in the land of the living. 28 But you too will be shattered and lie down among the uncircumcised, with those slain by the sword.

29 Edom is there, and all her kings and princes, who despite their might are laid among those slain by the sword. They lie down with the uncircumcised, with those who descend to the Pit. 30 All the leaders of the north and all the Sidonians are there; they went down in disgrace with the slain, despite the terror of their might. They lie uncircumcised with those slain by the sword and bear their shame with those who descend to the Pit.

31 Pharaoh will see them and be comforted over all his multitude— Pharaoh and all his army, slain by the sword, declares the Lord GOD. 32 For I will spread My terror in the land of the living, so that Pharaoh and all his multitude will be laid to rest among the uncircumcised, with those slain by the sword, declares the Lord GOD.”

What is the context?

The lament refers to warriors who went down with their weapons, then warns its hearer of being shattered among those slain by the sword. Military standing cannot remove the threatened judgment.

Edom’s rulers, the leaders of the north and the Sidonians are also there, despite the terror of their might. The repeated contrast is between what these powers once made others feel and the disgrace they now bear.

Pharaoh will see them and be comforted concerning his own multitude. This is the grim consolation of discovering that other mighty peoples share the same end. It is not a restoration of Egypt’s former power.

The Lord closes by declaring that He will spread His terror in the land of the living. Pharaoh and his multitude will be laid with the fallen. The judgments against the nations end with their inability to place themselves beyond His authority.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley reads verse 27 through a contrast in the honor given to fallen warriors. Cambridge considers the wording difficult and reads the burial scene as another expression of lives and deaths marked by violence. The BSB keeps the distinction between the groups who do not lie together, and refers to shields resting on the warriors’ bones.

Henry and Wesley both regard Pharaoh’s comfort as poor comfort: sharing the humiliation of others is no deliverance from his own. Henry also points beyond the fall of nations toward the resurrection and judgment taught by Christ.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 2:14–18 gives a larger answer to the fear of death. The Son shares human flesh and blood, makes atonement as a merciful and faithful high priest, and through His death breaks the devil’s power of death and frees those enslaved by that fear.

Hebrews does not explicitly quote this lament. It adds a hope greater than Pharaoh’s discovery that others have fallen too. The saving help comes from Christ entering human suffering and death on behalf of those He helps, rather than from finding a mightier earthly power in which to hide.
