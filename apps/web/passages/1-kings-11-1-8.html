Passage

1 Kings 11:1-8

How does Solomon’s heart turn away from the LORD?

Berean Standard Bible

1 King Solomon, however, loved many foreign women along with the daughter of Pharaoh—women of Moab, Ammon, Edom, and Sidon, as well as Hittite women. 2 These women were from the nations about which the LORD had told the Israelites, “You must not intermarry with them, for surely they will turn your hearts after their gods.” Yet Solomon clung to these women in love. 3 He had seven hundred wives of royal birth and three hundred concubines—and his wives turned his heart away.

4 For when Solomon grew old, his wives turned his heart after other gods, and he was not wholeheartedly devoted to the LORD his God, as his father David had been. 5 Solomon followed Ashtoreth the goddess of the Sidonians and Milcom the abomination of the Ammonites. 6 So Solomon did evil in the sight of the LORD; unlike his father David, he did not follow the LORD completely. 7 At that time on a hill east of Jerusalem, Solomon built a high place for Chemosh the abomination of Moab and for Molech the abomination of the Ammonites. 8 He did the same for all his foreign wives, who burned incense and sacrificed to their gods.

What is the context?

The account now makes Solomon’s failure explicit. He loves many foreign women and takes seven hundred royal wives and three hundred concubines. As he grows old, his wives turn his heart toward other gods.

The warning in Deuteronomy 7:3–4 explains the danger as being drawn away to serve other gods. Here that danger becomes reality: Solomon follows Ashtoreth and Milcom and builds places of worship for Chemosh and Molech, as well as providing for the worship of his other wives.

The text says Solomon clung to these women in love. The Hebrew verb dabaq, pronounced dah-BAHK, means to cling or hold close. Brown–Driver–Briggs lists this verse under the word’s use for attachment and loyalty. The account makes the direction of that attachment—and its effect on his devotion to God—the central concern.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads Solomon’s actions as participation in idol worship and warns that great wisdom and age do not protect someone from an unguarded heart. Wesley emphasizes the permission Solomon gives to this worship and considers his participation in its outward acts possible, while stopping short of saying he changed his beliefs about which God is true.

The difference concerns how to describe Solomon’s inward beliefs and involvement. The narrative itself is unambiguous about his responsibility: he follows other gods, establishes their worship and does evil in the LORD’s sight.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Solomon’s divided devotion belongs within a wider biblical warning about competing loyalties. In Matthew 6:24, Jesus explains that a person cannot serve two masters. His immediate example is God and money, but the principle helps explain why devotion cannot simply be divided without consequence.

This is a connection in theme, rather than a claim that Jesus is interpreting Solomon’s marriages. God’s purpose for His people calls for wholehearted faithfulness, and Solomon’s story shows what happens when another attachment takes priority.
