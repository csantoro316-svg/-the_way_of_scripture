Passage

Isaiah 25:1-12

What does it mean that God will swallow up death forever?

Berean Standard Bible

1 O LORD, You are my God! I will exalt You; I will praise Your name. For You have worked wonders— plans formed long ago— in perfect faithfulness. 2 Indeed, You have made the city a heap of rubble, the fortified town a ruin. The fortress of strangers is a city no more; it will never be rebuilt. 3 Therefore, a strong people will honor You. The cities of ruthless nations will revere You. 4 For You have been a refuge for the poor, a stronghold for the needy in distress, a refuge from the storm, a shade from the heat. For the breath of the ruthless is like rain against a wall, 5 like heat in a dry land. You subdue the uproar of foreigners. As the shade of a cloud cools the heat, so the song of the ruthless is silenced.

6 On this mountain the LORD of Hosts will prepare a lavish banquet for all the peoples, a feast of aged wine, of choice meat, of finely aged wine. 7 On this mountain He will swallow up the shroud that enfolds all peoples, the sheet that covers all nations; 8 He will swallow up death forever. The Lord GOD will wipe away the tears from every face and remove the disgrace of His people from the whole earth. For the LORD has spoken. 9 And in that day it will be said, “Surely this is our God; we have waited for Him, and He has saved us. This is the LORD for whom we have waited. Let us rejoice and be glad in His salvation.”

10 For the hand of the LORD will rest on this mountain. But Moab will be trampled in his place as straw is trodden into the dung pile. 11 He will spread out his hands within it, as a swimmer spreads his arms to swim. His pride will be brought low, despite the skill of his hands. 12 The high-walled fortress will be brought down, cast to the ground, into the dust.

What is the context?

The chapter answers the preceding judgment with praise. The Lord is exalted for faithfully accomplishing plans formed long ago, bringing down a fortified city and becoming a refuge for the poor and needy.

The images of shade and shelter contrast His care with the harshness of the ruthless. Strong peoples are brought to honor Him, and the noise of their opposition is subdued.

Then the Lord prepares a lavish banquet for all peoples on His mountain. He removes the covering spread over the nations, swallows up death forever and wipes tears from every face. His people’s disgrace is removed, and those who have waited for Him rejoice in His salvation.

The closing warning against Moab keeps judgment beside that promise. Pride and fortified walls are brought down into the dust. The chapter does not present human strength as the way into the feast; its hope rests in the Lord who saves and removes what His people cannot overcome.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley read the feast as the abundance God provides through the gospel for Jews and Gentiles. Henry connects it with the New Testament’s invitations to repentant sinners and sees the promised end of sorrow fully realized in the joy to come.

Gill discusses the gospel’s blessings, the marriage supper of the Lamb and the new Jerusalem as settings for the feast. He reads the defeat of death as including bodily resurrection, appealing to Paul’s use of this verse.

Henry and Wesley understand Moab in the final warning as also representing the enemies of God’s people. Across these readings, the feast and the fall of pride belong together: God comforts His people and ends the power that opposes His rule.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

First Corinthians 15:50–58 directly applies Isaiah’s promise to resurrection. Paul says the saying about death being swallowed up is fulfilled when the perishable is clothed with the imperishable and the mortal with immortality. He gives thanks for the victory God provides through Jesus Christ.

Revelation 21:1–5 also takes up the promise of tears wiped away, in a world where death, mourning and pain are no more. God dwells with His people and makes all things new.

The New Testament therefore carries this promise into the victory of Christ and the resurrection hope of His people. Death is not merely made easier to bear; its rule is overcome. Paul’s response is practical: remain steadfast and continue in the Lord’s work, knowing that it is not in vain.
