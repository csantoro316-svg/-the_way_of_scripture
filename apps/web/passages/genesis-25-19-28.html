Passage

Genesis 25:19-28

Jacob and Esau

Berean Standard Bible

19 This is the account of Abraham’s son Isaac. Abraham became the father of Isaac, 20 and Isaac was forty years old when he married Rebekah, the daughter of Bethuel the Aramean from Paddan-aram and the sister of Laban the Aramean.

21 Later, Isaac prayed to the LORD on behalf of his wife, because she was barren. And the LORD heard his prayer, and his wife Rebekah conceived.

22 But the children inside her struggled with each other, and she said, “Why is this happening to me?” So Rebekah went to inquire of the LORD, 23 and He declared to her:

“Two nations are in your womb,

and two peoples from within you will be separated;

one people will be stronger than the other,

and the older will serve the younger.”

24 When her time came to give birth, there were indeed twins in her womb. 25 The first one came out red, covered with hair like a fur coat; so they named him Esau. 26 After this, his brother came out grasping Esau’s heel; so he was named Jacob. And Isaac was sixty years old when the twins were born.

27 When the boys grew up, Esau became a skillful hunter, a man of the field, while Jacob was a quiet man who stayed at home. 28 Because Isaac had a taste for wild game, he loved Esau; but Rebekah loved Jacob.

What is the context?

The account now turns to Isaac and Rebekah's children. Isaac was forty when they married and sixty when the twins were born, so twenty years passed before they had children. He prays because Rebekah is unable to conceive, and the LORD answers. Genesis tells us about the prayer without saying that Isaac waited twenty years before first asking.

During the pregnancy, Rebekah is troubled by the children's struggle and brings her question to the LORD. His answer reaches beyond a difficult pregnancy: the two children will become two peoples, and the older will serve the younger. Their future is announced before either child has done anything to earn a preferred place.

Esau is born first, red and covered with hair. Jacob follows while grasping his brother's heel, giving the account its explanation for his name. They grow into different ways of life: Esau is a hunter, while Jacob stays among the tents. The Hebrew description of Jacob is תָּם, tām (tahm). Brown–Driver–Briggs gives a sense of sound or wholesome here, while also recording its moral use elsewhere. In this contrast it does not by itself prove that Jacob's later choices are all blameless.

The ending introduces divided affection in the household. Isaac favors Esau and enjoys his game; Rebekah favors Jacob. Those preferences prepare for the conflict in the chapters that follow. Genesis does not tell us whether Rebekah had shared God's message with Isaac.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that God's purpose is not controlled by the twins' birth order. They differ over how His choice here relates to His choice of people for salvation, especially when reading Paul's explanation in Romans 9.

Reformed churches following the Westminster Confession 3.5–6, and Baptists following the 1689 Confession 3.2, 5–6, understand God's choice for salvation as grounded in His grace rather than in faith or good works He foresees in a person. The Baptist confession explicitly cites Romans 9's account of the twins in support.

Wesley's notes on Romans 9:10–13 read the choice here as concerning the promised family line and the peoples descended from Jacob and Esau, rather than a declaration of each man's eternal fate. In his wider explanation, God's mercy receives believers from both Jews and Gentiles; ancestry does not secure that mercy, and faith is not a work that earns it.

The Catholic Church teaches that even the preparation to receive God's grace depends on His help, while His initiative calls for a free human response (Catechism 2001–2002). It also teaches that God predestines no one to hell (Catechism 1037). These are its broader teachings about grace and human response, rather than a detailed ruling on every part of this Genesis scene.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Romans 9:6–13 directly uses Rebekah's twins when answering whether God's word has failed. Paul draws attention to the announcement before their birth or any good or bad deeds. The promised future rests on God's calling rather than being a reward one of the unborn children has earned.

Paul's discussion goes on to people called from both Jews and Gentiles in Romans 9:24–33. He describes Gentiles receiving righteousness by faith and warns against pursuing it as though it came through works. The twins' story thus enters a larger explanation of God's faithfulness, mercy, and the people He gathers. Its reach is greater than the rivalry within one household, even though Christians differ over how all the parts of that explanation fit together.
