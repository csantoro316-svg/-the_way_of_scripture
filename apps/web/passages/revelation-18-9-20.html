Passage

Revelation 18:9-20

Why do the rulers and merchants mourn while heaven is called to rejoice?

Berean Standard Bible

9 Then the kings of the earth who committed sexual immorality and lived in luxury with her will weep and wail at the sight of the smoke rising from the fire that consumes her. 10 In fear of her torment, they will stand at a distance and cry out: “Woe, woe to the great city, the mighty city of Babylon! For in a single hour your judgment has come.”

11 And the merchants of the earth will weep and mourn over her, because there is no one left to buy their cargo— 12 cargo of gold, silver, precious stones, and pearls; of fine linen, purple, silk, and scarlet; of all kinds of citron wood and every article of ivory, precious wood, bronze, iron, and marble; 13 of cinnamon, spice, incense, myrrh, and frankincense; of wine, olive oil, fine flour, and wheat; of cattle, sheep, horses, and carriages; of bodies and souls of slaves. 14 And they will say: “The fruit of your soul’s desire has departed from you; all your luxury and splendor have vanished, never to be seen again.”

15 The merchants who sold these things and gained their wealth from her will stand at a distance, in fear of her torment. They will weep and mourn, 16 saying: “Woe, woe to the great city, clothed in fine linen and purple and scarlet, adorned with gold and precious stones and pearls! 17 For in a single hour such fabulous wealth has been destroyed!” Every shipmaster, passenger, and sailor, and all who make their living from the sea, will stand at a distance 18 and cry out at the sight of the smoke rising from the fire that consumes her. “What city was ever like this great city?” they will exclaim. 19 Then they will throw dust on their heads as they weep and mourn and cry out: “Woe, woe to the great city, where all who had ships on the sea were enriched by her wealth! For in a single hour she has been destroyed.”

20 Rejoice over her, O heaven, and you saints and apostles and prophets, because God has pronounced for you His judgment against her.

What is the context?

Three groups mourn Babylon: the kings who shared her luxury, the merchants who sold her goods, and those who profited from shipping. They stand at a distance because they fear her torment. The repeated cry about a single hour emphasizes how quickly the wealth they trusted has collapsed.

The cargo list moves through precious metals, jewels, fine fabrics, luxury materials, spices, food, animals and transport. It ends with the bodies and souls of slaves. Human beings have been included among the goods that sustain the city’s abundance.

The Greek word translated souls can refer to lives or persons. Thayer’s entry specifically connects its use here with enslaved people, as in Ezekiel 27:13. The list makes the human cost of Babylon’s commerce visible; its wealth includes people treated as merchandise.

The merchants’ stated reason for grief is that no one buys their cargo anymore. The sailors likewise lament the loss of the city by which they were enriched. Their cries look back to its former splendor and influence.

Heaven, the saints, apostles and prophets are then called to rejoice because God has judged on their behalf. The contrast is between mourning the loss of a profitable order and rejoicing that its victims have received justice.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The main explanations recognize the economic character of the lament. The USCCB notes emphasize its irony: the merchants mourn their lost market. Walvoord likewise understands the grief in terms of dependence on Babylon’s wealth and trade. The passage itself gives that reason rather than recording repentance for the city’s wrongdoing.

Wesley applies the merchandise to Rome’s luxury and religious commerce. Walvoord emphasizes a future political and economic order. These settings differ, while both explanations connect the apparent riches with a power that will face judgment.

The call to rejoice concerns God’s judgment for the saints, apostles and prophets. It marks His answer to the persecution and bloodshed described in the chapter.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The lament closely parallels Ezekiel 27:12–14 and 27–36. Tyre’s goods, trade in enslaved people, shipping and wealth are followed by the cries of seafarers and merchants at its downfall. Revelation reuses that pattern to expose how a wealthy power can draw many others into dependence on it.

The rejoicing also answers the martyrs’ appeal for justice in Revelation 6:9–11. The loss celebrated in heaven is the overthrow of a power responsible for harm, not the disappearance of ordinary work or trade as such. Within God’s larger purpose, His people’s suffering is neither invisible nor permanently unanswered.
