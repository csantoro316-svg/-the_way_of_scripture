Passage

Isaiah 13:1-22

What happens when Babylon’s greatness meets the Lord’s judgment?

Berean Standard Bible

1 This is the burden against Babylon that Isaiah son of Amoz received: 2 Raise a banner on a barren hilltop; call aloud to them. Wave your hand, that they may enter the gates of the nobles. 3 I have commanded My sanctified ones; I have even summoned My warriors to execute My wrath and exult in My triumph. 4 Listen, a tumult on the mountains, like that of a great multitude! Listen, an uproar among the kingdoms, like nations gathered together! The LORD of Hosts is mobilizing an army for war. 5 They are coming from faraway lands, from the ends of the heavens— the LORD and the weapons of His wrath— to destroy the whole country.

6 Wail, for the Day of the LORD is near; it will come as destruction from the Almighty. 7 Therefore all hands will fall limp, and every man’s heart will melt. 8 Terror, pain, and anguish will seize them; they will writhe like a woman in labor. They will look at one another, their faces flushed with fear. 9 Behold, the Day of the LORD is coming— cruel, with fury and burning anger— to make the earth a desolation and to destroy the sinners within it. 10 For the stars of heaven and their constellations will not give their light. The rising sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light. 11 I will punish the world for its evil and the wicked for their iniquity. I will end the haughtiness of the arrogant and lay low the pride of the ruthless. 12 I will make man scarcer than pure gold, and mankind rarer than the gold of Ophir. 13 Therefore I will make the heavens tremble, and the earth will be shaken from its place at the wrath of the LORD of Hosts on the day of His burning anger.

14 Like a hunted gazelle, like a sheep without a shepherd, each will return to his own people, each will flee to his native land. 15 Whoever is caught will be stabbed, and whoever is captured will die by the sword. 16 Their infants will be dashed to pieces before their eyes, their houses will be looted, and their wives will be ravished. 17 Behold, I will stir up against them the Medes, who have no regard for silver and no desire for gold. 18 Their bows will dash young men to pieces; they will have no mercy on the fruit of the womb; they will not look with pity on the children.

19 And Babylon, the jewel of the kingdoms, the glory of the pride of the Chaldeans, will be overthrown by God like Sodom and Gomorrah. 20 She will never be inhabited or settled from generation to generation; no nomad will pitch his tent there, no shepherd will rest his flock there. 21 But desert creatures will lie down there, and howling creatures will fill her houses. Ostriches will dwell there, and wild goats will leap about. 22 Hyenas will howl in her fortresses and jackals in her luxurious palaces. Babylon’s time is at hand, and her days will not be prolonged.

What is the context?

This chapter begins a series of messages concerning other nations. The first is identified as a burden against Babylon received by Isaiah son of Amoz. An army gathers at the Lord’s command, and the Day of the Lord is announced as a time of terror and destruction.

The language reaches from trembling hearts to darkened stars, sun and moon. God declares that He will punish evil, end arrogance and bring down the ruthless. What had seemed secure becomes a place from which people try to flee.

Verse 17 names the Medes as attackers. Wealth will not secure safety, and the description of the assault includes the killing of children and sexual violence. These are part of the horror of the announced judgment, not scenes of a clean or painless transfer of power.

The closing picture reverses Babylon’s splendor. The jewel of kingdoms becomes like Sodom and Gomorrah, with deserted houses and palaces occupied by wild creatures. Human pride cannot preserve what the Lord has marked for destruction.

For the historical background, Cyrus captured Babylon in 539 BC. The Metropolitan Museum’s account of the Cyrus Cylinder describes the capture and the establishment of Persian rule. The cylinder also records building work in Babylon, so the capture of the city and its complete desolation should not be treated as the same event without further explanation.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry, Wesley and Gill connect the chapter with Babylon’s defeat by the Medes and Persians. They understand the darkened heavens as prophetic pictures of overwhelming disaster. Gill explains the final desolation as something completed over time rather than all occurring at the moment of capture.

A future reading places greater weight on the worldwide judgment and permanent desolation. In discussing Revelation 18, Walvoord presents the reading that Babylon must be rebuilt and finally destroyed at Christ’s return, and finds that explanation preferable in several respects. He also acknowledges readings in which Babylon represents a wider political and religious power. These are differences among interpreters, rather than a single official position shared by all Christians.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus uses Isaiah’s language about the darkened sun and moon when speaking about the coming of the Son of Man (Matthew 24:29–31; Mark 13:24–27). The New Testament carries this language of judgment into its account of His coming and the gathering of His people.

Revelation 18:1–8, 20–24 also takes up Babylon’s fall, its desolation and its connection with the blood of God’s people. God calls His people away from sharing in its sins and judges the power that has enriched itself through evil.

These connections show that Babylon’s judgment belongs to Scripture’s larger account of God ending proud, destructive rule. The New Testament use of the images does not, by itself, settle every question about the timing or location of Isaiah’s fulfillment.
