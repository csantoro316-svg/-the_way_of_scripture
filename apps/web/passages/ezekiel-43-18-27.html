Passage

Ezekiel 43:18-27

Why must the altar be cleansed before regular offerings begin?

Berean Standard Bible

18 Then He said to me: “Son of man, this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘These are the statutes for the altar on the day it is constructed, so that burnt offerings may be sacrificed on it and blood may be splattered on it: 19 You are to give a young bull from the herd as a sin offering to the Levitical priests who are of the family of Zadok, who approach Me to minister before Me, declares the Lord GOD. 20 You are to take some of its blood and put it on the four horns of the altar, on the four corners of the ledge, and all around the rim; thus you will cleanse the altar and make atonement for it. 21 Then you are to take away the bull for the sin offering and burn it in the appointed part of the temple area outside the sanctuary.

22 On the second day you are to present an unblemished male goat as a sin offering, and the altar is to be cleansed as it was with the bull. 23 When you have finished the purification, you are to present a young, unblemished bull and an unblemished ram from the flock. 24 You must present them before the LORD; the priests are to sprinkle salt on them and sacrifice them as a burnt offering to the LORD.

25 For seven days you are to provide a male goat daily for a sin offering; you are also to provide a young bull and a ram from the flock, both unblemished. 26 For seven days the priests are to make atonement for the altar and cleanse it; so they shall consecrate it. 27 At the end of these days, from the eighth day on, the priests are to present your burnt offerings and peace offerings on the altar. Then I will accept you, declares the Lord GOD.’”

What is the context?

The Lord gives instructions for the altar’s first use. The priests from Zadok’s family receive a bull for a sin offering. Its blood is applied to the horns, corners and rim to cleanse the altar and make atonement for it. The bull is burned in the appointed place outside the sanctuary.

A male goat is presented on the second day, and further unblemished animals are offered. The priests sprinkle salt on the burnt offerings. Seven days are devoted to cleansing and setting the altar apart.

From the eighth day onward, the priests present the people’s burnt offerings and peace offerings, with the Lord’s assurance that He will accept them. The preparation teaches that approaching Him and serving Him are not casual matters.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Cambridge explains the blood applied to the altar as its purification and atonement. Wesley emphasizes God’s acceptance of those who give themselves to Him, through the mediator.

Henry applies the passage to Christian approach to God through Christ’s blood and explicitly rejects the need for new atoning sacrifices now. This differs from reading the final vision as instructions for a future physical sanctuary, while keeping attention on acceptance before God.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

A related pattern appears in Hebrews 13:10–16. The writer recalls the bodies of sin offerings being burned outside the camp, then connects this with Jesus suffering outside the city gate to sanctify His people by His own blood.

This is a connection through the wider sacrificial pattern; Hebrews does not identify Ezekiel’s altar ceremony as its quoted source. It leads to a further response: going to Jesus despite the disgrace, offering praise through Him, doing good and sharing with others. The goal of being brought near to God includes lives of faithful worship and care for people.
