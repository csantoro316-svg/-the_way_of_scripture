Passage

1 Samuel 15:10-23

Can a sacrifice make up for refusing to listen?

Berean Standard Bible

10 Then the word of the LORD came to Samuel, saying, 11 “I regret that I have made Saul king, for he has turned away from following Me and has not carried out My instructions.” And Samuel was distressed and cried out to the LORD all that night. 12 Early in the morning Samuel got up to confront Saul, but he was told, “Saul has gone to Carmel, and behold, he has set up a monument for himself and has turned and gone down to Gilgal.” 13 When Samuel reached him, Saul said to him, “May the LORD bless you. I have carried out the LORD’s instructions.” 14 But Samuel replied, “Then what is this bleating of sheep and lowing of cattle that I hear?” 15 Saul answered, “The troops brought them from the Amalekites; they spared the best sheep and cattle to sacrifice to the LORD your God, but the rest we devoted to destruction.” 16 “Stop!” exclaimed Samuel. “Let me tell you what the LORD said to me last night.” “Tell me,” Saul replied.

17 And Samuel said, “Although you were once small in your own eyes, have you not become the head of the tribes of Israel? The LORD anointed you king over Israel 18 and sent you on a mission, saying, ‘Go and devote to destruction the sinful Amalekites. Fight against them until you have wiped them out.’ 19 So why did you not obey the LORD? Why did you rush upon the plunder and do evil in the sight of the LORD?” 20 “But I did obey the LORD,” Saul replied. “I went on the mission that the LORD gave me. I brought back Agag king of Amalek and devoted the Amalekites to destruction. 21 The troops took sheep and cattle from the plunder, the best of the things devoted to destruction, in order to sacrifice them to the LORD your God at Gilgal.” 22 But Samuel declared: “Does the LORD delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices as much as in obedience to His voice? Behold, obedience is better than sacrifice, and attentiveness is better than the fat of rams. 23 For rebellion is like the sin of divination, and arrogance is like the wickedness of idolatry. Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, He has rejected you as king.”

What is the context?

The LORD tells Samuel that He regrets making Saul king because Saul has turned from following Him. Samuel is deeply distressed and cries to God through the night. Meanwhile, Saul has set up a monument for himself and gone to Gilgal.

Saul greets Samuel by claiming to have carried out the command. The sound of the surviving animals exposes the gap between that claim and what happened. Saul says the troops kept them for sacrifice. Samuel brings the issue back to the instruction Saul received and asks why he rushed upon the plunder.

Samuel’s answer sets obedience above sacrifice. The animals are not an acceptable way of compensating for rejecting God’s word. Rebellion and arrogance are compared with divination and idolatry, and Saul is told that his rejection of the word has brought rejection from kingship.

The Hebrew נָחַם, nacham (nah-KHAHM), can express being sorry, grieving or relenting; Brown–Driver–Briggs lists the uses in verses 11, 29 and 35. The chapter speaks of God’s regret while also saying He does not change His mind like a human being. Both statements belong to the story and need to be considered together.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley, Henry and Gill understand God’s regret through the change in His dealings with Saul, rather than God discovering information He did not know. Saul has turned away, and the royal favor he received will not continue in the same way. This reading preserves the seriousness of the response without making God an unreliable human ruler.

On sacrifice, Wesley and Henry emphasize that a religious offering cannot compensate for knowingly refusing the command. Their reading does not treat every sacrifice as worthless. It challenges the attempt to use a worshipful act as a substitute for listening.

The USCCB study notes likewise connect worship with attentiveness to God’s will. In this account Saul’s proposed offering is inseparable from the animals he was told not to keep. Calling the result religious does not change the choice that produced it.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The relationship between obedience and sacrifice reaches a fuller expression in Hebrews 10:5–10. Drawing from the Psalms, Hebrews describes Christ coming to do God’s will and offering His own body once for all.

Hebrews is not quoting Samuel’s words here, and Jesus’ offering is not another example of Saul’s failed proposal. It brings obedience and sacrifice together: Christ gives Himself in doing the Father’s will. The larger story points toward a saving offering that fully expresses faithfulness, rather than an offering used to cover refusal.
