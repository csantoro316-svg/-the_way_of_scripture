Passage

Luke 5:1-11

Why do the fishermen leave everything to follow Jesus?

Berean Standard Bible

1 On one occasion, while Jesus was standing by the Lake of Gennesaret with the crowd pressing in on Him to hear the word of God, 2 He saw two boats at the edge of the lake. The fishermen had left them and were washing their nets. 3 Jesus got into the boat belonging to Simon and asked him to put out a little from shore. And sitting down, He taught the people from the boat.

4 When Jesus had finished speaking, He said to Simon, “Put out into deep water and let down your nets for a catch.” 5 “Master,” Simon replied, “we have worked hard all night without catching anything. But because You say so, I will let down the nets.” 6 When they had done so, they caught such a large number of fish that their nets began to tear. 7 So they signaled to their partners in the other boat to come and help them, and they came and filled both boats so full that they began to sink.

8 When Simon Peter saw this, he fell at Jesus’ knees. “Go away from me, Lord,” he said, “for I am a sinful man.” 9 For he and his companions were astonished at the catch of fish they had taken, 10 and so were his partners James and John, the sons of Zebedee. “Do not be afraid,” Jesus said to Simon. “From now on you will catch men.” 11 And when they had brought their boats ashore, they left everything and followed Him.

What is the context?

The crowd is pressing around Jesus to hear God’s word. He steps into Simon’s boat, asks him to move a little from shore, and teaches from there. Simon is already known to the reader: Jesus has visited his home and healed his mother-in-law. (Luke 4:38–39)

After teaching, Jesus tells Simon to go into deep water and let down the nets. Simon explains that they have worked all night without a catch, but he acts on Jesus’ word. The catch is so large that the nets begin to tear and both boats begin to sink as the partners help bring it in.

Simon’s response turns attention from the fish to the One before him. He falls at Jesus’ knees and confesses his sinfulness. Jesus answers his fear with a new calling. James and John are named among the astonished partners, and the account ends with the fishermen leaving everything to follow Him.

The Greek verb behind catching people is ζωγρέω, zōgreō (dzoh-GREH-oh). It can mean to take alive; Thayer explains its use here as winning people for God’s kingdom through teaching. The fishing picture now points toward people being gathered through the message Jesus will send His followers to carry. (Thayer, G2221)

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly understand the catch as revealing Jesus’ power and the fishermen’s response as trust that changes the direction of their lives. The call prepares them to bring others to Him; Calvin also distinguishes this particular call to leave their work from a command that every believer must abandon an occupation. (Calvin on Luke 5:1–11; Wesley on Luke 5:8–11)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

There is a helpful parallel with Isaiah’s call. Faced with God’s holiness, Isaiah confesses his unclean lips; after receiving cleansing, he is sent to speak. Peter likewise recognizes his sinfulness in Jesus’ presence, then receives reassurance and a calling. Luke does not say Isaiah predicted this fishing scene, but the parallel shows God calling people who know their need for His mercy. (Isaiah 6:1–9)

Acts shows the outward reach of this calling. Peter proclaims the risen Jesus, calls his hearers to repent, and about three thousand receive the message and are added to the believers. The promised work of gathering people is taking shape through the gospel. A later catch in John also forms a striking parallel: after another empty night, Jesus provides fish and renews Peter’s calling to care for His people. These are distinct scenes, joined by dependence on Jesus and a commission to serve others. (Acts 2:37–42; John 21:1–19)
