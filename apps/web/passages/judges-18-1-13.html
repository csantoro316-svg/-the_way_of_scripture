Passage

Judges 18:1-13

Is a reassuring answer enough to show that God approves?

Berean Standard Bible

1 In those days there was no king in Israel, and the tribe of the Danites was looking for territory to occupy. For up to that time they had not come into an inheritance among the tribes of Israel. 2 So the Danites sent out five men from their clans, men of valor from Zorah and Eshtaol, to spy out the land and explore it. “Go and explore the land,” they told them. The men entered the hill country of Ephraim and came to the house of Micah, where they spent the night. 3 And while they were near Micah’s house, they recognized the voice of the young Levite; so they went over and asked him, “Who brought you here? What are you doing in this place? Why are you here?” 4 “Micah has done this and that for me,” he replied, “and he has hired me to be his priest.” 5 Then they said to him, “Please inquire of God to determine whether we will have a successful journey.” 6 And the priest told them, “Go in peace. The LORD is watching over your journey.”

7 So the five men departed and came to Laish, where they saw that the people were living securely, like the Sidonians, quiet and unsuspecting. There was nothing lacking in the land and no oppressive ruler. And they were far away from the Sidonians and had no alliance with anyone. 8 When the men returned to Zorah and Eshtaol, their brothers asked them, “What did you find?” 9 They answered, “Come on, let us go up against them, for we have seen the land, and it is very good. Why would you fail to act? Do not hesitate to go there and take possession of the land! 10 When you enter, you will come to an unsuspecting people and a spacious land, for God has delivered it into your hand. It is a place where nothing on earth is lacking.”

11 So six hundred Danites departed from Zorah and Eshtaol, armed with weapons of war. 12 They went up and camped at Kiriath-jearim in Judah. That is why the place west of Kiriath-jearim is called Mahaneh-dan to this day. 13 And from there they traveled to the hill country of Ephraim and came to Micah’s house.

What is the context?

With no king in Israel, a group of Danites searches for territory to occupy. Joshua 19:40–48 has already listed Dan’s allotted territory and records the later move against Leshem. Judges 1:34–35 describes the Amorites pressing Dan into the hill country. The search here belongs to that difficulty in securing a place to live.

Five scouts from Zorah and Eshtaol spend the night near Micah’s house and recognize the young Levite’s voice. After hearing about his employment, they ask him to inquire of God about their journey. He sends them off with reassurance that the LORD is watching over it. The account reports his answer without describing a message from God or how he obtained it.

At Laish they find people living quietly and unsuspectingly in a well-supplied land. The people are far from Sidon and have no alliance to bring help. The scouts return enthusiastically, urging immediate action and saying God has delivered the land into their hands. Six hundred armed Danites then set out and return by way of Micah’s house.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley distinguishes an inheritance allotted to Dan from territory the tribe has actually secured. He also explains the recognized voice through previous acquaintance. Gill gives a similar explanation, while allowing that a distinctive manner of speaking may have helped. The passage does not require a claim that all Levites had a separate accent.

Both commentators question the reliability of the priest’s reassurance. Wesley allows either an answer invented to please the visitors or a deceptive spiritual source; Gill discusses the same possibilities and notes how broadly the words could be understood. These are their explanations of an answer whose source the narrator does not disclose.

The scouts then combine that reassurance with what looks like an easy opportunity. Wesley sees their confidence as resting partly on the supposed promise and partly on the people’s vulnerability. Their conclusion that God has given them the place remains their reported conclusion.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The wider biblical call is to test spiritual claims rather than accept them merely because they reassure us. First John 4:1–6 makes confession of Jesus Christ come in the flesh central to discerning truth from deception. The question is whether a message is truly from God, not simply whether it promises what the hearer wants.

First Thessalonians 5:19–24 brings testing together with openness to the Spirit: examine what is said, hold to the good, and reject evil. It also grounds hope in the faithful God who prepares His people for Jesus’ coming. Discernment serves that larger purpose of a people made holy for Him.
