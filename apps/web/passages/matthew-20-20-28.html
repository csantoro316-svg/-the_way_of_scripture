Passage

Matthew 20:20-28

Greatness Through Service and the Son’s Ransom

Berean Standard Bible

20 Then the mother of Zebedee’s sons came to Jesus with her sons and knelt down to make a request of Him. 21 “What do you want?” He inquired. She answered, “Declare that in Your kingdom one of these two sons of mine may sit at Your right hand, and the other at Your left.” 22 “You do not know what you are asking,” Jesus replied. “Can you drink the cup I am going to drink?” “We can,” the brothers answered. 23 “You will indeed drink My cup,” Jesus said. “But to sit at My right or left is not Mine to grant. These seats belong to those for whom My Father has prepared them.” 24 When the ten heard about this, they were indignant with the two brothers.

25 But Jesus called them aside and said, “You know that the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their superiors exercise authority over them. 26 It shall not be this way among you. Instead, whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, 27 and whoever wants to be first among you must be your slave— 28 just as the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many.”

What is the context?

Immediately after Jesus describes His coming suffering, the mother of Zebedee's sons asks for her sons to sit at His right and left in the kingdom. The brothers say they can drink His cup. Jesus tells them they will share that cup, but the places of honor belong to those for whom His Father has prepared them. The other ten are indignant.

Mark 10:35–45 names the brothers as James and John and records their request directly. Both accounts connect the request with Jesus' coming suffering and His teaching about service.

Jesus contrasts rulers who dominate others with the life He expects among His followers. Servant is διάκονος, diakonos (dee-AH-koh-noss), someone who serves or attends to another. Thayer's entry includes this verse and the related saying in Mark. Slave is δοῦλος, doulos (DOOH-loss); Thayer explains its use here as devoting oneself to another's good. Greatness is expressed in giving oneself to serve.

Jesus then gives His own mission as the example. He came to serve and give His life as a ransom for many. Ransom is λύτρον, lytron (LOOH-tron), the price of release. Thayer's entry connects this use with freeing people from the misery and penalty of sin. The coming death He has announced is also His act of service for others.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly understand the cup as suffering and Jesus' instruction as a call to humble service. The US Catholic bishops' notes on 20:20–28 place His saving death at the height of that service; Wesley on 20:22–26 likewise connects the cup with suffering and greatness with serving.

Christians differ over whom Jesus means by many. The Catholic Church teaches that many here contrasts the one Redeemer with humanity as a whole, and that Christ died for everyone without exception (Catechism, 605). The Methodist Articles, XX, also describe His sacrifice as being for the sins of the whole world.

The Reformed Westminster Confession, 8.5 and 8.8, and 1689 Baptist Confession, 8.5 and 8.8, teach that Christ's death secures salvation for the people the Father has chosen and given Him. Christ brings them to faith and obedience and gives them the lasting inheritance He won for them. The shared conviction across these traditions is that His self-giving death brings rescue from sin.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus' language has a strong connection with Isaiah 53:10–12. There the Servant gives His life, bears the sins of many, and brings them into a right relationship with God. That is a strong scriptural parallel to the service Jesus describes in giving His life for others. 1 Timothy 2:3–6 also joins His role as the mediator between God and people with His giving Himself as a ransom.

His saving service also shapes the community He gathers. Philippians 2:3–11 calls believers to consider others and points to Christ's humility and obedience to death. 1 Peter 5:1–4 calls church leaders to care for the flock willingly, by example, as they look toward the Chief Shepherd's appearing. The One who gives His life teaches His people how to care for one another.
