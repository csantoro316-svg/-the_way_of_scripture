Passage

Exodus 21:12-36

How did the law deal with violence and responsibility?

Berean Standard Bible

12 Whoever strikes and kills a man must surely be put to death. 13 If, however, he did not lie in wait, but God allowed it to happen, then I will appoint for you a place where he may flee. 14 But if a man schemes and acts willfully against his neighbor to kill him, you must take him away from My altar to be put to death. 15 Whoever strikes his father or mother must surely be put to death. 16 Whoever kidnaps another man must be put to death, whether he sells him or the man is found in his possession. 17 Anyone who curses his father or mother must surely be put to death.

18 If men are quarreling and one strikes the other with a stone or a fist, and he does not die but is confined to bed, 19 then the one who struck him shall go unpunished, as long as the other can get up and walk around outside with his staff. Nevertheless, he must compensate the man for his lost work and see that he is completely healed. 20 If a man strikes his manservant or maidservant with a rod, and the servant dies by his hand, he shall surely be punished. 21 However, if the servant gets up after a day or two, the owner shall not be punished, since the servant is his property.

22 If men who are fighting strike a pregnant woman and her child is born prematurely, but there is no further injury, he shall surely be fined as the woman’s husband demands and as the court allows. 23 But if a serious injury results, then you must require a life for a life— 24 eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot, 25 burn for burn, wound for wound, and stripe for stripe. 26 If a man strikes and blinds the eye of his manservant or maidservant, he must let the servant go free as compensation for the eye. 27 And if he knocks out the tooth of his manservant or maidservant, he must let the servant go free as compensation for the tooth.

28 If an ox gores a man or woman to death, the ox must surely be stoned, and its meat must not be eaten. But the owner of the ox shall not be held responsible. 29 But if the ox has a habit of goring, and its owner has been warned yet does not restrain it, and it kills a man or woman, then the ox must be stoned and its owner must also be put to death. 30 If payment is demanded of him instead, he may redeem his life by paying the full amount demanded of him. 31 If the ox gores a son or a daughter, it shall be done to him according to the same rule. 32 If the ox gores a manservant or maidservant, the owner must pay thirty shekels of silver to the master of that servant, and the ox must be stoned.

33 If a man opens or digs a pit and fails to cover it, and an ox or a donkey falls into it, 34 the owner of the pit shall make restitution; he must pay its owner, and the dead animal will be his. 35 If a man’s ox injures his neighbor’s ox and it dies, they must sell the live one and divide the proceeds; they also must divide the dead animal. 36 But if it was known that the ox had a habit of goring, yet its owner failed to restrain it, he shall pay full compensation, ox for ox, and the dead animal will be his.

What is the context?

These cases distinguish deliberate violence from an unintended death, and an unexpected accident from a danger someone knowingly failed to prevent. A planned killing cannot be hidden behind religious protection: even the altar is no shelter for the murderer. Striking or cursing parents and kidnapping another person also carry severe penalties.

When an injured person recovers enough to walk, the person who struck him must pay for lost time and healing. The rules for enslaved people are different. An owner who beats a servant to death is subject to punishment; verse 21 withholds that punishment if the servant gets up after a day or two, giving the owner’s property interest as the reason. But destroying a servant’s eye or knocking out a tooth requires the servant’s release. The passage limits a master’s power while still treating the servant as property. Both features need to be faced honestly.

Verses 22–25 address a pregnant woman injured when men fight. The BSB describes a premature birth: if there is no further injury, a court-approved fine is paid; serious injury brings the corresponding penalty. The following eye-for-eye language sets the consequence in relation to the harm done. How this case is understood has been an important point of disagreement.

The animal laws also turn on responsibility. An ox that kills a person must be killed. If its owner knew it was dangerous and failed to restrain it, the owner can also face death, though a ransom may be permitted in that case. A servant’s death brings a fixed payment of thirty shekels to the master. Uncovered pits and injuries between animals require repayment or shared loss, with greater liability when a known danger was neglected. The ransom allowed for the negligent ox owner should not be extended to deliberate murder; Numbers 35:31 expressly forbids accepting a ransom for a murderer.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The distinction between intentional killing, accidental death and preventable harm is widely recognized. Wesley reads the injury penalties as the work of the courts, not permission for an injured person to take revenge. On refuge for an unintended killing, Numbers 35:22–28 later explains both protection from the avenger and release after the high priest’s death; refuge is not described there as imprisonment that can never end.

On the pregnancy case, Calvin understands the serious harm in verses 23–25 to include harm to either the mother or her child. Gill also allows both to be included. In contrast, Rashi understands the fine as payment for the loss of the offspring and the further harm as harm to the woman. These are different readings of what happened and whose injury the next penalty concerns. They should not be presented as a single agreed interpretation, or as every member of a modern denomination speaking with one voice.

There is also a difference over how injury was to be repaid. Calvin describes the corresponding loss of an eye or limb as a judicial penalty, while also speaking of proportionate compensation. The Jewish Mishnah, Bava Kamma 8:1, explains bodily injury through payments for damage, pain, medical care, lost livelihood and humiliation. Its treatment shows that the Jewish legal reading cannot simply be reduced to physically repeating each injury.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Jesus explicitly returns to the eye-for-eye wording in Matthew 5:38–48. He teaches His followers to give up retaliation, respond generously and love their enemies, including praying for those who persecute them. The Father’s goodness toward both the righteous and the unrighteous becomes the pattern for their conduct. This carries the discussion beyond calculating what an offender owes: Jesus calls for people whose response to wrongdoing reflects their Father.

There is another, more sobering connection. Gill draws a parallel between the thirty shekels paid for a servant’s death here and the thirty silver pieces offered for Jesus’ betrayal in Matthew 26:14–16. Matthew does not identify Exodus 21 as a prediction being fulfilled, so this is a parallel rather than an explicit prophecy. Alongside it, Philippians 2:5–11 describes the Son taking a servant’s place and humbling Himself to death, followed by God’s exaltation of Him. The one treated so cheaply is the Lord before whom every knee will bow.
