Passage

Galatians 6:11-16

What counts when we belong to Christ?

Berean Standard Bible

11 See what large letters I am using to write to you with my own hand! 12 Those who want to make a good impression outwardly are trying to compel you to be circumcised. They only do this to avoid persecution for the cross of Christ. 13 For the circumcised do not even keep the law themselves, yet they want you to be circumcised that they may boast in your flesh.

14 But as for me, may I never boast, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world. 15 For neither circumcision nor uncircumcision means anything. What counts is a new creation. 16 Peace and mercy to all who walk by this rule, even to the Israel of God.

What is the context?

Paul calls attention to the large letters he is writing with his own hand. The personal note leads directly into his final contrast between the motives of those pressing circumcision and the message he has defended.

He says the teachers want an outwardly impressive result and are avoiding persecution for the cross of Christ. They do not keep the law themselves, yet want to boast about the Galatians’ circumcision.

Paul’s own boast is in the cross of the Lord Jesus Christ. Through it, the world has been crucified to him and he to the world. The relationship that matters is now centered on Christ and His self-giving.

Neither circumcision nor uncircumcision is decisive. What counts is a new creation. Paul pronounces peace and mercy on those who walk by this rule, using the expression the Israel of God.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly see the cross and new creation at the center of Paul’s closing appeal. Wesley’s notes on verses 11–16 and the USCCB notes on verses 11–16 connect this conclusion with the letter’s earlier warning against making circumcision the basis of belonging.

The expression the Israel of God is read in different ways. Wesley’s note on verse 16 identifies it with the church made up of people from every nation who follow this rule. The Catholic USCCB note on verse 16 allows the church as a possibility and also allows a reference to God’s ancient people, Israel. Its note recognizes the range of readings rather than defining one as the only Catholic position.

A dispensational reading, which keeps Israel and the church distinct, is explained in a faculty publication from Shepherds Theological Seminary. It understands the expression as Jewish believers within the churches, alongside Gentile believers who also follow Christ. This is the reading defended in that publication, rather than a statement that every evangelical Christian reads the verse that way.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

2 Corinthians 5:14–21 connects Christ’s death with a changed direction of life: those who live are to live for Him. It also explicitly describes anyone in Christ as a new creation and places that change within God’s work of reconciliation.

This connection brings the closing emphasis of Galatians into a larger picture. The cross leads to a people whose identity and way of life have been renewed through Christ. The new creation He brings is central to the peace and mercy Paul pronounces.
