Passage

Isaiah 20:1-6

What does Isaiah’s unsettling sign expose about misplaced hope?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Before the year that the chief commander, sent by Sargon king of Assyria, came to Ashdod and attacked and captured it, 2 the LORD had already spoken through Isaiah son of Amoz, saying, “Go, remove the sackcloth from your waist and the sandals from your feet.” And Isaiah did so, walking around naked and barefoot. 3 Then the LORD said, “Just as My servant Isaiah has gone naked and barefoot for three years as a sign and omen against Egypt and Cush, 4 so the king of Assyria will lead away the captives of Egypt and the exiles of Cush, young and old alike, naked and barefoot, with bared buttocks—to Egypt’s shame.

5 Those who made Cush their hope and Egypt their boast will be dismayed and ashamed. 6 And on that day the dwellers of this coastland will say, ‘See what has happened to our source of hope, those to whom we fled for help and deliverance from the king of Assyria! How then can we escape?’”

What is the context?

The chapter connects Isaiah’s sign with the Assyrian capture of Ashdod by a commander sent by Sargon. The Lord had told Isaiah to remove his sackcloth and sandals, and Isaiah obeyed, walking naked and barefoot.

The Lord explains the sign in terms of Egypt and Cush. As Isaiah has gone exposed and barefoot for three years, Assyria will lead away their captives, young and old, in humiliating exposure. The description includes bared buttocks and Egypt’s shame.

The final verses address those who had made Cush their hope and Egypt their boast. The failure of the powers they trusted leaves them asking how they themselves can escape.

The sign therefore confronts more than Egypt’s coming humiliation. It makes the people watching reconsider the strength on which they have based their own safety.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry and Wesley understand Isaiah’s exposure as going without his outer garment rather than complete nakedness. They read the action as a visible representation of the shame of captives. This explanation should not hide the severity of verse 4, which explicitly describes the prisoners’ uncovered bodies.

Gill discusses different ways of relating the three years to the sign and the coming judgment. The BSB attaches the period to Isaiah’s action. Across these readings, the main warning remains the same: Egypt and Cush cannot be a secure refuge if they themselves are being led into captivity.
