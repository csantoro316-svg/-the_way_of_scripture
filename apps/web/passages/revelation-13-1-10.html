Passage

Revelation 13:1-10

Why does the beast’s power lead people into false worship?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I saw a beast with ten horns and seven heads rising out of the sea. There were ten royal crowns on its horns and blasphemous names on its heads. 2 The beast I saw was like a leopard, with the feet of a bear and the mouth of a lion. And the dragon gave the beast his power and his throne and great authority. 3 One of the heads of the beast appeared to have been mortally wounded. But the mortal wound was healed, and the whole world marveled and followed the beast. 4 They worshiped the dragon who had given authority to the beast, and they worshiped the beast, saying, “Who is like the beast, and who can wage war against it?”

5 The beast was given a mouth to speak arrogant and blasphemous words, and authority to act for 42 months. 6 And the beast opened its mouth to speak blasphemies against God and to slander His name and His tabernacle—those who dwell in heaven. 7 Then the beast was permitted to wage war against the saints and to conquer them, and it was given authority over every tribe and people and tongue and nation. 8 And all who dwell on the earth will worship the beast—all whose names have not been written from the foundation of the world in the Book of Life belonging to the Lamb who was slain.

9 He who has an ear, let him hear: 10 “If anyone is destined for captivity, into captivity he will go; if anyone is to die by the sword, by the sword he must be killed.” Here is a call for the perseverance and faith of the saints.

What is the context?

The dragon’s hostility now takes shape through a beast rising from the sea. It has seven heads, ten horns and royal crowns, with names that dishonor God. Its body combines a leopard, a bear and a lion. These features recall the beasts in Daniel 7, where violent kingdoms oppose God’s people.

The dragon gives the beast power, a throne and authority. One head appears mortally wounded, yet the wound is healed. The world marvels and follows the beast. Admiration becomes worship of both the beast and the dragon behind it, expressed in the question of who could possibly defeat such a power.

The beast’s character appears in what it says and does. It speaks arrogantly against God, slanders His name and dwelling, and wages war on the saints. Its authority reaches across peoples and nations, but its stated period of action is forty-two months. The vision describes a fearsome power within an appointed limit.

Those who worship it are contrasted with those whose names are written in the Lamb’s Book of Life. The slain Lamb remains the point of belonging for God’s people, even when the beast appears successful against them. The closing call is to hear and endure faithfully through captivity and death.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The main differences concern the beast’s identity and timing. The USCCB notes understand the first beast as the ancient Roman Empire and connect its heads with emperors. Wesley, reading the vision through later church history, identifies it with papal political power. These are different historical explanations, rather than a single shared identification of Rome.

Walvoord expects a future ruler and revived Roman Empire. He favors understanding the healed wound as the empire’s recovery. David Guzik instead favors a ruler who suffers a mortal wound and is healed, presenting an imitation of Christ’s death and resurrection. Thus even readers expecting a future fulfillment differ over the wound.

The final warning concerns perseverance under oppression. Older commentaries sometimes discuss wording about the captor becoming a captive or the killer being killed. The BSB quoted here speaks of those destined for captivity or death. That wording matters when comparing an explanation about judgment on persecutors with one about the endurance of persecuted believers.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Daniel 7:1–8 and 21–27 provides a strong scriptural parallel: beasts, horns, arrogant speech and war against the saints. Daniel’s vision also gives the outcome. The hostile power loses its dominion, and God gives an enduring kingdom to His people. Revelation’s beast should be read against that larger promise, rather than as a power capable of overthrowing God’s purpose.

There is also a contrast with the Lamb in Revelation 5:6–10. The Lamb was slain and stands before God, receiving praise because He purchased people for God. The beast’s healed wound attracts a different worship, directed toward the dragon. This is a literary parallel and contrast: impressive power must be judged by the one it serves and the worship it demands.
