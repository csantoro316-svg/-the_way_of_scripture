Passage

Exodus 5:1-5

Why did Pharaoh refuse to listen?

Berean Standard Bible

1 After that, Moses and Aaron went to Pharaoh and said, “This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: ‘Let My people go, so that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness.’” 2 But Pharaoh replied, “Who is the LORD that I should obey His voice and let Israel go? I do not know the LORD, and I will not let Israel go.” 3 “The God of the Hebrews has met with us,” they answered. “Please let us go on a three-day journey into the wilderness to sacrifice to the LORD our God, or He may strike us with plagues or with the sword.” 4 But the king of Egypt said to them, “Moses and Aaron, why do you draw the people away from their work? Get back to your labor!” 5 Pharaoh also said, “Look, the people of the land are now numerous, and you would be stopping them from their labor.”

What is the context?

After Israel’s believing response, Moses and Aaron deliver God’s demand to Pharaoh: release His people so they can hold a feast to Him in the wilderness. The Hebrew verb for holding a feast, hagag, describes a religious festival; Brown–Driver–Briggs includes this verse under keeping a pilgrim feast. Worship is at the heart of the request.

Pharaoh challenges both the LORD’s identity and His authority. He says he does not know the LORD and will not let Israel go. Moses and Aaron explain that the God of the Hebrews has met with them and ask for a three-day journey to offer sacrifices, warning that failure to worship Him could bring judgment upon them.

Pharaoh treats the request as an interruption of labor. Where God calls Israel His people, Pharaoh sees workers whose time he controls. The confrontation is beginning to show whose claim upon them he will recognize.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The shared reading understands Pharaoh’s reply as a refusal to recognize the LORD’s authority. Wesley and the Catholic USCCB notes both emphasize his defiance: whatever he may have heard about Israel’s God, he rejects the demand to obey Him.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

This confrontation begins a recurring biblical theme: what happens when a ruler’s command conflicts with obedience to God? In Acts 4:18–20, Peter and John are ordered to stop speaking in Jesus’ name and answer that they must heed God. In Acts 5:27–29, the apostles state that obligation directly.

The settings and tasks differ, but the parallel is clear. God’s purpose continues through people who must answer to Him while facing an authority that resists His word. Moses and Aaron’s commission to lead Israel into worship belongs within that wider story of God gathering a people who serve Him.
