Passage

Revelation 9:13-21

Why do the survivors still refuse to turn from their sins?

Berean Standard Bible

13 Then the sixth angel sounded his trumpet, and I heard a voice from the four horns of the golden altar before God 14 saying to the sixth angel with the trumpet, “Release the four angels who are bound at the great river Euphrates.” 15 So the four angels who had been prepared for this hour and day and month and year were released to kill a third of mankind. 16 And the number of mounted troops was two hundred million; I heard their number.

17 Now the horses and riders in my vision looked like this: The riders had breastplates the colors of fire, sapphire, and sulfur. The heads of the horses were like the heads of lions, and out of their mouths proceeded fire, smoke, and sulfur. 18 A third of mankind was killed by the three plagues of fire, smoke, and sulfur that proceeded from their mouths. 19 For the power of the horses was in their mouths and in their tails; indeed, their tails were like snakes, having heads with which to inflict harm.

20 Now the rest of mankind who were not killed by these plagues still did not repent of the works of their hands. They did not stop worshiping demons and idols of gold, silver, bronze, stone, and wood, which cannot see or hear or walk. 21 Furthermore, they did not repent of their murder, sorcery, sexual immorality, and theft.

What is the context?

The sixth trumpet brings a voice from the golden altar before God. Four angels bound at the Euphrates are released at the appointed hour, day, month and year. Their release leads to the death of a third of mankind. The scene returns to the altar already associated with the prayers of God’s people in chapter 8.

John hears the number of the mounted troops: two hundred million. He then describes riders with fiery red, dark blue and yellow breastplates, and horses with lion-like heads. Fire, smoke and sulfur come from their mouths. Their serpent-like tails also do harm. The vision presents destructive power from both the front and the rear.

The movement from the fifth trumpet to the sixth is a serious escalation. The locusts were forbidden to kill; this army does kill. The passage identifies the deadly agents as the fire, smoke and sulfur, but it does not directly identify a modern nation, weapon or military technology.

The final verses turn attention from the army to the survivors. They continue worshiping demons and lifeless idols, and they refuse to turn from murder, sorcery, sexual immorality and theft. The chapter ends with the condition of their hearts. Surviving a judgment has not brought them to abandon what is wrong.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Historical readings connect the army with different conquests. Wesley relates this woe to early Arab expansion, while Gill favors the Ottoman Turks. Their explanations treat the imagery and its timing as referring to events in history, although they disagree about which events.

Future readings also differ. Walvoord sees a future destructive force and considers whether the imagery could describe modern warfare. David Guzik discusses that possibility but favors a demonic army. The USCCB notes explain the vision as a diabolical attack pictured through the threat of a Parthian invasion. These readings should not be merged into a single agreed identification.

The two hundred million may be understood as an exact number or as a way of expressing an overwhelming force. The closing refusal to repent remains central across the readings. Identifying the army does not remove the warning about people who cling to false worship and destructive conduct even when confronted with judgment.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The horses and fire have a scriptural parallel in Joel 2:1–11, where an overwhelming force advances amid darkness and devastation. The resemblance places this vision within the Bible’s larger warnings about the Day of the LORD. It does not, by itself, establish that Joel and John are describing the very same army.

The survivors’ response also recalls Pharaoh in Exodus 9:27–35: even after acknowledging wrongdoing during a plague, he returns to resistance. Psalm 115:4–8 exposes the helplessness of idols made by human hands. Revelation brings those themes together: people can witness great power and still keep trusting what cannot save them.

Romans 2:4–8 connects God’s patience with the call to repentance and warns against a hard, unrepentant heart. The wider purpose is more than bringing calamity to an end. It calls for a real turning toward God, including a change in worship and conduct. The passage presses that question on its readers while describing the refusal of the people in the vision.
