Passage

Revelation 6:1-8

What happens when the Lamb opens the first four seals?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then I watched as the Lamb opened one of the seven seals, and I heard one of the four living creatures say in a voice like thunder, “Come!” 2 So I looked and saw a white horse, and its rider held a bow. And he was given a crown, and he rode out to overcome and conquer.

3 And when the Lamb opened the second seal, I heard the second living creature say, “Come!” 4 Then another horse went forth. It was bright red, and its rider was granted permission to take away peace from the earth and to make men slay one another. And he was given a great sword.

5 And when the Lamb opened the third seal, I heard the third living creature say, “Come!” Then I looked and saw a black horse, and its rider held in his hand a pair of scales. 6 And I heard what sounded like a voice from among the four living creatures, saying, “A quart of wheat for a denarius, and three quarts of barley for a denarius, and do not harm the oil and wine.”

7 And when the Lamb opened the fourth seal, I heard the voice of the fourth living creature say, “Come!” 8 Then I looked and saw a pale green horse. Its rider’s name was Death, and Hades followed close behind. And they were given authority over a fourth of the earth, to kill by sword, by famine, by plague, and by the beasts of the earth.

What is the context?

The Lamb begins opening the scroll He received in chapter 5. With each of the first four seals, a living creature calls out and a horse and rider appear. The repeated pattern brings the four scenes together, while each adds a different feature.

The white horse’s rider carries a bow, receives a crown and goes out conquering. The red horse’s rider takes peace from the earth and receives a great sword. People turn against one another in bloodshed. The black horse’s rider holds scales as a voice announces grain prices and a command concerning oil and wine.

The price of wheat is a denarius for a quart. In Matthew 20:1–2, a vineyard worker agrees to a denarius for a day’s work. That comparison helps convey the hardship in the vision: a day’s earnings buys only a small amount of staple food. Leviticus 26:26 similarly pictures scarce bread being measured out by weight.

The fourth horse is pale green, and its rider is explicitly named Death, with Hades following. The Greek color word chloros can describe green or a yellowish, pale shade; see Thayer’s entry. Hades refers to the realm of the dead; see Thayer’s entry. Death and the gathering of the dead are pictured together.

The authority given them extends over a fourth of the earth, with killing by sword, famine, plague and wild beasts. The scene is devastating, but the riders act with authority that is given and bounded. The Lamb’s opening of the seals remains the setting for everything John sees.

How is this passage commonly understood?

The first rider has several major readings. Gill understands Christ advancing through the gospel. Wesley reads imperial conquest, particularly under Trajan. The USCCB notes also emphasize conquering power. Walvoord favors a future ruler opposed to Christ. These explanations differ over the rider’s identity and whether the scene concerns earlier history or future events.

The later horses are widely connected with war, scarcity and death. Wesley reads the preserved oil and wine as a limit on the shortage; Gill contrasts those supplies with the scarcity of basic bread. Their particular historical applications differ, but both hear an actual restraint in the command not to harm them. The verse itself does not identify a country exempted from the crisis.

The absence of arrows from the description of the first rider does not by itself prove that he has no weapons to use or must conquer peacefully. Likewise, the crown and white horse do not name him for us. Each proposal needs to be considered alongside the whole group of riders and the wider book.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The colored horses recall Zechariah 6:1–8, where heavenly agents go out from before the Lord of all the earth. The parallel places the movement of earthly events under His authority, without requiring every horse in the two visions to have an identical meaning.

The sword, famine, plague and wild beasts also recall the four judgments named in Ezekiel 14:21–23. Revelation draws on language already used to show that violence and judgment are not beyond God’s knowledge or rule. These connections help locate the terrifying scenes within the larger biblical account of His authority over evil and His right to judge.
