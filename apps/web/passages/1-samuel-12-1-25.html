Passage

1 Samuel 12:1-25

Does having a king change Israel’s responsibility to God?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Then Samuel said to all Israel, “I have listened to your voice in all that you have said to me, and I have set over you a king. 2 Now here is the king walking before you, and I am old and gray, and my sons are here with you. I have walked before you from my youth until this day. 3 Here I am. Bear witness against me before the LORD and before His anointed: Whose ox or donkey have I taken? Whom have I cheated or oppressed? From whose hand have I accepted a bribe and closed my eyes? Tell me, and I will restore it to you.” 4 “You have not cheated us or oppressed us,” they replied, “nor have you taken anything from the hand of man.” 5 Samuel said to them, “The LORD is a witness against you, and His anointed is a witness today, that you have not found anything in my hand.” “He is a witness,” they replied.

6 Then Samuel said to the people, “The LORD is the One who appointed Moses and Aaron, and who brought your fathers up out of the land of Egypt. 7 Now present yourselves, so that I may confront you before the LORD with all the righteous acts He has done for you and your fathers. 8 When Jacob went to Egypt, your fathers cried out to the LORD, and He sent them Moses and Aaron, who brought your fathers out of Egypt and settled them in this place. 9 But they forgot the LORD their God, and He sold them into the hand of Sisera the commander of the army of Hazor, and into the hands of the Philistines and the king of Moab, who fought against them. 10 Then they cried out to the LORD and said, ‘We have sinned, for we have forsaken the LORD and served the Baals and Ashtoreths. Now deliver us from the hands of our enemies, that we may serve You.’ 11 So the LORD sent Jerubbaal, Barak, Jephthah, and Samuel, and He delivered you from the hands of your enemies on every side, and you dwelt securely. 12 But when you saw that Nahash king of the Ammonites was moving against you, you said to me, ‘No, we must have a king to rule over us’—even though the LORD your God was your king.

13 Now here is the king you have chosen, the one you requested. Behold, the LORD has placed a king over you. 14 If you fear the LORD and serve Him and obey His voice, and if you do not rebel against the command of the LORD, and if both you and the king who rules over you follow the LORD your God, then all will be well. 15 But if you disobey the LORD and rebel against His command, then the hand of the LORD will be against you as it was against your fathers. 16 Now, therefore, present yourselves and see this great thing that the LORD will do before your eyes. 17 Is it not the wheat harvest today? I will call on the LORD to send thunder and rain, so that you will know and see what a great evil you have committed in the sight of the LORD by asking for a king.” 18 So Samuel called to the LORD, and on that day the LORD sent thunder and rain. As a result, all the people greatly feared the LORD and Samuel.

19 They pleaded with Samuel, “Pray to the LORD your God for your servants so that we will not die! For we have added to all our sins the evil of asking for a king.” 20 “Do not be afraid,” Samuel replied. “Even though you have committed all this evil, do not turn aside from following the LORD, but serve the LORD with all your heart. 21 Do not turn aside after worthless things that cannot profit you or deliver you, for they are empty. 22 Indeed, for the sake of His great name, the LORD will not abandon His people, because He was pleased to make you His own. 23 As for me, far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you. And I will continue to teach you the good and right way. 24 Above all, fear the LORD and serve Him faithfully with all your heart; consider what great things He has done for you. 25 But if you persist in doing evil, both you and your king will be swept away.”

What is the context?

Samuel addresses Israel with its king now in place. He first invites testimony about his own service: has he cheated, oppressed or taken a bribe? The people say he has not. This distinguishes his conduct from the corrupt judging described earlier and establishes the setting for his warning.

He then recalls the LORD’s acts of rescue, from Egypt through the judges. The pattern has included Israel forgetting God, suffering oppression, confessing sin, and receiving deliverance. Their latest demand for a king is set against that history: the LORD was already their king.

The new arrangement does not release either the people or the ruler from obedience. Samuel places both under the call to fear God, serve Him and listen to His voice. He then asks for thunder and rain during the wheat harvest as a public sign of the evil involved in their request. The sign comes, and the people confess and ask for prayer.

Samuel does not tell them that their wrongdoing has made return pointless. They must keep following the LORD, rather than turn to things that cannot save. God will not abandon His people for the sake of His name. Samuel will continue to pray for them and teach them, while urging them to remember God’s great deeds and warning against continued evil.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Henry reads Samuel’s public accounting as both a defense against unjust blame and an example for Saul. Wesley likewise understands it as clearing the way to address the people’s responsibility. The testimony concerns Samuel’s treatment of them; it is not a claim that he is without any sin of any kind.

Readers must hold the assurance of verse 22 together with the warning of verse 25. Wesley emphasizes God’s undeserved mercy while retaining the seriousness of people thrusting Him away. Gill stresses that the people were chosen by God’s free favor and that their preservation rests on that same favor. These commentators give different emphasis to human rejection and God’s keeping, while both recognize that Israel’s standing is not earned by merit.

Henry emphasizes Samuel doing more than the people request: he will teach as well as pray. Gill also sees these as continuing responsibilities even with a king in place. Samuel’s change in public role does not mean his service to the people or his duty before God has ended.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The promise not to abandon His people rests on God’s name and choice, while the warning calls for a faithful response. A related question appears in Romans 11:1–6: has God rejected His people? Paul answers no, identifies himself as an Israelite, and points to a remnant chosen by grace.

Paul’s discussion is not an explicit quotation of Samuel’s speech, and these verses do not settle every question about later history. They do carry forward the theme of God’s faithfulness amid real human failure. Samuel’s assurance and warning should both remain audible: wrongdoing is no reason to give up following God, and belonging to His people is no reason to treat obedience as optional.
