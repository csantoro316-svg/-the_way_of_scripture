Passage

Matthew 13:47-50

The Net and the Coming Separation

Berean Standard Bible

47 Once again, the kingdom of heaven is like a net that was cast into the sea and caught all kinds of fish. 48 When it was full, the men pulled it ashore. Then they sat down and sorted the good fish into containers, but threw the bad away.

49 So will it be at the end of the age: The angels will come and separate the wicked from the righteous 50 and throw them into the fiery furnace, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

What is the context?

The final kingdom story returns to the sea. A net gathers fish of many kinds, and the catch is sorted after it is brought ashore. The good fish are kept and the bad discarded. The gathering and the final sorting are distinct moments in the picture.

The Greek word is σαγήνη, sagēnē (sah-GAY-nay), a large fishing net or dragnet. Thayer's entry identifies this use in Matthew 13:47. The image is of a broad catch followed by separation; Jesus does not describe each fish's history.

He then states the point directly. At the end of the age, the angels separate the wicked from the righteous, with a severe warning about the outcome for the wicked. This repeats the final separation of the weeds explanation, using a different everyday scene.

The passage places weight on the coming judgment rather than on impressive totals gathered into the net. The final distinction is real, and it belongs within the Son of Man's authority already described in the chapter.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read this as a warning that gathering people around the kingdom's message is not the same as the final separation of the righteous and wicked. Wesley on Matthew 13:45–50 applies the net to the preached gospel gathering a mixed response and the angels completing the separation. The Catholic Catechism, 1038–1041, likewise emphasizes the reality of final judgment and the call to respond while the time of mercy remains.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The reality of judgment becomes a reason to bring God's appeal for reconciliation. In 2 Corinthians 5:10–11, Paul connects giving account before Christ with seeking to persuade people. He then describes the message entrusted to Christ's ambassadors: be reconciled to God through Him (2 Corinthians 5:18–21).

The appeal continues in 2 Corinthians 6:1–2, which calls the present the time of God's favor and salvation. The coming separation therefore gives urgency to receiving and sharing His mercy now. God's plan includes both a just end to evil and a real invitation to life through Christ.
