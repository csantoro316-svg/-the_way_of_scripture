Passage

1 Corinthians 9:24-27

Why is Paul so concerned about finishing well?

Berean Standard Bible

24 Do you not know that in a race all the runners run, but only one receives the prize? Run in such a way as to take the prize. 25 Everyone who competes in the games trains with strict discipline. They do it for a crown that is perishable, but we do it for a crown that is imperishable. 26 Therefore I do not run aimlessly; I do not fight like I am beating the air. 27 No, I discipline my body and make it my slave, so that after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified.

What is the context?

Paul turns to runners and athletes who train seriously for a prize. Their crown will perish; the goal he sets before believers will not. The comparison calls for purpose and self-control, rather than suggesting that only one Christian can receive God’s approval.

He applies the pictures to himself: he does not run without a goal or box at empty air. His own desires and habits must serve the work entrusted to him. Teaching other people does not remove his need to live faithfully.

The word translated disqualified is adokimos (ah-DOH-kee-moss), ἀδόκιμος, meaning not approved or failing the test; see Thayer’s entry. The word makes the warning serious, but does not by itself settle whether the loss in view is ministry approval, reward or final salvation. That question requires reading the whole argument.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read the athletic picture as a call to disciplined, faithful living. They differ over what Paul means by the possibility of being disqualified.

The USCCB note on verse 27 connects Paul’s discipline with his own salvation, and Wesley’s note treats rejection as a real possibility even for someone already called to faith. The Catholic Catechism 1861 and the Methodist Articles, Article XII, provide the wider teaching that grace can be lost and restoration is possible.

The Reformed Westminster Confession 17.1–3 holds that those God truly accepts in Christ will be preserved to the end. In his own discussion of this verse, Sam Storms favors a warning about losing approval and rewards in apostolic service, while also considering a warning about perseverance in faith. This is his explanation, not a single reading required of every Reformed Christian. In each case, Paul’s self-discipline is taken seriously rather than treated as unnecessary.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Hebrews 12:1–3 also describes the life of faith as a race, directing believers to Jesus, who endured the cross and is seated at God’s right hand. Their endurance looks to Him rather than resting only on determination.

2 Timothy 4:6–8 returns to the picture near the end of Paul’s life: finishing the race, keeping the faith and looking to the Lord’s award. The promised crown is also for all who long for Christ’s appearing. God’s purpose calls His people toward lasting life with Christ, not a contest in which another believer’s faithfulness takes away their opportunity.
