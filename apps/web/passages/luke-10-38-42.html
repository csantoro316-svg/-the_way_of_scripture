Passage

Luke 10:38-42

Why does Jesus defend Mary’s choice to listen?

Berean Standard Bible

38 As they traveled along, Jesus entered a village where a woman named Martha welcomed Him into her home. 39 She had a sister named Mary, who sat at the Lord’s feet listening to His message. 40 But Martha was distracted by all the preparations to be made. She came to Jesus and said, “Lord, do You not care that my sister has left me to serve alone? Tell her to help me!”

41 “Martha, Martha,” the Lord replied, “you are worried and upset about many things. 42 But only one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion, and it will not be taken away from her.”

What is the context?

Martha welcomes Jesus into her home, while her sister Mary sits at His feet and listens to His message. Martha becomes distracted by the preparations and asks Jesus to tell Mary to help her.

Jesus answers Martha directly and acknowledges her worry over many things. He identifies one necessary thing and defends the good portion Mary has chosen. Her opportunity to listen will not be taken from her.

The scene follows a story in which mercy takes the form of costly service. Read together, the passages call for both attention to Jesus and care for others. Martha’s hospitality provides the setting, but her many tasks have become a distraction from the One she has welcomed.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christian readings commonly see Jesus giving priority to receiving His teaching. Calvin explains that other responsibilities still have their place, while hearing Jesus must remain central; the Catholic bishops’ notes likewise emphasize listening to the Lord. (Calvin on Luke 10:42; USCCB notes on Luke 10:38–42)

Where does this fit in God's plan?

Deuteronomy describes Israel learning through the manna that life depends on every word from God’s mouth. Mary’s attention to Jesus can be read alongside that larger theme: receiving God’s word is necessary nourishment, not an optional extra after every other task is finished. This is a connection between the passages, rather than an explicit identification made by Luke. (Deuteronomy 8:3)

James brings hearing and action together. God’s word is to be welcomed and then practiced, including care for people in distress. Listening and serving therefore belong together in the life God forms; hearing His word gives direction to the care His people offer. (James 1:19–27)
