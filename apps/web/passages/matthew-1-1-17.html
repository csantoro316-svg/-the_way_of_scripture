Passage

Matthew 1:1-17

The Genealogy of Jesus Christ

Berean Standard Bible

1 This is the record of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham:

2 Abraham was the father of Isaac, Isaac the father of Jacob, and Jacob the father of Judah and his brothers. 3 Judah was the father of Perez and Zerah by Tamar, Perez the father of Hezron, and Hezron the father of Ram. 4 Ram was the father of Amminadab, Amminadab the father of Nahshon, and Nahshon the father of Salmon. 5 Salmon was the father of Boaz by Rahab, Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth, Obed the father of Jesse, 6 and Jesse the father of David the king. Next: David was the father of Solomon by Uriah’s wife,

7 Solomon the father of Rehoboam, Rehoboam the father of Abijah, and Abijah the father of Asa. 8 Asa was the father of Jehoshaphat, Jehoshaphat the father of Joram, and Joram the father of Uzziah. 9 Uzziah was the father of Jotham, Jotham the father of Ahaz, and Ahaz the father of Hezekiah. 10 Hezekiah was the father of Manasseh, Manasseh the father of Amon, Amon the father of Josiah, 11 and Josiah the father of Jeconiah and his brothers at the time of the exile to Babylon.

12 After the exile to Babylon: Jeconiah was the father of Shealtiel, Shealtiel the father of Zerubbabel, 13 Zerubbabel the father of Abiud, Abiud the father of Eliakim, and Eliakim the father of Azor. 14 Azor was the father of Zadok, Zadok the father of Achim, and Achim the father of Eliud. 15 Eliud was the father of Eleazar, Eleazar the father of Matthan, Matthan the father of Jacob, 16 and Jacob the father of Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus, who is called Christ. 17 In all, then, there were fourteen generations from Abraham to David, fourteen from David to the exile to Babylon, and fourteen from the exile to the Christ.

What is the context?

Matthew opens by identifying Jesus through two great names in Israel's history: David and Abraham. Before describing the birth, he places Jesus within the family story of promise, kingship, exile, and continuing hope. The genealogy answers a question about who Jesus is, not simply who His ancestors were.

Christ is a title, not a family surname. The Greek word is Χριστός, Christos (khris-TOSS), the anointed one. Thayer's Greek Lexicon explains its use for the expected Messiah. Calling Jesus Christ presents Him as the promised One whose place in the story the following names will show.

The list moves from Abraham to David, from David to the Babylonian exile, and from the exile to Jesus. Matthew deliberately presents three groups of fourteen generations. It is a selected genealogy: comparison with 1 Chronicles 3:10–12 shows kings between Joram and Uzziah who are not individually named here. The arrangement gives the history a clear shape rather than attempting to name every generation.

Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Uriah's wife also appear in the family record. Their stories bring remembered hardship, unexpected relationships, and people from outside Israel into the line leading to Jesus. The genealogy does not offer a family history free from wrongdoing or painful circumstances.

At the end, the wording changes. Joseph is identified as Mary's husband, and Jesus is born of her. Matthew does not simply repeat the pattern by saying Joseph fathered Jesus. That distinction prepares the next passage's explanation of conception through the Holy Spirit.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians commonly read the genealogy as showing Jesus' place in the promises to David and Abraham. Wesley's notes on Matthew 1:1, 6, 16 emphasize both connections. The history reaches through kings and exile to the Messiah rather than ending with the loss of the kingdom.

Christians have offered different explanations when comparing this list with Luke 3:23–38. Wesley's note on Luke 3:23 understands Matthew to trace Joseph's line and Luke to trace Mary's, with Joseph related to Heli as a son-in-law.

An earlier explanation, preserved from Julius Africanus in Eusebius, Church History 1.7.2–10, understands both lists through Joseph: one gives natural descent and the other a legal family line connected with a child raised for a deceased relative. These are attempts to explain the relationship between the accounts. Neither Gospel explicitly supplies all the family details needed to confirm one complete reconstruction.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The two opening names point to promises God has been carrying forward. 2 Samuel 7:12–16 speaks of David's enduring house and kingdom, and 2 Timothy 2:8 identifies the risen Jesus as descended from David. The royal family story reaches beyond the exile to the One Matthew calls Christ.

The Abraham connection reaches outward as well. Galatians 3:8, 16 connects the blessing promised to the nations with Christ. Matthew will end with Jesus commissioning disciples among all nations in Matthew 28:18–20. The opening genealogy belongs to that larger movement: God's faithfulness within Israel's history brings a Savior whose blessing reaches beyond one people.
