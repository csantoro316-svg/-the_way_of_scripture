Passage

1 Samuel 13:8-15

Will Saul wait when his army begins to leave?

Berean Standard Bible

8 And Saul waited seven days for the time appointed by Samuel, but Samuel did not come to Gilgal, and the troops began to desert Saul. 9 So he said, “Bring me the burnt offering and the peace offerings.” And he offered up the burnt offering. 10 Just as he finished offering the burnt offering, Samuel arrived, and Saul went out to greet him. 11 “What have you done?” Samuel asked. And Saul replied, “When I saw that the troops were deserting me, and that you did not come at the appointed time and the Philistines were gathering at Michmash, 12 I thought, ‘Now the Philistines will descend upon me at Gilgal, and I have not sought the favor of the LORD.’ So I felt compelled to offer the burnt offering.”

13 “You have acted foolishly,” Samuel declared. “You have not kept the command that the LORD your God gave you; if you had, the LORD would have established your kingdom over Israel for all time. 14 But now your kingdom will not endure; the LORD has sought a man after His own heart and appointed him ruler over His people, because you have not kept the command of the LORD.” 15 Then Samuel set out from Gilgal and went up to Gibeah in Benjamin. And Saul numbered the troops who were with him, about six hundred men.

What is the context?

Saul waits seven days for the appointed meeting, but Samuel has not arrived and troops are slipping away. Saul orders the offerings brought and offers the burnt offering. Samuel arrives just as he finishes and asks what he has done.

Saul explains the pressure as he saw it: the people were deserting, Samuel had not come, and the Philistines might attack before he sought God’s favor. He describes himself as feeling compelled to act. The explanation shows why he acted; Samuel does not accept it as obedience.

Samuel identifies the offense as failure to keep God’s command. Saul’s kingdom will not endure, and the LORD has sought a ruler after His own heart. The rebuke concerns the future of Saul’s rule and house; Saul does not immediately leave the throne in this scene. After Samuel departs, he counts about six hundred men still with him.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill locate the failure in not waiting for Samuel as commanded. They understand the seventh day as not yet completed when Saul acts. That is their explanation of the timing; the narrator directly states the wait, Saul’s action, Samuel’s arrival and the charge that the command was broken.

Gill expressly distinguishes that charge from making the passage solely about a king taking over a priest’s office. He discusses whether Saul performs the act himself or has a priest do it, while keeping the central issue as disregarding the command. The text’s rebuke should remain central when explaining the seriousness of what happens.

The prospect of Saul’s kingdom enduring also receives different explanations. Wesley and Gill understand the duration as a long continuation through his family, allowing for the later kingship of Judah. Wesley also distinguishes this early warning from the later fuller rejection in chapter 15. Those readings address the relationship between the passages; the warning here plainly says that disobedience has cost Saul an enduring kingdom.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The New Testament explicitly takes up the description of a man after God’s heart. In Acts 13:21–23, Paul recalls Saul, then David, whom God identifies with that description. He immediately connects David’s descendants with the promised Savior, Jesus.

That connection establishes the larger direction of the royal story without making David sinless or reducing God’s purpose to Saul’s failure. The ruler announced here belongs to the history through which the promised Savior comes. Acts makes the movement toward Jesus explicit rather than leaving it dependent on a guessed symbolic detail.
