Passage

Matthew 24:32-35

Recognizing the Season and Trusting Jesus' Words

Berean Standard Bible

32 Now learn this lesson from the fig tree: As soon as its branches become tender and sprout leaves, you know that summer is near. 33 So also, when you see all these things, you will know that He is near, right at the door.

34 Truly I tell you, this generation will not pass away until all these things have happened. 35 Heaven and earth will pass away, but My words will never pass away.

What is the context?

After describing the Son of Man's coming and the gathering of His people, Jesus turns to a familiar seasonal lesson. Tender branches and new leaves tell people that summer is near. In the same way, His hearers are to recognize the nearness indicated by the things He has described.

The comparison itself explains the tree's role: people recognize a season from what they see. Luke 21:29–33 gives the related lesson with the fig tree and all the trees, then speaks of knowing that God's kingdom is near. The tree in this illustration should be read within that comparison before another symbolic meaning is assigned to it.

Jesus then says that this generation will not pass away before all these things happen. Generation is γενεά, genea (geh-neh-AH). Thayer's entry includes family descent, people sharing a character, and people living at the same time; it lists Matthew 24:34 under that last sense. Readers' different understandings of the events and the people in view are set out below.

The closing assurance gives the passage its firm foundation: heaven and earth will pass away, but Jesus' words will never pass away. The next verse warns that the day and hour are unknown. The lesson joins recognition and confidence in His words with a limit on what His followers are told about timing.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Christians agree that Jesus calls His followers to recognize what He has described and trust His words. They differ over the generation in view and how all these things relate to the temple's destruction and His coming.

Wesley on 24:32–35 connects this generation with people then living and the destruction of Jerusalem. Calvin on 24:34 likewise says the disciples' generation would experience the foretold troubles, while later generations would continue to face such suffering. The US Catholic bishops' note on 24:34 offers the generation of Matthew and his community as another explanation.

A future reading understands the generation as those alive when the final signs occur. Walvoord's discussion of 24:32–36 includes this among the possible explanations and applies the tree's seasonal lesson to those who see the coming tribulation. He treats the tree as an illustration of recognizing a season, rather than a direct identification of modern Israel.

A different reading in Scofield's note on 24:34 takes generation in the sense of the people of Israel. It understands the promise to mean that Israel will be preserved until the foretold events are fulfilled. These are distinct readings of the passage; the dictionary's range of meanings does not by itself settle the choice between them.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

The permanence of Jesus' words belongs within a wider promise about God's word. Isaiah 40:6–8 contrasts human life's passing glory with the word that endures. 1 Peter 1:22–25 applies that promise to the good news believers have received and connects it with their new birth and love for one another. The word that announces God's future also brings people into a changed life now.

Hebrews 12:25–29 describes created things being shaken while God's people receive an unshakable kingdom. 2 Peter 3:8–14 joins the coming passing of the present order with God's promise of a new heaven and earth, calling believers to holy and peaceful lives as they wait. Trust in His enduring promise is meant to shape how His people live, rather than merely satisfy curiosity about a date.
