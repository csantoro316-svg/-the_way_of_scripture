Passage

1 Samuel 23:1-6

Why does David risk helping Keilah while he is being hunted?

Berean Standard Bible

1 Now it was reported to David, “Look, the Philistines are fighting against Keilah and looting the threshing floors.” 2 So David inquired of the LORD, “Should I go and attack these Philistines?” And the LORD said to David, “Go and attack the Philistines and save Keilah.” 3 But David’s men said to him, “Look, we are afraid here in Judah; how much more if we go to Keilah against the armies of the Philistines?” 4 Once again, David inquired of the LORD, and the LORD answered him: “Go at once to Keilah, for I will deliver the Philistines into your hand.” 5 Then David and his men went to Keilah, fought against the Philistines, and carried off their livestock, striking them with a mighty blow. So David saved the people of Keilah. 6 (Now Abiathar son of Ahimelech had brought the ephod with him when he fled to David at Keilah.)

What is the context?

David hears that the Philistines are attacking Keilah and looting its threshing floors. Although he is still escaping Saul, he asks the LORD whether he should attack and save the town. The answer is to go.

His men are afraid. They already feel unsafe in Judah, and fighting the Philistine armies seems to add another danger. David asks again rather than simply dismissing their concern. The LORD repeats the direction and promises to deliver the Philistines into his hand.

David and his men go, defeat the attackers and carry off their livestock. Keilah is rescued. The account also reminds us that Abiathar brought the ephod when he fled to David. The surviving priest now accompanies the man Saul is pursuing.

The episode shows David carrying out the work of protecting Israel before he has the throne. His own unsettled circumstances do not keep him from responding to another community’s need.

How is this passage commonly understood?

Wesley and Gill understand the second inquiry as reassurance for David’s men. Their fear is answered through another appeal to God and a clearer promise of victory, rather than David relying only on his authority as leader.

Henry emphasizes David’s willingness to act as a protector while being mistreated by the king. He sees an example of continuing to do good when service has brought little gratitude. The rescue directs David’s attention beyond his own danger.

Wesley and Gill associate the ephod with the high priest’s means of seeking God’s direction. The passage identifies Abiathar and the ephod, while leaving the precise way the answer was received undescribed.

Where does this fit in God's plan?

David’s rescue of Keilah shows leadership serving people in need while the leader himself bears hardship. It offers a helpful parallel with the kind of kingship Jesus reveals.

In Mark 10:42–45, Jesus teaches that greatness among His followers takes the form of service. He gives His own mission as the fullest example: He comes to serve and give His life as a ransom for many. David rescues one threatened community; Jesus gives Himself to bring a greater deliverance.

The connection is a parallel in serving and rescuing, rather than a claim that Mark directly identifies Keilah as a prediction. It helps us recognize why David’s care matters before he receives public power: a ruler’s calling is already visible in how he uses what he has for others.
